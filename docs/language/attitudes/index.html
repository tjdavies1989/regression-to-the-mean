Propositional Attitude Reports

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.4

Everywhere else in the language, swapping one name for another name of the same thing preserves truth: if Superman can fly, Clark Kent can fly. But embed the names under "believes that" and the principle collapses. "Lois believes Superman can fly" is true; "Lois believes Clark Kent can fly" seems flatly false, though the two sentences differ only in co-referring names. Attitude verbs — believe, hope, fear, doubt — create contexts in which reference is not the whole story, and the problem is to say what more there is. The stakes are high: a semantics that cannot handle its most common verbs is not finished, and belief reports are our principal instrument for describing minds.

Frege's own solution is reference shift: inside an attitude context, a term refers not to its customary referent but to its customary sense, so the two reports genuinely say different things. This explains the data with maximal directness, at the cost of an infinite hierarchy of indirect senses and a collision with the Kripkean case against descriptive senses made at reference. The Russellian response (Salmon, Soames) bites the bullet: names contribute only their bearers, so the two reports express the same singular proposition and both are true — the appearance of difference is pragmatic, a matter of which guise of the proposition the sentence conveys. Its strength is a clean, direct-reference semantics; its weakness is telling nearly every competent speaker that their truth-value judgments are systematically mistaken. Hidden-indexical theories (Schiffer, Crimmins and Perry) split the difference: a belief report tacitly quantifies over modes of presentation supplied by context, so substitution can flip truth values without abandoning Russellian propositions. The apparatus is powerful; the objection is that the postulated hidden constituent answers to no visible syntax.

Attitude contexts are also the chief pressure toward fine-grained content. If propositions are sets of possible worlds, believing one necessary truth is believing them all — logical omniscience by fiat. Structured-proposition theories build sense-like or Russellian constituents into the proposition itself precisely to give the attitudes objects with enough grain.

Kripke's Pierre is the case that embarrasses everyone. A monolingual Frenchman assents to "Londres est jolie," moves to an ugly quarter of London without making the connection, and assents to "London is not pretty." By principles no one wants to deny — that sincere assent reveals belief, that translation preserves it — Pierre believes London is pretty and believes it is not, though he has made no logical error. No substitution of names occurs; the puzzle springs from disquotation and translation alone, which is Kripke's point: the machinery generating Frege's puzzle is everyone's machinery, so the puzzle refutes no one and indicts all.

A second axis cuts across the first. "Ralph believes someone is a spy" is ambiguous between a general suspicion (de dicto) and there being a particular person Ralph suspects (de re) — Quine's problem of quantifying into attitude contexts. And some attitudes seem irreducibly first-personal: Perry's shopper trailing sugar from his own cart, Lewis's two omniscient gods who know every worldly fact yet not which god they are. Lewis concluded that the objects of attitudes are not propositions but self-ascribed properties — belief de se outrunning belief de dicto. Recent work (Cappelen and Dever, Magidor) pushes back: the indexical cases show something about how content is grasped, not a special kind of content, and the de se is philosophically ordinary. Whether essential indexicality is a discovery or an artifact is now the node's liveliest front.
