Theories of Meaning

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.4

Every competent speaker knows what "The cat is on the mat" means — and knows it for sentences never uttered before. A theory of meaning owes an account of what this knowledge is knowledge of. The constraints are demanding: it must explain how finite minds command an infinite language (meanings must compose — a sentence's meaning must be determined by its parts and their arrangement); it must connect meaning to communication, language being a public instrument; and it must connect meaning to truth, since what a sentence means fixes what would make it true. Each rival program takes one constraint as the master key.

The truth-conditional program takes the link to truth as constitutive. Davidson's proposal is austere: a theory of meaning for a language is nothing more than a Tarski-style truth theory for it — a finite set of axioms entailing, for each of infinitely many sentences, a theorem like "'Schnee ist weiss' is true if and only if snow is white." A theory delivering all such theorems, confirmable by radical interpretation from behavior alone, says everything a meaning theory could. Montague, arriving from mathematical logic, showed the program could be executed in detail, treating English with the model-theoretic rigor of a formal calculus; his inheritance is formal semantics, the one branch of the field that became a cumulative science, now largely housed in linguistics. The program's strength is precision and an explanation of compositionality; its worry is that truth conditions seem too coarse — "equilateral" and "equiangular" apply to the same triangles — and silent about what grasping them consists in.

Use theories take the public instrument as the key. The later Wittgenstein's slogan — meaning is use — becomes, in Sellars and systematically in Brandom, inferentialism: an expression's meaning is its role in the game of giving and asking for reasons — what its assertion commits one to, what entitles one to it. The strength is an answer where truth-conditional theories go quiet: understanding is practical mastery, not mute contemplation of a truth condition; the challenge is recovering compositionality and objective correctness from inferential role. Grice takes communication as basic and meaning as derived psychology: a speaker means that p by intending her audience to believe that p partly through recognizing that very intention; sentence meaning is built from regularities in such intentions. The strength is reductive ambition — semantics from mind, without semantic primitives. Verificationism, which identified meaning with the method of empirical verification, is the field's cautionary tale: a sharp proposal that condemned metaphysics as meaningless and then could not state its own criterion in a form that survived scrutiny.

Across the programs runs the internalism–externalism dispute. Chomsky treats meaning as a property of I-language, the internal computational system, and doubts a shared external language is even a coherent object of theory; Putnam and Burge argue that "meanings just ain't in the head" — Twin Earth shows environment and linguistic community fixing content without the speaker's knowledge. And it remains unsettled whether the programs compete at all: Davidson describes the structure a meaning theory must have, Grice the psychology of communication, Brandom the practice sustaining both — rival answers to one question, or complementary answers to three.

Bridges

  • language.rule-following: every theory here inherits Kripkenstein's challenge — whatever meaning is said to be, some fact must make it determinate, and the skeptic doubts any candidate suffices.
  • mind.intentionality: Grice derives language's aboutness from thought's; Davidson and Brandom run the dependence the other way; which is prior is the border dispute itself.
  • logic: Tarski's truth definitions and Montague's model theory are the truth-conditional program's engineering; formal semantics is joint property.
