Political and Social Philosophy

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 0  ·  pass 2026-08-26.11

States coerce. They take property on pain of prison, conscript, punish, and exclude — and they do it to people who are supposed to be one another's equals and who disagree about nearly everything that matters. Political philosophy is the sustained attempt to say when any of this can be justified, to whom the justification is owed, and what social arrangements could survive being defended to everyone living under them. The modern field has a precise birthday: Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971) revived normative political theory from its positivist hibernation and set both its agenda and its method; the field since is largely commentary, rebellion, and extension — with a standing counter-tradition insisting that the frame itself (the neutral state, the ideal society, the consenting citizen) is what most needs explaining.

Justice is the flagship. Rawls's original position and two principles, and just as durably his method of reflective equilibrium, define the terrain; Nozick's entitlement theory is the great rival — justice as history, not pattern, with the Wilt Chamberlain argument showing that liberty upsets patterns; luck egalitarianism refines the Rawlsian intuition that the natural lottery is arbitrary, and Anderson's relational counterattack (equality as a kind of society, not a distributive inspection) redraws the egalitarian camp from within; the capabilities approach changes the currency to what people can actually do and be; and Mills's critique of ideal theory — tools forged for a compliant utopia cannot diagnose centuries of domination — has made the ideal/nonideal dispute the field's live methodological front.

Legitimacy and authority ask the prior question: what entitles the state to rule at all? Consent, the intuitive answer, fails for every actual state — Hume's point that most of us can no more leave than jump ship — and the successor accounts (fair play, natural duty, associative obligation, Raz's service conception, democratic legitimacy) each capture cases and leak elsewhere; philosophical anarchism, the conclusion that no state has quite earned its authority though many are worth obeying anyway, remains the position most argued against and least refuted. Liberty divides along Berlin's two concepts until MacCallum's triadic analysis dissolves the dichotomy and republicanism's non-domination (freedom as the absence of arbitrary power, not merely of interference) supplies the genuine third; Mill's harm principle still governs the paternalism debates, and the republican critique has migrated inward, to markets and workplaces as sites of private government. Equality runs the currency dispute (welfare, resources, capabilities), the luck/relational contest, and the alternatives that dodge the leveling-down objection — sufficiency, priority — while asking the prior question of why equality as such should matter. Democracy is defended procedurally (as the embodiment of political equality) and epistemically (Condorcet's fragile theorem, deliberation, the diversity arguments), attacked from voter-ignorance data, and renegotiated in proposals — epistocracy, lottocracy, citizens' assemblies — that test how much of the ideal survives the evidence.

Rights supply the field's grammar — Hohfeld's incidents, the will/interest contest over what rights are, the orthodox/political split over human rights — and philosophy of law its institutional face: Hart's positivism against Dworkin's interpretivism on what law is, natural law's revival, and punishment's justification carried jointly with ethics.moral-responsibility. Social ontology builds the world the others presuppose: Searle's status functions, collective intentionality, money and institutions, and the social kinds — race and gender above all, in Haslanger's ameliorative analysis — where metaphysics and politics are one subject, and Hacking's looping effects make the objects of social science answer back.

Global justice tests whether any of it stops at borders: cosmopolitanism against statism on the scope of egalitarian duty, the ethics of immigration from Carens's open-borders argument to its communitarian critics, duties to the global poor, territorial rights, and climate justice as the intersection where this division meets ethics.future-people. And the critical approaches — ideology critique from Marx to the present, power's three faces and Foucault's dispersal of it, the Frankfurt inheritance, feminist political philosophy from Okin's critique of Rawls to the politics of care, recognition against redistribution, Mills's racial contract — are housed not as an appendix but as the division's standing opposition party: the tradition that reads the rest of the table of contents as itself a political artifact.

The figures register carries the division's spine — hobbes, rousseau, marx, mill, rawls, arendt (the last three still stubs, queued) — and its densest outward border is ethics, from which it took its rebirth and to which it returns every question about what individuals owe.
