Liberty

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.11

Everyone is for freedom; the fights begin when we ask what it is and when the state may curtail it. A law against heroin, a seatbelt mandate, a ban on hate speech, an employment contract that lets a manager search your phone — each is defended in freedom's name by one side and attacked in freedom's name by the other. The concept is doing double duty, and the first task is to sort out what it could mean.

Berlin's 1958 lecture set the terms. Negative liberty is the absence of interference by others: you are free to the extent that no one blocks your options. Positive liberty is self-mastery: being governed by your own reason rather than your appetites, your circumstances, or a manipulated will. Berlin's warning — that positive liberty invites tyrants to force people to be "truly" free — gave the negative conception the upper hand in liberal theory, and froze the debate into a binary. MacCallum's dissolution is the classic response: every freedom claim is triadic — some agent is free from some constraint to do or become something — so the two "concepts" are just rival emphases within one schema, and the real disputes are normative, about which constraints and which doings matter. What is strongest in Berlin survives this: the political point that freedom is one value among several, not a container for everything good.

Republican freedom is the genuine third position. For Pettit and Skinner, drawing on Roman and early-modern sources, freedom is the absence of domination — of subjection to another's arbitrary power — not merely of interference. The slave of a kindly master is unfree though never interfered with; a fair law, publicly controlled, interferes without dominating. The view's strength is diagnostic: it explains why servility, dependence, and unchecked discretion are freedom-problems even when no one lifts a finger, and it yields a distinctive politics of contestation and the rule of law rather than mere non-intervention.

A second axis asks not what liberty is but when it may be limited. Mill's harm principle holds that the only purpose for which power may rightfully be exercised over a person against their will is to prevent harm to others — never their own good, physical or moral. The principle's power lies in what it excludes; its difficulty lies in specifying "harm." Feinberg's four-volume mapping distinguishes harm from offense, and both from legal moralism (punishing wrongless immorality) and legal paternalism, arguing that only the first two can ground criminalization, and offense only weakly. Paternalism divides into soft — intervening where consent is absent or compromised, which Mill himself licenses — and hard, overriding a competent person's voluntary choice, which remains the contested case; nudging and public-health regulation keep the question live.

A third axis asks where unfreedom lives. Classical liberals locate the threat in the state and see markets as freedom's natural habitat. Socialist critics answer that propertyless workers face structurally forced choices, and republicans extend the point inside the firm: Anderson's "private government" argument holds that employers wield arbitrary, unaccountable authority over workers that liberals would never tolerate from a state. The dispute over economic liberty is thus not an application of the concept but a test of it: which conception you hold largely decides whether the labor contract is freedom exercised or freedom lost.

Bridges

  • To ethics: autonomy, well-being, and the wrongness of paternalism are ethical questions before they are legal ones; Mill's principle is utilitarianism applied to institutions.
  • To metaphysics: political liberty presupposes but is distinct from free will — whether anyone is metaphysically free is homed there.
  • To politics.equality: non-domination converges with relational egalitarianism — both locate injustice in subjection rather than in distributive shortfall.
  • To politics.legitimacy: republican theory ties freedom to legitimacy — law that citizens contest and control interferes without dominating.
