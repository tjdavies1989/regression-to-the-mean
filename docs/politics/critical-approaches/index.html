Ideology, Power, and Critical Approaches

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.11

Mainstream political philosophy asks whether our institutions are just. The traditions gathered here ask a prior question: why does the inquiry take the shape it does — why these idealizations, these silences — and whose interests does the shape serve? The suspicion is not that philosophers are corrupt but that theory built from within an unjust society may inherit its distortions, so that the frame itself becomes the thing to be explained.

The founding version is Marx's theory of ideology: the ruling ideas of an age are the ideas of its ruling class, and their characteristic trick is to present contingent arrangements — wage labor, private property — as natural and eternal. Althusser radicalized this: ideology is not primarily false belief but lived practice, reproduced by "ideological state apparatuses" (schools, churches, media) that interpellate individuals into subjects who experience their position as freely chosen. Contemporary ideology critique (Haslanger, Jaeggi, Shelby, Stanley) inherits the explanatory power and confronts the epistemological debt: if ideology distorts everyone's cognition, from where does the critic see clearly? Answers appeal to internal contradiction, to functional explanation (beliefs stabilized because they serve domination are thereby discredited), or to standpoint epistemology's claim that the dominated see what the comfortable cannot. The tradition's strength is that it explains what contract theory cannot: the durability of injustice with the apparent consent of its victims.

A parallel line theorizes power itself. Lukes distinguished three faces: prevailing in open conflict; keeping issues off the agenda; and — the deepest — shaping preferences so that conflict never arises, so the dominated want what domination requires. Foucault went further: power is not a possession of sovereigns but a dispersed network of practices that is productive — it makes subjects, knowledges, and norms rather than merely repressing them. The standard objection (pressed by Fraser) is that if power is everywhere and constitutive, critique loses its footing: in whose name is normalization condemned? The Frankfurt School had already posed this as its own problem — Horkheimer's "critical theory" is theory with emancipatory intent that must account for its own possibility — and Habermas's answer, norms implicit in communication itself, is treated at the tradition's home (see traditions.critical-theory).

Feminist political philosophy turned these instruments on the liberal canon. Okin showed that Rawls exempts the family from the principles of justice while relying on it to produce just citizens — the "personal" sphere is inside the basic structure, not before it. MacKinnon argued that the law's prized neutrality is the standpoint of dominance made official: objectivity as the epistemology of a male state. The ethics of care adds a constructive politics: contract theory presupposes independent adults, but independence is a product of unpaid dependency work; Kittay's point is that any theory silent about care assumes what it never justifies.

Two further programs sharpen the field. Honneth reconstructs injustice as misrecognition — damage to the relations (love, rights, esteem) in which identities form; Fraser replies that recognition cannot absorb maldistribution (nor vice versa) and proposes participatory parity as the common measure. And Mills's racial contract argues that the social contract's idealizations are not innocent abstractions but an "epistemology of ignorance": actual states were founded on racial exclusion, and ideal theory that abstracts from this functions as ideology. His methodological conclusion — begin from actual, structural injustice — is the sharpest statement of what unites everything on this page.
