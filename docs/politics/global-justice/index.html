Global Justice and Migration

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.11

A child born in Niger can expect a life radically shorter, poorer, and less free than a child born in Norway, and neither did anything to deserve the difference. Within a state, Rawlsians call such unchosen inequality the primary subject of justice. Across borders, most theory has historically fallen silent. The problem of global justice is whether that silence is principled or an evasion: do the demanding distributive principles that hold among fellow citizens hold among human beings as such — and if not, why does a line on a map carry such moral weight?

Cosmopolitans answer that it cannot. Beitz and Pogge argue that Rawls's own logic overruns the border he drew around it: there is now a global basic structure — trade regimes, capital markets, borders themselves — that profoundly shapes life prospects, and the veil of ignorance, consistently applied, should hide one's citizenship along with one's talents. The strongest card here is the sheer arbitrariness of birthplace, which resembles nothing so much as the natural lottery Rawls set out to neutralize. Rawls himself refused the extension: his Law of Peoples holds that liberal societies owe well-ordered peoples toleration and burdened societies assistance to the threshold of decent institutions — not ongoing egalitarian distribution. Statists such as Nagel and Blake give the refusal its best defense: egalitarian justice is not a response to inequality per se but to a special relationship — coercion imposed in the name of those coerced, or joint authorship of a legal order — and no global institution stands in that relationship to humanity. The dispute is thus less about compassion than about what triggers justice: humanity, cooperation, or coercion.

Duties to the global poor can be argued without settling that question. Singer's shallow pond scales up: if you must ruin your shoes to save a drowning child, distance and multiplicity do not obviously excuse letting distant children die when aid is cheap; the argument's power is that it needs only premises almost everyone accepts. Pogge's alternative needs even less: the affluent are not innocent bystanders but participants in an institutional order — resource and borrowing privileges for dictators, rigged trade rules — that foreseeably produces avoidable poverty, so the duty is the stringent negative one not to harm. Critics press each at its strongest point: Singer's argument seems to demand too much, Pogge's causal claim too much of the evidence.

Migration makes the border question concrete. Carens argues that citizenship in a wealthy state is the modern equivalent of feudal privilege — an inherited status that determines life chances — and that the liberal commitments states profess (moral equality, freedom of movement within borders) condemn the closure they practice. Miller and Walzer answer that political communities are not accidental aggregates: self-determination includes some control over membership, and Walzer adds that distributive justice itself presupposes a bounded community whose shared understandings define what goods mean. Beneath both runs the unsettled question of territorial rights — what could entitle a state to exclude others from a portion of the earth in the first place.

Climate change is where these threads now intersect and pull taut: historical emitters, present beneficiaries, and future victims are distributed across borders and generations at once, making climate justice the field's live testing ground.
