Philosophy of Law

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.11

A statute passed under a wicked regime, in due form, commanding something monstrous: is it law? The question is not idle. Judges must decide whether Nazi-era enactments were valid law badly made or no law at all; the answer determines whether a grudge informer broke the law or obeyed it. Philosophy of law begins here, with the relation between law and morality, and widens into a cluster of problems: what makes something law, what courts are doing when they interpret it, and what could justify the distinctive thing law does at the end of the line — punish.

The modern debate is framed by H. L. A. Hart. Law, for Hart, is a union of primary rules, which govern conduct, and secondary rules, which govern the rules themselves — how to change them, adjudicate under them, and above all recognize them. A legal system exists where officials accept a rule of recognition from the internal point of view: not merely predicting sanctions, as earlier command theories had it, but treating the rule as a common standard. The strength of this positivism is its explanatory economy: it accounts for wicked legal systems, for law's systematic character, and for the difference between being obliged and being obligated, all without moral premises. Law's existence is one thing, its merit another.

Dworkin's interpretivism denies the separation. Judges in hard cases appeal to principles — no one may profit from his own wrong — that carry weight without ever being enacted, and that no rule of recognition could certify. Adjudication, Dworkin argues, is constructive interpretation: finding the reading of the legal record that both fits it and shows it in its best moral light. Law as integrity thus makes moral judgment internal to saying what the law is, and underwrites his notorious claim that hard cases have right answers. Positivists split in response. Inclusive positivists allow that a rule of recognition may incorporate moral tests; Raz's exclusive positivism forbids it — law claims legitimate authority, and an authority's directives can serve their point only if identifiable without recourse to the very moral reasons they are meant to settle. That argument from the nature of authority is the sharpest weapon in the positivist arsenal.

The older tradition runs the dependence the other way. Natural law theory, from Aquinas to Finnis, holds that law is intelligible only as an ordinance of reason for the common good; an unjust enactment is law only in a defective, secondary sense. Finnis's revival grounds law in basic goods that practical reason cannot coherently deny, and its strength is that it explains why law claims our obedience rather than merely our compliance. From the other flank, legal realists insist that "the law" in books underdetermines what courts do, and that prediction of official behavior is the practitioner's real object; critical legal studies radicalizes the point, arguing that doctrinal indeterminacy lets adjudication launder political choice — a jurisprudential echo of the ideology critique in this division's parent article.

Two further problems anchor the field. Punishment needs justifying: retributivists say wrongdoers deserve suffering and desert suffices; deterrence theorists look to prevented harms and are pressed with using the punished as means; expressive theories say punishment communicates deserved censure. All presuppose responsible agency. And interpretation: textualists tie judges to enacted public meaning, purposivists to the point the enactment serves — a debate about democratic authority as much as about meaning.

Bridges

  • To ethics.moral-responsibility: punishment's justification presupposes an account of responsible agency; legal excuses and insanity doctrine are applied moral responsibility.
  • To language: legal interpretation turns on contested notions of meaning, speaker's intention, and vagueness — statutes are the great natural laboratory of applied philosophy of language.
  • Within politics: to legitimacy (law claims authority; positivism's best argument runs through what authority is) and to critical-approaches (realism and CLS on adjudication as politics).
