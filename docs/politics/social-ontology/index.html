Social Ontology

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.11

A twenty-dollar bill is paper and ink; a border is an imaginary line; a corporation cannot be touched. Yet the bill settles debts, the border can be crossed illegally, and the corporation can sue you. Social ontology asks how there can be facts like these — facts that exist only because we collectively take them to exist, and that are nonetheless objective: no act of private wishing makes your mortgage vanish. The puzzle is that the social world seems both made by us and stubbornly independent of any one of us.

The best-known constructive answer is Searle's. Institutional facts arise when we collectively impose status functions through rules of the form "X counts as Y in context C": these pieces of paper count as money in the United States; this utterance counts as a marriage. What makes status functions distinctive is that they carry deontic powers — rights, duties, permissions — which mere physics never confers; and they persist only as long as collective acceptance persists, which is why currencies and regimes can collapse overnight. Critics press two points. Electronic money and other "free-standing Y terms" seem to have no X at all, forcing Searle to amend the theory; and Guala and others argue that institutions are better modeled as rules in equilibrium — self-enforcing patterns of incentive — making collective acceptance an effect rather than the foundation. Money is the shared test case: is it a status collectively declared, or a convention nobody could profitably defect from?

Beneath institutions lies collective intentionality: what is it for us to intend, believe, or act together? Gilbert argues that joint commitment is primitive and normatively loaded — even two people walking together acquire obligations neither could unilaterally dissolve. Bratman offers a reductive rival: shared agency is nothing over and above interlocking individual intentions and mutual knowledge, a view whose strength is parsimony. Tuomela distinguishes acting in the "we-mode" from mere I-mode cooperation. The debate extends to group agents proper — List and Pettit show that a group's judgments cannot in general be any simple function of its members', which is an argument that corporations and committees are agents in their own right, aptly held responsible.

Social kinds raise a different question: how do classifications behave when the classified can read them? Hacking's looping effects mark the contrast with nature — quarks are indifferent to being labeled, but people sorted as "refugees" or "geniuses" change under the description, which then forces the category itself to shift. The stakes are highest for race and gender. Constructionists hold that these are social kinds masquerading as biological ones; Haslanger's ameliorative project goes further, asking not what our concepts do mean but what they should mean if the point of having them is to expose hierarchy — defining "woman," controversially, in terms of subordination. Here metaphysics and politics visibly meet: an account of what gender is doubles as an intervention in what it ought to become.

Finally, the field increasingly frames its oldest dispute — do social facts reduce to facts about individuals? — in the idiom of grounding. Epstein argues the individualism debate conflates two relations: what grounds a social fact (this paper's being money) and what anchors the frame that makes such grounding possible. Whether that machinery clarifies or multiplies the questions is itself contested.

Bridges

  • To metaphysics.grounding: the individualism–holism dispute is now standardly posed as a question about what grounds, and what anchors, social facts.
  • To mind: collective intentionality is shared territory — whether "we-intentions" reduce to individual intentions is a problem in the philosophy of action.
  • To language: Searle's status functions are built from his speech-act theory; declarations are the engine that creates institutional facts.
  • To politics.critical-approaches: constructionist accounts of race and gender supply the metaphysics that ideology critique presupposes.
