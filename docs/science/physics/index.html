Philosophy of Physics

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.9

Quantum mechanics is the most accurate theory ever produced, confirmed in some quantities to parts in a trillion. Nearly a century after its completion, there is no agreement about what it says the world is like. The theory assigns systems a wave function evolving smoothly and deterministically under the Schrödinger equation — an equation indifferent to size, which therefore puts pointers, cats, and observers into superpositions of incompatible states. But measurements have single outcomes. The textbook theory handles this by fiat: upon "measurement," the wave function collapses to one outcome with probabilities given by the Born rule. Since a measurement is a physical interaction like any other, singled out by no physical mark, the theory as taught contains two incompatible dynamical rules and no principled statement of when each applies. This is the measurement problem, arguably the deepest interpretive question in science.

The serious responses form a trilemma: deny the wave function is the whole story, deny it always obeys the Schrödinger equation, or deny that measurements have single outcomes. Bohmian mechanics takes the first horn: particles always have definite positions, choreographed by the wave function; measurement is ordinary correlation, quantum probability is ignorance of initial positions. Its costs are manifest nonlocality and a strained fit with relativity. Dynamical-collapse theories (GRW) take the second: collapse is a real stochastic process, negligible for one particle, nearly instantaneous for macroscopic aggregates — a rival, in-principle-testable physics, with its own puzzle about the never-quite-vanishing "tails." Everett takes the third: the Schrödinger equation holds universally, and measurement is branching — every outcome occurs, in decohered worlds. Its austerity is unmatched; its notorious cost is probability: if everything happens, what are Born probabilities probabilities of? Decision-theoretic and self-locating answers exist, controversially. The Copenhagen inheritance survives less as a theory than as a stance, refined into QBism and other epistemic views on which the wave function describes not the world but an agent's expectations. Decoherence — the fast, practically irreversible entanglement of systems with their environments — explains why macroscopic superpositions never exhibit interference, and every camp now leans on it; alone it does not solve the problem, yielding the appearance of definiteness without definite outcomes.

Bell's theorem stands apart from interpretation: any theory reproducing the confirmed correlations between distant systems must violate local causality. Loophole-free experiments thus rule out not merely local hidden variables but locality itself; what exactly relativity loses — given that the correlations permit no signaling — is the live question.

Statistical mechanics supplies the field's other great cluster. The microdynamics is time-symmetric; thermodynamic behavior is not. Boltzmann's account makes equilibration overwhelmingly probable, but by symmetry it retrodicts a higher-entropy past just as it predicts a higher-entropy future. The standard remedy is the Past Hypothesis — the universe began in extraordinarily low entropy — a boundary condition asked to ground every arrow: thermodynamic, epistemic, arguably causal. Whether a boundary condition explains, and what probability means in a deterministic dynamics (ignorance, frequency, or objective Humean chance), remain open. Finally, symmetries carry interpretive weight beyond their formal role: Noether's theorems tie them to conservation laws, and gauge symmetries look like descriptive redundancy, raising the question of when a symmetry shows a distinction to be unreal.
