Theory Change and Scientific Revolutions

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.9

Science's authority rests on a picture of steady accumulation: each theory keeps its predecessor's truths and adds more. The history of science embarrasses the picture. Newtonian mechanics did not refine Aristotelian physics; it replaced its ontology, its questions, and its standards of what counted as an answer. If theory succession is rupture rather than growth, what makes the winning side's victory rational — and if nothing does, why trust the current winners?

The modern debate begins with Kuhn's Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962). A mature science works within a paradigm — in Kuhn's later, careful usage, both a disciplinary matrix of shared commitments and a set of exemplars, concrete problem-solutions that train practitioners' judgment. Normal science is puzzle-solving under those commitments, and its dogmatism is functional: only relentless articulation of a framework exposes the anomalies that eventually break it. Crisis and revolution follow, and the successor paradigm is incommensurable with the old — a claim with three distinguishable strands. Semantic: key terms ("mass," "planet") shift meaning across the divide, so the theories do not straightforwardly contradict each other. Methodological: paradigms carry their own standards of problem-importance and solution-adequacy, so there is no neutral scorecard. Perceptual: trained scientists in some sense see different worlds. What Kuhn did not claim is that theory choice is irrational. His considered view was that shared values — accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness — genuinely constrain choice but underdetermine it, functioning as values weighed in trained judgment rather than as an algorithm. He spent much of his later career protesting the "mob psychology" reading; incommensurability, he insisted, meant no common measure, not no comparison.

The rationalist repair came from Lakatos: the unit of appraisal is not the theory but the research programme — a hard core of commitments protected by an adjustable belt of auxiliary hypotheses. A programme is progressive while it predicts novel facts and degenerating while it only accommodates; rationality lies in tracking the difference over time. The strength of the account is that it explains why clinging to a battered theory can be rational without licensing clinging forever — though Lakatos conceded that only hindsight says when persistence became unreasonable. Laudan's alternative measures progress by problem-solving effectiveness, letting a research tradition progress without its theories approaching truth — a rationalism decoupled from realism. Feyerabend pressed the provocation to its limit: every proposed methodological rule has been fruitfully violated at some pivotal episode (Galileo is his showcase), so the only universal principle is "anything goes" — best read as a reductio of rule-based rationalism, not a celebration of caprice.

Reception history turned the debate sociological. The Strong Programme in the sociology of scientific knowledge took Kuhn further than he wished: explain true and false belief symmetrically, by social causes. The ensuing "science wars" of the 1990s generated more heat than argument, but left a real question — how much of theory choice is community dynamics — that social epistemology now treats soberly. Meanwhile the continuity thesis doubts the explanandum: mathematical structure and empirical content carry over across even the deepest ruptures (Fresnel's equations survived the ether), suggesting that "revolution" may be partly a historiographical artifact — an argument that feeds directly into structural realism.
