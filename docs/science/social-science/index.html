Philosophy of the Social Sciences

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.9

A physicist studying electrons studies things that do not know they are being studied. An economist studying inflation studies people who read the economist's forecasts and change their behavior accordingly. That single difference generates the field: are the social sciences sciences in the same sense as physics — immature ones, awaiting their Newton — or is the study of human beings a different kind of enterprise altogether, one whose objects mean things, follow rules, and answer back?

The founding quarrel is naturalism against interpretivism. Naturalists hold that human phenomena admit causal explanation and, in principle, laws or law-like generalizations: societies are part of nature, and the apparent messiness of social regularities is the messiness of meteorology, not a difference in kind. Their strongest card is track record — demography, epidemiology, and economics do find robust patterns, and nothing else has produced comparable predictive grip. Interpretivists answer that social phenomena are constituted by meanings: a piece of paper is money, a gesture is an insult, only under shared self-understandings, so the basic task is not subsumption under law but Verstehen — Weber's interpretive understanding, Winch's argument that social action is rule-following and so conceptually, not merely causally, connected to its descriptions, Geertz's thick description of what a wink means. Their strongest card: you have not explained a riot, a ritual, or a market panic until you know what it meant to the participants, and no physics-style vocabulary can capture that.

Rational-choice theory is naturalism's most developed program: explain social outcomes as the aggregate of individually rational decisions. Its power is unification — voting, markets, crime, and marriage under one apparatus. Its embarrassments are systematic: experimental subjects cooperate in one-shot prisoner's dilemmas, reject profitable offers in ultimatum games, and violate expected-utility axioms in patterned ways. Whether these anomalies call for repair (behavioral economics), reinterpretation (the theory as normative standard rather than description), or replacement is a live debate.

Beneath both programs lies an ontological question: methodological individualism holds that social facts must ultimately be explained by facts about individuals — there is no "society" over and above people and their interactions — while holists reply that individuals are themselves constituted by social positions (one cannot be a debtor without an institution of credit), so the reduction never completes. The nature of the social entities at stake — institutions, groups, collective intentions — is homed in political philosophy's social ontology.

Two complications distinguish the field from its natural-science siblings. First, Hacking's interactive kinds: classifying people ("refugee," "ADHD," "genius") changes the people classified, who conform to or rebel against the label, which forces reclassification — looping effects with no analogue among quarks. Second, reflexivity: the social scientist is inside the society studied, and predictions can be self-fulfilling or self-defeating. Both pressures have pushed the field's recent consensus away from laws toward mechanisms — recurrent causal patterns (segregation dynamics, self-fulfilling prophecies, threshold effects) that explain locally without pretending to universality, a deflation many now see as the realistic ambition for social explanation. Whether objectivity survives these entanglements, and what disciplined reflexivity would look like, connects to the general problem of values in science but takes its sharpest form here.

Bridges

  • To politics: social ontology — institutions, group agents, collective intentionality — is homed there; the individualism–holism debate is its methodological shadow.
  • To epistemology: reflexivity and standpoint are a special case of the situatedness of knowers; testimony and self-knowledge bear on interpretive method.
  • To mind: action, intention, and reasons-as-causes underwrite every account of what social explanation explains.
  • Within science: the sibling values node — inductive risk and the value-free ideal bite hardest where the scientist studies her own society.
