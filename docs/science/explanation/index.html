Scientific Explanation

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.9

Science tells us not only that things happen but why. A barometer's fall predicts a storm without explaining it; the approaching pressure front explains both. Any account of science that cannot mark this difference — between tracking a phenomenon and understanding it — has missed what theories are prized for. The problem of scientific explanation is to say what the difference consists in.

The modern debate begins with Hempel's covering-law model: to explain an event is to derive it from laws of nature plus particular facts, so that explanation and prediction share one logical form. The model's austerity was its appeal — no occult "understanding," just derivation — and its downfall. Three counterexamples proved fatal. Asymmetry: from the height of a flagpole and the sun's elevation one can derive the length of its shadow, but equally derive the height from the shadow; only the first derivation explains, though both satisfy Hempel's schema. Chance without high probability: untreated syphilis explains paresis even though few syphilitics develop it, so explanation cannot require that the explanandum be expectable. Irrelevance: a man's taking birth-control pills "covers" his failure to become pregnant under a true generalization, yet explains nothing. Derivation from laws is thus neither necessary nor sufficient; something more is doing the explanatory work.

The successor programs each name that something differently. Salmon's causal-mechanical account says explanations trace the causal processes and interactions that produce the phenomenon — the flagpole causes the shadow and not conversely, which is exactly why the asymmetry runs as it does. Kitcher's unificationism says explanation is global: the best explanations are the argument patterns that derive the most phenomena from the fewest assumptions, so Newton explained by unifying tides, orbits, and falling apples. Van Fraassen's pragmatic account relocates the whole question: an explanation is an answer to a contextually fixed why-question with its contrast class ("why this rather than that?"), and there is no context-free relation of explanation for a theory to capture — a diagnosis its critics say concedes too much and its defenders say captures actual explanatory practice.

The causal approach now dominates, in two forms. Woodward's interventionism analyzes causal explanation as exhibiting what the outcome depends on — what would change under manipulation. And the new mechanist movement (Machamer, Darden, Craver) argues from the life sciences that explanation there proceeds not by subsuming under laws, of which biology has few, but by decomposing systems into organized entities and activities: the mechanism of protein synthesis or synaptic transmission is the explanation.

Two pressures push back against causal hegemony. Some explanations seem non-causal: the mathematics of graph theory explains why no one can cross Königsberg's seven bridges exactly once, and equilibrium and symmetry explanations in physics seem to work by constraint rather than causation (bridge: logic.mathematics.practice). And much scientific explanation runs through idealized models — frictionless planes, infinite populations — that are false of every actual system; how deliberate falsehood can explain is jointly claimed with models. Finally, explanation is not only an achievement to be analyzed but an engine of inference: scientists accept theories because they best explain the evidence, and whether inference to the best explanation is truth-conducive is a load-bearing wall of the realism debate (bridge: realism).
