Philosophy of Biology

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.9

Biology explains the appearance of design without a designer: the eye, the wing, the honeybee's dance are products of variation and selection, not intention. Philosophy of biology asks what that explanation actually commits us to — and finds that nearly every load-bearing concept in the science (adaptation, function, gene, species, organism) is philosophically unsettled in ways the science itself cannot ignore.

Start with adaptation. Adaptationists treat organisms as bundles of traits, each optimized by selection for a task, and the research program has real victories — optimality models predict foraging behavior and sex ratios with startling precision. Gould and Lewontin's "spandrels" critique charged that this becomes storytelling: some traits are byproducts of architecture, constraints of development, or accidents of drift, and a method that reflexively asks "what is it for?" cannot tell the difference. The live position is not either pole but a question of evidential discipline: what would confirm an adaptive hypothesis against its structural rivals?

Selection itself has a contested logic. What does selection act on — genes, organisms, groups? The gene's-eye view (Williams, Dawkins) says genes are the only replicators with the permanence selection requires, and dissolves puzzles about altruism via kin selection. Critics answer that bookkeeping in gene frequencies is not causation, and multilevel selection theory readmits groups as genuine units wherever heritable fitness differences exist at that level. Much of the debate now turns on whether the rival formalisms are empirically distinct or notational variants.

Function is where biology's teleological idiom meets naturalism. Biologists say hearts are for pumping blood and can malfunction — normative talk, on its face. Etiological theories (Wright, Millikan) naturalize it: a token's function is what its ancestors were selected for, which grounds malfunction (a heart that cannot pump still has the function) at the price of making function turn on history — a molecule-for-molecule swampman's heart would have none. Causal-role theories (Cummins) instead relativize function to a capacity of a containing system, matching physiologists' usage but losing normativity. The stakes are large: teleosemantics builds mental content on the etiological notion, so the theory of function is load-bearing far outside biology.

The species problem asks what kind of thing Homo sapiens is. The traditional answer — a natural kind with an essence — fails outright for evolving populations, whose variation is the fuel of selection, not noise around a type. Ghiselin and Hull's alternative: species are individuals — spatiotemporally bounded lineages of which organisms are parts, not instances. Meanwhile more than twenty species concepts (biological, phylogenetic, ecological) partition the living world differently, and pluralists conclude there is no single species category, only legitimate cross-cutting classifications.

Three newer fronts. Evo-devo challenges gene-centrism from within: development channels and biases variation, so explaining form may require developmental architecture, not selection alone. Biological individuality asks what counts as one organism — a symbiont-laden "holobiont," an aspen grove, a colony? — with counting consequences for fitness itself. And "genetic information" divides those who take coding talk literally from those who see a metaphor doing no explanatory work that causation could not.

The field articulates along exactly these debates, each with its own literature; evolutionary theory is the spine, but function, species, and individuality reach into metaphysics, mind, and ethics.
