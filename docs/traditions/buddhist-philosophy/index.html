Buddhist Philosophy

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.3

Buddhist philosophy is not one school but a two-millennium argument — conducted in Pāli, Sanskrit, Tibetan, Chinese, and Japanese — about what follows from a small set of shared starting points. The most consequential is no-self (anātman): there is no enduring substance underlying a person, only a causally connected stream of physical and mental events, conventionally sorted into five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, dispositions, consciousness). The canonical statement is the chariot dialogue of the Questions of King Milinda: as "chariot" names no entity over and above pole, axle, and wheels rightly arranged, so "Nāgasena" names nothing over and above the aggregates. The second shared commitment, dependent origination, generalizes the point: everything arises in dependence on conditions, and nothing possesses the independent, self-standing existence that common sense and rival metaphysics assign to things.

What divides the schools is how far the analysis cuts. Abhidharma, the tradition's first systematic metaphysics, halted it at the dharmas: momentary, unanalyzable event-particulars — flashes of color, instants of contact, pulses of feeling — that exist ultimately, while persons, chariots, and all composites exist only conventionally, as useful fictions projected onto dharma-streams. The doctrine of momentariness radicalized this into an ontology of pure process: nothing persists; apparent endurance is the rapid succession of resembling momentary events. Madhyamaka, founded by Nāgārjuna (c. 150 CE), turned the analysis on the dharmas themselves: whatever arises dependently is empty (śūnya) of intrinsic nature, so nothing — not even the Abhidharma's ultimates — exists from its own side. Nāgārjuna's signature instrument is the tetralemma, which canvasses all four positions on a question (is, is not, both, neither) and rejects each; his doctrine of two truths distinguishes conventional truth, where ordinary assertion functions perfectly well, from ultimate truth, at which no theory of intrinsic natures survives. The charge the school has answered for eighteen centuries is nihilism; its perpetual reply is that emptiness is not nonexistence but the very condition of dependent, conventional existence — indeed that only empty things can arise, change, and act. Yogācāra took a different exit from Abhidharma realism, arguing that the experienced world cannot be shown to be more than mind: what appear as external objects are transformations of consciousness, structured by the deep dispositions of a "storehouse" awareness — a position whose exact idealism is still contested by interpreters.

From Dignāga and Dharmakīrti (fifth–seventh centuries) the tradition's lingua franca became pramāṇa theory — the epistemology of perception and inference already met from logic.history. Restricting knowledge to non-conceptual perception and rule-governed inference, and analyzing general terms by apoha (exclusion), the Buddhist epistemologists fought Nyāya's realists for centuries on shared ground and by agreed rules; each side's texts are among the best sources for the other's arguments. Ethics, meanwhile, was argued from the metaphysics: Śāntideva's case for impartial compassion turns on no-self — if there is no owner of suffering, suffering is simply bad, and the boundary between "my" pain and "yours" carries no moral weight.

Transmission transformed the argument. Tibet built a scholastic culture of curriculum and formal public debate that preserved and extended the Indian controversies — its Madhyamaka disputes remain the sharpest available commentary on Nāgārjuna. Chan/Zen in East Asia moved the opposite way, cultivating a deliberate anti-scholasticism whose gestures against doctrine are themselves philosophically pointed, not artless. Modern reception runs both risks the register names: "Buddhist modernism" repackages the tradition as an empirical mind-science stripped of metaphysics, while the genuine and productive engagement with cognitive science (on attention, introspection, and the self-model) tempts a subtler flattening — reading the tetralemma as anticipatory paraconsistency, or no-self as Parfit avant la lettre. The comparisons are worth making; this article's descendants make them carefully or not at all.

Bridges

The oldest and densest bridge runs to metaphysics.personal-identity (landing this pass): the aggregate analysis and the chariot are reductionism about persons argued a millennium before its Western statements, and the Nyāya–Buddhist exchange is the debate's other great archive. Yogācāra and the meditation-and-attention literature bridge mind; pramāṇa theory bridges epistemology and, for the inference schema and apoha, logic.history. Within this register, indian-philosophy is the standing interlocutor — the same debates seen from the āstika side — and kyoto-school carries the Zen inheritance into twentieth-century philosophy.
