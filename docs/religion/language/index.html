Religious Language

◔ sketch  ·  satellite  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.13

A believer says that God is wise, that God loves the world. But "wise" and "loves" are words we learned from human cases — from finite, embodied, fallible agents. Applied unchanged to an infinite, simple, timeless being, they seem plainly false: God's wisdom is not accumulated, God's love not felt in time. Applied in some wholly different sense, they say nothing the believer can grasp: she no longer knows what she is affirming. Religious language thus threatens to be either anthropomorphic falsehood or pious noise, and every theology needs an account of how it escapes.

The oldest answer embraces the threat. The via negativa — Pseudo-Dionysius in the Christian East and West, Maimonides in the Jewish tradition, with a structural cousin in Vedānta's neti neti — holds that positive terms fail of God and that theology's proper work is denial: God is not finite, not composite, not even "existent" in the creaturely sense. Its strength is seriousness about transcendence — an unqualifiedly speakable God would not be God. Its standing problem is that unrelieved negation collapses into silence; worship seems to need something to be true of its object.

Aquinas's doctrine of analogy is the classical middle path: "wise" is said of God and creatures neither univocally nor equivocally but analogically — the perfection is really in God, as its source, though our concept of it is drawn from creatures and applies imperfectly. Against him, Scotus argued that some univocal core is non-negotiable: unless "being" and "good" mean the same in premise and conclusion, no argument from creatures to God could ever be valid. Analogy without a univocal element, Scotus holds, is just equivocation with better manners. The exchange remains the field's finest, because each side has the other's problem exactly right.

The twentieth century sharpened falsehood into meaninglessness. Verificationism ruled that a sentence with no possible empirical verification is cognitively empty, and Flew's falsification challenge pressed the point: what would the believer accept as counting against "God loves us"? A claim that dies the "death by a thousand qualifications" was never a claim. The program collapsed — no formulation of the criterion spared science while condemning theology, and the criterion failed its own test — but the challenge left a permanent demand: say what religious sentences are for.

Non-cognitivists answered by conceding the point. Braithwaite read religious assertions as declarations of commitment to a way of life, accompanied by stories one need not believe — an account whose strength is its fit with religion's action-guiding character and whose cost is revisionism, since believers take themselves to be stating truths. Wittgensteinian accounts (D. Z. Phillips) refuse the concession's terms: religious language is not a failed quasi-scientific theory but the expression of a form of life with its own grammar — "eternal life" is not a hypothesis about post-mortem duration. The position's insight is that meaning lives in use; the standing charge (Nielsen's "Wittgensteinian fideism") is that it insulates religion from criticism by fiat. A fourth line rehabilitates cognitive ambition through metaphor and models: Soskice argues that metaphor can be reality-depicting without paraphrase, as in science's models, so theology may refer to God while describing him only obliquely.
