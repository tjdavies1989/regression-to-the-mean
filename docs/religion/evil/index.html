The Problem of Evil

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.13

An omnipotent God could prevent every evil; an omniscient God would know how; a perfectly good God would want to. Yet a child dies slowly of a disease no free choice caused. The problem of evil is the demand that these four facts be made to fit — and the standing suspicion that they cannot. It is the oldest and still the strongest argument against theism, and unlike the arguments for God's existence it needs no technical apparatus to feel: every hospital ward states the premise.

The logical argument, given canonical form by Mackie, claims outright contradiction: the theist's own commitments entail that evil should not exist, and it does. Plantinga's free-will defense answers that no contradiction has been shown — it is possible that God could not have created free creatures guaranteed always to choose rightly, since perhaps every creaturely essence suffers transworld depravity, going wrong in some circumstance in any world where it is significantly free. A defense needs only possibility, not truth, and for decades the received verdict, conceded even by prominent atheists, was that Mackie's argument is dead. That verdict has lately been reopened: the defense leans on contested Molinist machinery about what free creatures would do, it says nothing obvious about natural evil (Plantinga's demons are widely felt to strain even mere possibility), and critics such as Sterba argue that a good God would at least prevent the horrendous evils that no free-will good could plausibly require.

Most philosophers therefore moved to the evidential argument, where the claim is not contradiction but disconfirmation. Rowe's fawn — burned in a forest fire no human witnesses, suffering days before dying — is offered as an evil that, so far as we can see, serves no outweighing good; probably some such evils are gratuitous; a perfect God would permit none. Draper sharpens the logic by comparison: the actual distribution of pleasure and pain is far less surprising on an indifferent universe than on theism, so evil is evidence, in the Bayesian sense, against God.

Skeptical theism replies that "so far as we can see" is worthless testimony: if God exists, we should expect many divine reasons to lie beyond our ken, so our failure to find a justifying good for the fawn's agony confirms nothing. The reply is powerful and priced accordingly. If we can never judge that an evil is unjustified, can we judge that we ought to prevent one — or trust any inference from how things seem to how they are where God's purposes might intrude? The threatened costs are moral paralysis and a skepticism that leaks out of theology into everything.

Theodicies attempt more: to say what the justifying goods actually are. Hick's soul-making theodicy holds that a world of real hazard is the only forge for genuine virtue; free-will theodicies extend Plantinga's possibility into an asserted truth; felix culpa proposes that a fallen and redeemed world is better than an innocent one. Against the whole genre stands the anti-theodicy tradition — from Dostoevsky's Ivan to post-Holocaust Jewish thought — which holds that explaining horrendous evils mislocates them morally: some suffering should be protested, not priced.

Alongside evil now stands its quieter companion. Schellenberg's hiddenness argument observes that a perfectly loving God would be open to relationship with every willing creature, yet there is nonresistant nonbelief; the argument needs no suffering at all, only silence, and has become atheism's second engine.
