Religious Diversity and Pluralism

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.13

The world's religions cannot all be right. God is triune or God is strictly one; persons are reborn or they are not; ultimate reality is a personal creator, or an impersonal Brahman, or no thing at all. Yet every one of these incompatible claims is held by intelligent, morally serious, epistemically careful people — and held, overwhelmingly, by people born into the tradition that teaches it. Had the devout Christian been born in Varanasi, she would in all likelihood be a devout Hindu, and she knows it. The problem of religious diversity is what to make of this: whether any tradition can reasonably claim truth in the face of the others, and whether the facts of plurality themselves constitute evidence that all are mistaken.

Exclusivism holds that one tradition is true and the others err where they conflict with it. Its strength is that it is not a special doctrine but the ordinary logic of belief: to believe anything is to regard its deniers as wrong, and the geographer does not abandon the round earth because flat-earthers exist. Plantinga defends the position's epistemic decency directly — the exclusivist violates no duty and need claim no superiority, only that her faculties, if working as designed, deliver truth. Inclusivism, classically Rahner's "anonymous Christians," keeps one tradition's truth while extending its saving reach through the others: grace operates incognito in the sincere Buddhist. It honors the evident sanctity found everywhere at the price of telling other traditions what they really are — a courtesy few wish to receive.

Hick's pluralist hypothesis is the field's most ambitious proposal: behind the personae (Yahweh, Allah, Vishnu) and impersonae (Brahman, the Dao, śūnyatā) of the traditions stands the Real an sich, never experienced as it is in itself; each great faith is a culturally conditioned but authentic response to it, and each is validated not doctrinally but soteriologically, by its capacity to turn lives from self-centeredness to Reality-centeredness. The machinery is Kantian — phenomena all the way out — and the standing worries are self-undermining ones. If the Real transcends every substantial predicate, including "one," "good," and "personal," it is unclear that the hypothesis says anything, or that the Real could ground the traditions rather than float free of them. And pluralism must tell every tradition that its self-understanding is false — the Christian does not take herself to be responding to an unknowable X — which makes pluralism look like one more exclusivism, located a level up, about which the same diversity problem recurs.

The epistemic version of the problem is now often run through the general theory of peer disagreement: if my religious peers — equally informed, equally able — reach incompatible conclusions, conciliationism seems to demand that all of us retreat toward agnosticism. The twist that makes the religious case the hardest instance is that there are no neutral experts: each tradition's authorities are formed by its practices, texts, and reported experiences, so the disagreeing parties do not share evidence and cannot easily be certified as peers — which either blunts the skeptical argument or radicalizes it, since the contingency of one's own formation is precisely the datum. The salvific version — who can be saved, not who can be justified — cross-cuts the first: one can be an epistemic exclusivist and a salvific universalist without strain. Whether pluralism is itself a religious position — a revisionary theology competing with the faiths rather than refereeing them — remains the field's sharpest open question.
