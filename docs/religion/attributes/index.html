Divine Attributes

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.13

Before asking whether God exists, one needs to know what "God" is supposed to mean — and the classical answer is a stack of superlatives: all-powerful, all-knowing, perfectly good, eternal, without parts, beyond change. The divine attributes are the project of saying precisely what each superlative amounts to, and the repeated discovery that precision breeds paradox. The stakes are not decorative. If the attributes cannot be stated coherently, atheism wins before any evidence is heard; if they can be stated only by weakening them, the being that survives may not be worth the name.

The engine of the whole enterprise is perfect-being methodology, Anselm's formula made into a research program (Morris): God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived, so God has the maximal consistent set of great-making properties. The method both generates the attributes and tests them — when an analysis collapses, revise the attribute rather than surrender the being. Its strength is discipline: theology becomes conceptual engineering with a criterion. Its weakness is that intuitions about "greatness" are trained on human excellences and may be parochial; the method risks building an idol of theory.

Omnipotence meets its stone: can God create a stone too heavy for God to lift? Either answer names something God cannot do. The quick repair — omnipotence ranges only over the possible, and "a stone its omnipotent maker cannot lift" describes nothing — handles the stone but not the harder cases: a necessarily perfect God cannot lie, sin, or despair, while any human can. The Flint–Freddoso analysis answers with maximal power over states of affairs, relativized to a world's history and consistent with the agent's own nature, so that necessary goodness restricts what God can do without diminishing power. Geach's dissent stands at the edge of the field: no analysis works, and faith needs only "almighty," not "omnipotent."

Omniscience collides with creaturely freedom. If God already believed yesterday what I will do tomorrow, and the past is fixed, my act seems fixed too. The responses map the field: the Boethian view takes God outside time — timeless knowledge is not foreknowledge and threatens nothing; Ockhamism counts God's past beliefs as soft facts over which we hold counterfactual power; Molinism gives God middle knowledge of what any free creature would do in any circumstance, buying meticulous providence without determinism at the price of the grounding objection — what makes such counterfactuals true?; open theism denies that future free acts are there to be known, keeping omniscience as knowledge of all that can be known and accepting a God who takes risks.

Eternity divides the timeless from the everlasting. The classical view (Boethius, Aquinas) makes God atemporal, defended by Stump and Kretzmann's ET-simultaneity, on which one eternal present can be co-present with every moment of time. The everlastingness view (Wolterstorff, Swinburne) objects that a timeless being could not respond, remember, or intervene — could not be the living God of any scripture. Simplicity is the austerest classical thesis: God has no parts, no distinction between essence and existence, attributes identical with God and one another (Augustine, Ibn Sīnā, Aquinas). Plantinga's critique — a simple God would be a property, and a property cannot create — set the analytic agenda; truthmaker-style defenses answer that simplicity misread as identity-of-abstracta was never the doctrine. Impassibility, the corollary that God cannot suffer or be acted upon, held for two millennia and cracked in the twentieth century, when passibilists argued that a God who cannot suffer cannot love a suffering world.
