Logic

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 0  ·  pass 2026-08-21.1

Some arguments cannot fail. If all whales are mammals and Moby is a whale, then Moby is a mammal — and the "cannot" here seems absolute: no discovery about whales, no rearrangement of the world, could break the link. Logic is the discipline that takes that "cannot" as its subject. What is it for a conclusion to follow from premises? Why does the guarantee seem to hold in virtue of the argument's shape alone, and to be knowable from an armchair? And what happens to the guarantee at the edges — where language talks about itself, where predicates go vague, where mathematics outruns proof?

The division's center is the theory of consequence, where the pretheoretic notion resolves into three strands — the conclusion cannot be false if the premises are true (necessity); this can be known without investigating the subject matter (apriority); and it holds in virtue of form, not content (formality) — and two great analyses compete to cash them out. The Tarskian, model-theoretic account says an argument is valid when every reinterpretation of its nonlogical vocabulary that makes the premises true makes the conclusion true: formality as invariance. The proof-theoretic account, descending from Gentzen, says validity is derivability by the rules that give the logical constants their meanings: an epistemically satisfying picture — derivations are surveyable, totalities of models are not — with a standing obligation, since Prior's rogue connective "tonk," to say which rules are meaning-conferring. For first-order logic the two provably coincide; that is Gödel's completeness theorem, and each side reads the coincidence as its own vindication. They come apart in second-order logic, where consequence outruns every proof procedure — which is why the dispute is substantive, not notational. Over the whole debate hangs Etchemendy's charge that the Tarskian definition is a disguised material generalization whose match with genuine consequence would be a set-theoretic accident, and Kreisel's squeezing argument, which many take to certify the extension while leaving the analysis unblessed.

Classical logic — propositional and first-order — is the system in which all this is standardly conducted: the default logic, the one against which every rival defines itself, with bivalence and excluded middle as its signature commitments. Its metatheory is philosophy by other means. Completeness yields compactness, compactness yields nonstandard models, and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems yield the embarrassment that no first-order theory pins down the natural numbers or distinguishes the countable from the uncountable by brute force — results that Skolem pressed toward set-theoretic relativism and Putnam toward wholesale skepticism about determinate reference. Second-order logic buys back categoricity at the price of completeness, and the half-century dispute over whether it is logic at all (Quine: "set theory in sheep's clothing"; Boolos and Shapiro in the defense) is really a dispute about what logic is for.

Around the classical core stand two different kinds of neighbor. The modal and intensional logics are extensions: add "necessarily" and its dual, and Kripke's relational semantics turns rival axiom systems into rival pictures of relative possibility — the field's great success, since different modalities demonstrably want different structures. Quantifying into modal contexts revived essentialism from Quine's condemnation of it, and the possible-worlds apparatus runs up an ontological bill — Lewis's concrete worlds, ersatz constructions, or fiction — that is presented for payment in metaphysics, not here. The intensional fleet (epistemic, deontic, temporal logic, and the Lewis–Stalnaker treatment of counterfactuals) exports the machinery across philosophy, and the hyperintensional phenomena at its far edge suggest even worlds are too coarse. The nonclassical logics, by contrast, are rivals: systems on which classically valid arguments fail. Intuitionism rejects excluded middle, with two independent motivations — Brouwer's constructive mathematics and Dummett's meaning-theoretic antirealism; relevance logic indicts the material conditional and explosion; paraconsistent logics tolerate contradiction, with Priest's dialetheism willing to call the liar sentence both true and false; many-valued and fuzzy systems bid for vagueness; substructural logics locate the whole terrain's fault lines in the structural rules; and quantum logic's rise and fall is the cautionary tale about physics revising logic. What would make one of these correct is not their question but philosophy of logic's.

The paradoxes are where the rivalries stop being optional. The liar sentence is a proof that classical logic, semantic self-reference, and the transparency of truth are jointly inconsistent; every theory of truth chooses which to surrender. Tarski stratified truth into a hierarchy; Kripke let it go partial and met the revenge liar one level up; Field and Priest revise the logic itself; Curry's paradox, which runs without negation, referees them all and pushes some theorists to blame the rule of contraction. The sorites does for vagueness what the liar does for truth — epistemicism, supervaluationism, and degree theories are the live escapes — and Russell's paradox, having killed naive set theory, left both the cumulative hierarchy and the suspicion (Priest's inclosure schema, indefinite extensibility) that all the paradoxes are one. By editorial decision the nature of truth — correspondence, deflationism, pluralism — is homed in language.truth; the formal theories and the paradoxes live here.

Philosophy of logic is the division's self-knowledge: which expressions are the logical ones (Tarski's permutation-invariance proposal and its critics); whether there is one correct logic, many (Beall–Restall pluralism), or none; whether logic is normative for thought, as Frege held and Harman doubted; and whether logic is epistemically exceptional or just the most abstract science, revisable like the rest — the anti-exceptionalist position now setting much of the agenda.

Logic's most consequential export remains mathematics. The technical cluster — proof theory, model theory, set theory, computability — is housed as mathematical logic, each limb carrying philosophical yield (Gentzen's consistency proofs, nonstandard models, independence and large cardinals, the Church–Turing thesis); Gödel's incompleteness theorems live there technically while their import is adjudicated next door. Philosophy of mathematics, the division's largest limb, now descended to its own layer, organizes itself around Benacerraf's dilemma — our best semantics wants mathematical objects, our best epistemology cannot reach them — with platonism, nominalism, structuralism, logicism, formalism, and intuitionism as the classic escape routes, the foundations question (set-theoretic, categorial, univalent) and the philosophy of practice as its modern fronts.

At the margins, deliberately thin: argumentation, the study of real arguments, where formal validity underdetermines rational persuasion; and the division's memory, the history of logic — which records, among other things, that formal logic was invented at least twice, in Greece and in India, that the "closed and completed" science Kant described was two generations from revolution, and that the revolution was Frege's.

Bridges

  • Language: logical form and regimentation, the truth predicate's home division, and intensional semantics — Frege's revolution belonged to both fields at once.
  • Metaphysics: modality and possible worlds, essentialism, abstract objects, and vagueness-in-the-world make this the busiest border in the encyclopedia.
  • Epistemology: the apriority of logic, logical knowledge and evidence, and formal epistemology's Bayesian machinery next door.
  • Mind: the Church–Turing thesis and the Lucas–Penrose arguments about minds and machines; logical omniscience as a problem for any logic of knowledge.
  • Figures and traditions: aristotle, frege, godel in the figures register; the Stoics, the Nyāya school, the medievals, and the Vienna Circle through history and the traditions register.
