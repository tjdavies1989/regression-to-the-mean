The Sorites and Vagueness

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-08-21.2

One grain of sand is not a heap, and surely one grain never makes the difference: if n grains are not a heap, neither are n+1. But chain ten thousand instances of that conditional together by modus ponens and you have proved that ten thousand grains are not a heap — that nothing is. Run the argument on "bald," "tall," "red," "child," and it consumes most of a natural language. The premises are near-irresistible, the logic is elementary, and the conclusion is absurd.

The comfortable reply — "just draw a line somewhere" — mistakes the problem for a bookkeeping chore. Suppose the line falls at 4,317 grains, so that 4,317 make a heap and 4,316 do not. What could make that true? No speaker's usage discriminates the two; no dictionary, intention, or convention reaches that far down. The sorites does not complain that we have failed to draw a line; it argues that nothing — not meaning, not use, not the world — is in a position to draw one, while classical logic quietly insists that one is drawn.

Epistemicism (Williamson) accepts the insistence. There is a sharp cutoff, fixed somehow by the total pattern of use, and our ignorance of it is principled: knowledge requires a margin for error, and no belief about a cutoff our usage barely constrains could be safe from error in nearby cases. Its strength is conservatism — classical logic and bivalence survive untouched, and the ignorance is explained rather than posited. The price is semantic incredulity: that our drifting talk of heaps determines a fact to the single grain, which no one could ever know.

Supervaluationism locates the softness in the semantics. A vague predicate admits many acceptable sharpenings; a sentence is true if true on all of them — supertrue — false if false on all, otherwise neither. Borderline attributions get no value, yet penumbral connections are preserved: "anything redder than a red thing is red" holds on every sharpening, and so, awkwardly, does "some single grain makes the difference," though no instance of it does. The persistent leak is higher-order vagueness: "acceptable sharpening" is as vague as "heap," the border between the definite and the borderline is as soft as the one it replaced, and the repair regenerates the disease one level up.

Degree theories let truth itself shade off: each conditional premise is very nearly true, long chains of almost-valid steps slowly bleed truth away, and the sorites becomes a lesson in the leakage. Their standing embarrassment is an artifact of the machinery — some sentence true to degree exactly 0.5, some adjacent pair of grains differing precisely in truth value — sharper facts than the ones the theory was built to deny.

Contextualism (Raffman, Shapiro) says the cutoff is real but restless: items considered together must be judged alike, so wherever your attention lands, the boundary is not there. This fits the forced-march experience — competent speakers do jump somewhere, never where they are looking — though critics ask whether the function from contexts to cutoffs is not itself soritical. Nihilism (Unger) takes the argument at face value: it is sound, so there are no heaps, no bald men, perhaps no tables or persons. Its honesty is its argument; its cost is everything.

Beneath the rivalry lies the classificatory question: is vagueness a feature of our representations, of our knowledge, or of the world itself? The last option — vague objects, vague identity — is homed in metaphysics.
