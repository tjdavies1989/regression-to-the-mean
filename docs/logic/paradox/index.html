Paradox and Truth

◕ article  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-09-09.20

"This sentence is false." If it is true, then what it says holds, so it is false; if it is false, then what it says fails, so it is true. The liar is not a curiosity; it is a proof. Three things we cannot easily surrender — classical logic, a language's capacity to talk about its own sentences, and the principle that "A" is true if and only if A — are jointly inconsistent, and every theory of truth is at bottom a decision about which to give up and how to present the bill. This node maps the paradoxes as a family and the theories built to survive them. Its six children each own a region: the liar and the structural problem of revenge (logic.paradox.liar); the formal theories of truth and their proof-theoretic scorecard (logic.paradox.formal-truth); Curry's paradox and the case against structural rules (logic.paradox.curry); the sorites and the theories of vagueness (logic.paradox.vagueness); the set-theoretic paradoxes and the absolute-generality debate they spawned (logic.paradox.set-paradoxes); and Yablo's paradox as the family's controlled experiment (logic.paradox.yablo). This article treats each region at depth and then takes up the question the children keep handing upward: whether the paradoxes are one phenomenon or several.

Two editorial decisions frame everything below. First, the home split with language.truth: the nature of truth — correspondence, coherence, deflationism, pluralism, primitivism — is homed there; this node and its children own the paradoxes and the formal theories. The border is busy in both directions: deflationism stakes itself on the schema the liar refutes, and which formal theory one accepts constrains what truth can be. Second, the nonclassical logics deployed against the paradoxes — paracomplete, paraconsistent, substructural — are homed as systems at logic.nonclassical; here they appear as diagnoses, and the paradoxes are the strongest working argument that any of them is needed.

In the usage that has settled since Sainsbury, a paradox is an apparently sound argument from acceptable premises to an unacceptable conclusion; the label carries no verdict. The family treated here shares more than that form: each member is generated by a totality turning on itself — a sentence on its own truth, a set on its own membership, a series of small steps on the predicate they exhaust — and that shared genesis is the raw material of the unity question with which the article ends.

Origins and the modern turn

The liar and the sorites have the same author. Eubulides of Miletus, a contemporary of Aristotle in the Megarian school, is credited by Diogenes Laërtius with a list of puzzles including the liar, the heap, the bald man, and the hooded man; that self-reference and vagueness were born together is one of the odder facts of the family's history. Chrysippus wrote on the liar at length and prescribed for the sorites a policy of silence: the wise man stops answering before the doubtful cases. Bobzien has argued that the policy is best read as an early epistemicism rather than a rejection of bivalence. The thirteenth and fourteenth centuries produced solutions to the insolubilia whose sophistication was recovered only in the twentieth: the restringentes forbade self-reference; the cassantes held that the liar says nothing; Bradwardine argued that a proposition signifies everything that follows from what it signifies, so that any proposition signifying its own falsity also signifies its own truth — the liar therefore signifies both and is simply false; Paul of Venice catalogued fifteen solutions. Read's revival of Bradwardine's theory as a principled restriction of the truth schema shows the medieval material to be a live source, not merely an ancestry.

Modern paradoxology is a product of the 1890s and 1900s, and its first generation was set-theoretic. Burali-Forti's argument about the greatest ordinal appeared in 1897; Cantor found the same cliff among the cardinals; Russell found his class of non-self-membered classes in 1901, sent it to Frege in June 1902, and published it in 1903. The semantic paradoxes were rediscovered in the same decade as if to insist on the family resemblance: Richard's definable reals (1905), König's least indefinable ordinal, Berry's least integer not nameable in fewer than nineteen syllables, and the Grelling–Nelson paradox of "heterological" (1908). Russell's response was the first uniform diagnosis: the vicious-circle principle, which forbids any totality to contain members definable only in terms of that totality, enforced by the ramified theory of types (1908) and Principia Mathematica.

Ramsey's partition of 1925 undid the uniformity. The logical paradoxes — Russell, Burali-Forti, Cantor — involve only mathematical notions and fall to the simple theory of types; the semantic ones — the liar, Richard, Berry, Grelling — involve meaning, truth, and definability, belong to linguistics rather than mathematics, and need no ramification. The partition licensed the century's division of labor. Then Gödel and Tarski turned the liar into theorems. Gödel's 1931 paper notes the analogy between its construction and the liar; Tarski's monograph on truth (Polish 1933, German 1935) proved that no classical language expressing enough arithmetic can contain its own truth predicate, by showing that if it did, the liar could be built in it. The diagonal lemma — for any predicate, a sentence saying of itself that it satisfies the predicate — became the family's engine, and the paradoxes ceased to be puzzles about natural language and became limitative results about formal systems, with natural language, on Tarski's verdict, inconsistent.

The contemporary field begins in a short span of years: Parsons's contextual approach (1974); the fixed-point constructions of Martin and Woodruff and of Kripke (1975); the 1975 papers of Dummett, Fine, and Wright from which every modern theory of vagueness descends; Priest's "The Logic of Paradox" (1979); Burge's contextual hierarchy (1979); Herzberger's and Gupta's revision theory (1982); and Yablo's work on groundedness (1982, 1985).

The liar, the truth-teller, and revenge

The liar's inconsistency proof repays dismantling, because every solution is a decision about which part to disown. Let L be a sentence provably equivalent to "L is not true" — which the diagonal lemma guarantees in any language with modest arithmetic and a truth predicate. The truth schema gives T(L) ↔ L; substitution gives T(L) ↔ ¬T(L); classical logic turns a biconditional between a sentence and its negation into a contradiction, and explosion turns the contradiction into everything. Four ingredients, then, not three: the schema in both directions; the sentence's existence; the operational rules for negation; and, least visibly, the structural rules — contraction, since the supposition T(L) is used more than once, and cut, since the short derivations are chained. logic.paradox.liar treats the first three; logic.paradox.curry the fourth.

The comfortable response — the liar is neither true nor false — meets the strengthened liar, "this sentence is not true," which says exactly what the gap theorist has just asserted of it. The child article draws two further lessons. The truth-teller, "this sentence is true," is consistent on either assignment and therefore constrained by nothing, so the pathology in the neighborhood is ungroundedness, of which contradiction is only the loudest symptom. And Kripke's Nixon–Dean cases show that whether an ordinary attribution of truth is paradoxical can depend on the contingent distribution of facts, so that paradox cannot be quarantined by syntax. Together the lessons retire the oldest cure, the prohibition on self-reference: self-reference is not sufficient for paradox (the truth-teller), not detectable in advance, and, on the reading logic.paradox.yablo contests, not necessary either (Yablo's sequence).

The solutions now on offer sort by the ingredient surrendered.

Hierarchical and contextual theories keep classical logic and the schema and restrict the sentence. Tarski's hierarchy does so by fiat. Its contextual descendants — Parsons, Burge, Glanzberg, Simmons's "singularity" theory — accept that natural language has one word "true" but hold that its extension shifts with context: the liar, assessed from the standpoint its utterance creates, comes out not true, and that verdict, made from a later standpoint, is true without contradiction. Their best argument is that this is what speakers do: confronted with the liar's reasoning we say "so it isn't true after all." Their cost is that the hierarchy has been hidden in pragmatics rather than dissolved, and that no one has stated in the theory's own terms, without paradox, the generalization that every liar is untrue at some level.

Paracomplete theories keep the schema and the sentence and weaken the logic by refusing to assert excluded middle for the pathological sentences. Kripke's construction is the model; Soames, Maudlin, and above all Field are its philosophical developments. Field adds a conditional, interpreted by a transfinite revision over Strong Kleene fixed points, for which the biconditionals hold without restriction, and a determinacy operator iterable through the transfinite. The approach's best argument is that it preserves the naive schema — the one thing about truth everyone begins by accepting — and that its consistency is a model-theoretic theorem. The best objections are revenge, treated below, and the complaint that the logic is intricate and its silence about exclusion negation no answer.

Paraconsistent theories, Priest's dialetheism first among them, keep the schema and the sentence and weaken the logic in the other direction: the liar is both true and false, and since explosion fails in the Logic of Paradox the contradiction does not spread. The best argument is the one the child names: dialetheism alone takes the paradox at face value and treats the strengthened liar as a datum. Its costs are the standing objections to true contradictions — what is it to deny something, if asserting its negation does not exclude it? — the "false only" revenge, and Curry, which yields not a contradiction but everything and forces Priest, like Field, to build a special conditional. Beall's Spandrels of Truth (2009) is the most austere version, treating gluts as by-products of the truth device.

The revision theory, Gupta and Belnap's, keeps classical logic and the sentence and reads the schema as a rule of revision; the liar's signature is instability. Inconsistency theories — Chihara (1979), Eklund, Scharp's Replacing Truth (2013) — hold that the concept of truth is inconsistent and that the right response is not to find the consistent theory we always had but to replace the concept for theoretical purposes, as Scharp does with paired "ascending" and "descending" truth predicates. Their best argument is candor: every other theory proposes a new concept while pretending to analyze the old one. Classical restriction — typed theories, Horwich's confinement of the minimalist schema to unproblematic instances — keeps everything but the full schema, at the cost that no principled specification of the unproblematic instances exists (McGee).

Revenge is the problem that organizes the field, and logic.paradox.liar states its two readings: engineering (Field), on which the remaining liars deploy notions no consistent language could express, so that their inexpressibility is a discovery; and phenomenon, on which every consistent theory buys safety with silence and differs only in where it hides what it cannot say. Two recent developments sharpen the choice. Murzi and Rossi's "generalised revenge" (2020) argues that a wide class of theories — paracomplete, paraconsistent, substructural — that can express, with their own resources, that a sentence is not defective face a revenge liar under minimal assumptions. And Williamson's abductive argument (2017) reframes the dialectic: if logic is chosen by the theoretical virtues, classical logic's simplicity, strength, and track record outweigh the cost of restricting the truth schema. Field and Priest reply that the naive schema is not a hypothesis to be weighed but the meaning of the word, and that a classical theory unable to say whether the liar is true has restricted more than a schema.

Formal theories of truth and the proof-theoretic scorecard

The cures, stated precisely enough to be billed, are collected in logic.paradox.formal-truth; this section supplies the history of their measurement and the state of the scorecard.

Tarski's monograph did three things that are often run together: it laid down Convention T as the criterion of adequacy — a definition of truth must entail every instance of the schema; it gave a definition meeting the criterion, by recursion on satisfaction, for any formalized language not containing its own truth predicate; and it proved the restriction forced, since a classical language rich enough to express its own syntax and containing its own truth predicate would contain the liar. The indefinability theorem is Gödel's diagonal argument turned on semantics, and its yield is that "true" must be stratified, that natural language, which refuses stratification, is on Tarski's view inconsistent, and — the point the deflationists took — that a theory of truth can be adequate without saying what truth is.

Kripke's construction removed the stratification by making truth partial. Start with an empty extension for T; apply a jump that adds to the extension every sentence that comes out true under the Strong Kleene scheme, and to the anti-extension every sentence that comes out false; the jump is monotone, so iteration reaches fixed points, and at the least of them a sentence has a value exactly when it is grounded — when the question of its truth bottoms out in the world. The liar is never reached; the truth-teller is reached in non-minimal fixed points and assigned opposite values in different ones. Kripke's own verdict was that the hierarchy's ghost survives, since groundedness and the fixed points are described in a classical metalanguage in which "the liar is ungrounded" is true and the object language cannot say so.

The axiomatic turn is the last forty years' main technical development: Feferman's KF (published 1991) axiomatizes Kripke's fixed points in classical logic; Friedman and Sheard (1987) surveyed the consistent combinations of natural truth principles and isolated FS; Cantini calibrated KF and VF; Halbach's Axiomatic Theories of Truth organized the field. The scorecard, as the child states it: TB, the Tarski biconditionals for truth-free sentences, is conservative over Peano arithmetic. CT, compositional truth with the truth predicate admitted into induction, proves the consistency of PA and matches arithmetical comprehension with full induction. FS keeps the symmetric rules from A to T(A) and back, and is by McGee's theorem (1985) ω-inconsistent. KF proves the liar and proves that the liar is not true, an external classical theory outrunning the partial notion it describes, and matches ramified analysis through every level below ε₀. VF, axiomatizing supervaluational fixed points, is stronger still, at the level of the theory of one inductive definition.

Three developments since the child's sketch bear on the scorecard. First, the conservativeness of compositional truth without extended induction, CT⁻ — established model-theoretically by Kotlarski, Krajewski, and Lachlan (1981) and proof-theoretically by Leigh (2015) — shows where CT's strength comes from: not the compositional axioms but the admission of truth into induction. The deflationist reply to Shapiro and Ketland is thereby a theorem, not a hope. Second, the "Tarski boundary" program (Cieśliński 2017; Łełyk and Wcisło; Enayat and Pakhomov) maps the line between conservative and non-conservative truth theories and finds it startlingly close to CT⁻: bounded induction for the truth predicate, or even the principle that a disjunction of whatever length is true just in case a disjunct is, crosses it. Whether a deflationist may stand on the conservative side, or whether so weak a predicate cannot do the generalizing work deflationism assigns it (Waxman 2017), is the present state of the conservativeness debate. Third, the comparison between KF and its nonclassical counterpart PKF (Halbach and Horsten, 2006) shows that giving up classical logic costs mathematical strength — PKF reaches only the levels of ramified analysis below ω^ω — and Halbach and Nicolai (2018) trace the loss to induction under nonclassical logic rather than to any truth principle. This "cost of nonclassical logic" now weighs against Field-style theories; the reply that mathematics can be done in a classical fragment while truth requires the weaker logic is the current front.

The revision theory stands to one side of the scorecard. It treats the truth schema as a rule for revising a hypothesized extension — apply it, iterate, watch which sentences stabilize — and offers the liar's endless oscillation not as a defect but as an explanation; its philosophical claim is that truth is a circular concept and that circular concepts are legitimate objects of a general semantics. Its best argument is that it keeps classical logic throughout and explains rather than silences the liar. Its costs, as the child notes, are the disputed limit rules — Herzberger's, Gupta's, and Belnap's policies at limit ordinals give different verdicts — and the complexity results (Kremer; Welch) placing the stably true sentences far beyond hyperarithmetical definability, which raises the question whether so inaccessible a concept can be the one speakers deploy.

Curry's paradox and the structural turn

Curry's 1942 paper showed that combinatory logic with an unrestricted conditional is inconsistent; Moh Shaw-Kwei (1954), Geach (1955), and Prior (1955) carried the point into the theory of truth, where "if this sentence is true, then A" yields A, for arbitrary A, from the truth schema, conditional proof, and modus ponens alone. Löb's theorem (1955) is the same derivation formalized in arithmetic with "provable" for "true." For decades it was a specialists' footnote (Meyer, Routley, and Dunn 1979; Restall 1993). It became central when the gap and glut theories of the liar were found, as logic.paradox.curry reports, helpless before a paradox containing no negation.

The child's inventory isolates contraction — the structural rule that lets a supposition be used twice and discharged once — as the ingredient every other response leaves in place. The responses now divide into three families.

The first restricts the conditional. Field's conditional does not contract — from A → (A → B) one may not pass to A → B — and Priest's and Beall's reject the same principle; each keeps the naive schema and modus ponens at the price of a conditional that misbehaves under nested supposition. Their best argument is minimal disruption: the structural rules, and with them classical mathematics, stay put. Their weakness, the substructuralists argue from the validity paradox, is that the repair is local to a connective while the problem is not.

The second is substructural, and it is the turn that most distinguishes the field's last fifteen years. Non-contractive theories — Zardini's (2011) is the most developed — drop contraction across the board, so that no paradox of self-reference, the liar included, can complete. Their cost is arithmetic, which leans on contraction in induction: recapturing it without smuggling contraction back is unsolved, and non-contractive theories with infinitary quantifier rules harbor ω-paradoxes of their own (Fjellstad; Da Ré and Rosenblatt). Non-transitive theories keep contraction and drop cut. Ripley (2013), with the strict-tolerant logic ST of Cobreros, Égré, Ripley, and van Rooij, validates every classically valid inference and every instance of the naive schema, and blocks the paradoxes only by refusing to chain derivations: cut fails exactly at the pathological sentences. ST's best argument is that it is classical logic at the level of inferences, with the transparent truth predicate classical logic was supposed to be unable to have. The objection, pressed by Barrio, Pailos, and Szmuc (2020) and Scambler (2020), is that its classicality is one level deep: the metainferences — inferences among inferences, cut itself among them — are not classical, and the pattern iterates into a hierarchy of logics each classical at one level and not at the next, a structural revenge. Non-reflexive theories (French 2016; Nicolai and Rossi 2018) drop the rule that A follows from A for the pathological sentences.

The third family disputes the validity paradox that motivates the second. Validity Curry — anticipated by Whittle (2004), stated in deflationary form by Shapiro (2011), named by Beall and Murzi (2013) — replaces "true" with "valid": an argument from a sentence to absurdity, where the sentence says that that very argument is valid, yields absurdity from the principles that valid arguments may be applied and that what is derived is validly derived, together with contraction. No conditional appears, so the first family's repair is unavailable. Replies attack the principles. Ketland (2012) observes that for any fixed logic validity is definable in arithmetic and the arithmetized predicate does not satisfy the derivation-certifying rule, so the paradox shows only that naive validity, like naive truth, is not the validity of any one logic; Cook (2014) indexes the validity predicate to the logic it expresses, dissolving the paradox into a hierarchy; Field (2017) holds that the object-language rule certifying a derivation as valid is unmotivated in the form the paradox requires. Zardini (2014) and Murzi and Shapiro (2015) reply that these are restrictions on naive validity of just the kind Tarski imposed on truth. The child's verdict — that v-Curry is the strongest argument that the substructural response is where the dialectic was always headed — describes the present balance without settling it.

The Löb parallel is the region's standing puzzle: the same diagonal reasoning is catastrophe for naive truth and a theorem for provability, because provability declines reflection — a theory proves "if A is provable then A" only when it proves A. On the reading the child favors, the derivation is beyond reproach and paradox lives entirely in the bridge principles a predicate obeys.

The sorites and vagueness

Vagueness has the family's longest continuous history and the most theories. Frege set the modern terms by demanding that every concept have sharp boundaries — a predicate lacking one has no reference — and so excluded vagueness from logic rather than accommodating it. Russell (1923) accepted the exclusion and concluded that logic applies only to an ideal language; Black (1937) introduced consistency profiles, the ancestor of degree theories; Waismann's open texture and Wittgenstein's blurred-edged concepts made the phenomenon respectable while leaving its logic untouched. The modern field dates from 1975, when Dummett, Fine, and Wright published together: Dummett argued that observational predicates are governed by inconsistent rules; Wright isolated tolerance — a small enough change never affects the predicate's application — as the sorites premise; Fine built supervaluationism; Kamp (1981) built the first contextual semantics. Williamson's Vagueness (1994) made epistemicism, hitherto the position of Cargile, Campbell, and Sorensen, the view every other had to answer, and Keefe's Theories of Vagueness (2000) is the standard survey and the standard defense of supervaluationism.

logic.paradox.vagueness sets out the sorites and the rival lodgings for the blame; what follows is the state of each.

Epistemicism holds that vague predicates have sharp boundaries fixed by the total pattern of use, that classical logic and bivalence hold without exception, and that our ignorance of the boundaries is explained by the margin-for-error principle: knowledge requires that one could not easily have been wrong, and beliefs about a boundary, in cases barely distinguishable from it, could easily have been. Its best argument is that it alone revises neither logic nor the disquotational schema, that every rival posits sharp facts somewhere — about admissible sharpenings, degrees, or contexts — and that the ignorance it posits is of a kind we recognize in inexact knowledge generally. Its standing objection is that use cannot determine boundaries to the grain and the theory has never supplied the mechanism; Williamson replies that no theory of meaning has supplied a mechanism for anything.

Supervaluationism holds that a vague predicate admits many admissible sharpenings, that a sentence is true if true on all of them and false if false on all, and that borderline attributions are neither. It preserves every classical theorem while abandoning bivalence — its best argument, since it explains why "either he is bald or he is not" sounds right when neither disjunct does, and why penumbral connections hold. Its costs are that "some single grain makes the difference" comes out supertrue with no true instance; that its global consequence relation fails conditional proof, contraposition, and reductio in the presence of a "definitely" operator, as Williamson showed; and higher-order vagueness: "admissible sharpening" is as vague as "heap," and Fara (2003) showed that not every plausible gap principle for iterated "definitely" can be accepted without a sorites of its own. Whether higher-order vagueness is a hierarchy without end, collapses after a few iterations, or is, as Bobzien argues, a radical unclarity best met by agnosticism, is the child's first stubbed child.

Degree theories, from Goguen and Machina to Edgington's verities (1996) and Smith's Vagueness and Degrees of Truth (2008), take the tolerance premise to be very nearly true and the sorites to be a long chain of almost-valid steps that bleed truth. Their best argument is phenomenological, and Smith's closeness principle — a small change makes only a small change in truth value — gives it a principled rationale. The objection that a value of exactly 0.5 is sharper than anything the theory was built to deny is met by making the degrees themselves vague, or by treating verities as probability-like.

Contextualism — Kamp, Raffman (1994, 1996), Soames (1999), Fara's interest-relative theory (2000), Shapiro's Vagueness in Context (2006) — holds that the boundary is real but moves: items judged together must be judged alike, so the boundary is never where attention is. Its best argument is that it fits what subjects do; the hysteresis effects documented by Égré and colleagues (2013) are its strongest evidence. Its objections are that the function from context to boundary looks soritical in turn, and that it explains why we cannot see the boundary without explaining why there is one. Raffman's Unruly Words (2014) has since moved to a multiple-range theory on which a vague predicate has several admissible ranges of application, none privileged.

Nihilism, Unger's and Wheeler's (1979), takes the sorites as sound: nothing satisfies a vague predicate. Braun and Sider's "Vague, So Untrue" (2007) is the semantic version — vague sentences are untrue but assertible because approximately true. Its best argument is that it alone accepts every premise and every rule. Incoherentism — Dummett's original view, Eklund's "Inconsistent Languages" (2002) — holds that tolerance is meaning-constitutive and inconsistent with the predicate's having any application, so that competent use is the management of an inconsistent rule; it explains why the sorites premises feel compulsory: they are. Glut-theoretic and subvaluational treatments (Hyde 1997; Weber 2010) accept that borderline items are both F and not F, and the finding of Alxatib and Pelletier (2011) that subjects readily assent to "tall and not tall" of a borderline case is claimed by them and by contextualists alike. The strict-tolerant logic ST was built for vagueness before truth: it validates tolerance and blocks the sorites by failing cut, so that no chain of tolerant steps composes — the point at which the theory of vagueness and the theory of Curry converge on one structural diagnosis. Bacon's Vagueness and Thought (2018) opens a further front, treating vagueness as a property of propositions and attitudes rather than of words, within a classical and bivalent theory.

The phenomenal sorites — nontransitive indiscriminability, disputed among Wright, Fara, and Raffman — is the child's second stubbed child; whether vagueness can be in the world is homed at metaphysics.material-objects, where Evans's argument against vague identity, the Lewis–Sider argument for unrestricted composition, and Barnes and Williams's metaphysical indeterminacy are adjudicated.

The set-theoretic paradoxes, indefinite extensibility, and absolute generality

The set-theoretic paradoxes are the family's most consequential members, since they did not merely puzzle but remade a discipline. logic.paradox.set-paradoxes exhibits Russell, Burali-Forti, and Cantor as one family — a totality and an operation that manufactures a member the totality cannot contain — and sets out the two diagnoses, limitation of size and the iterative conception, together with Dummett's deeper one.

Three responses competed in the decade after 1903. Russell's ramified theory of types stratified both objects and the definitions of properties, and needed the axiom of reducibility to recover analysis — a device Ramsey's simplification removed once the semantic paradoxes had been sent to linguistics. Zermelo's axiomatization (1908) replaced comprehension by separation — a condition carves a set from an existing set but creates none from nothing — without a diagnosis of why the axioms were right. Von Neumann's axiomatization (1925) introduced the set–class distinction and made limitation of size an axiom: a class is a set just in case it cannot be mapped onto the universe. The cumulative hierarchy was described by Zermelo in 1930, given philosophical voice by Gödel in 1947, and defended in Boolos's 1971 paper and "Iteration Again" (1989); Parsons (1977) and Hallett (1984) are the standard critical treatments. The child records Boolos's conclusion that neither heuristic yields all the axioms and that set theory rests on two ideas, neither reducible to the other.

Two alternatives matter here. Quine's New Foundations (1937), which blocks the paradoxes by stratifying comprehension rather than by stages and admits a universal set, shows that limitation of size is not forced; its consistency, long open, was claimed by Holmes and machine-verified in 2024, though mathematicians still decline to work in it because its universe has no natural picture. Aczel's non-well-founded set theory (1988) replaces foundation by an anti-foundation axiom on which every graph, cyclic ones included, pictures a set; consistent relative to ZFC, it supplied the semantics of Barwise and Etchemendy's The Liar (1987), and is the point at which the set-theoretic and semantic halves of the family formally touch.

Dummett's diagnosis, indefinite extensibility, has generated the region's liveliest current debate. The concepts set, ordinal, and cardinal are such that any definite totality of their instances yields a further instance outside it; the paradoxes are the signature of concepts that cannot be totalized; the casualty is unrestricted quantification over them. Williamson's "Everything" (2003) replied that absolutism is a commitment even its deniers make, since "every domain can be extended" quantifies over all domains; that the alternative is a hierarchy of quantifiers with all the ghostliness of the truth hierarchy; and that absolute generality, expressed with plural or higher-order resources, meets the paradoxes because the totality of everything is not a set and need not be. The relativist reply, in Rayo and Uzquiano's Absolute Generality (2006) and since, takes three forms. Glanzberg's contextualism makes quantifier domains context-dependent as the liar's contextualists make "true" context-dependent. Parsons's and Lavine's schematic relativism reads the relativist's generalizations as schematic — true of each domain without quantifying over all. Linnebo's and Studd's potentialism, now the center of the field and descended from Fine's modal relativism (2006), reads the hierarchy modally: the sets there could be are never all there are, the iterative conception's stage-talk is cashed by a modal logic on which every stage is followed by a possible further one, and unrestricted quantification gives way to a modalized quantifier over whatever there could be. Linnebo (2013, 2018), Studd (2019), and Florio and Linnebo (2021) are the reference works; Linnebo and Shapiro's distinction between liberal and strict potentialism (2019) marks the internal divide over whether the modalized quantifiers obey classical logic. The absolutist's standing objection is that the potentialist's modal operators are either primitive and unexplained or explained by quantification over all the possibilities, which reintroduces the absolute domain one level up — once more, each side's best case is the other's self-undermining.

Yablo's paradox and the anatomy of reference

Yablo's sequence — infinitely many sentences, each saying that every later one is untrue — appeared in 1985 and was named "paradox without self-reference" in 1993; Visser's 1989 survey had already built a non-self-referential infinite liar. logic.paradox.yablo sets out the three positions: Priest's charge (1997), seconded by Beall (2001), that circularity is relocated into the fixed-point schema by which the list is specified; Sorensen's (1998) and Yablo's reply that the sentences carry the paradox whether or not we specify them uniformly; and the ω-inconsistency finding (Ketland 2005; Barrio 2010) that the sequence, formalized with local disquotation, is consistent but has no standard model, so that paradox appears only when the infinite totality is taken at face value.

Two lines of work have grown around the experiment. Cook (2014) generalizes unwinding: any paradox of the liar type can be converted into a Yablo-style sequence with the same paradoxical status, so that circularity is dispensable across the board; Schlenker (2007) had shown the same for Curry. What they share is non-well-foundedness of reference. The graph-theoretic program — Cook; Rabern, Rabern, and Macauley (2013); Beringer and Schindler (2017) — treats reference among sentences as a directed graph and asks which graphs can support paradox; its basic finding is that every dangerous graph contains a cycle or an infinite descending path, so a well-founded reference structure is safe — Kripke's groundedness result in graph-theoretic dress. The converse fails — the truth-teller's graph is a cycle and the truth-teller is not paradoxical — but the finding vindicates, at the level of structure, the ungroundedness diagnosis of Yablo (1982) and Leitgeb (2005). From this vantage the dispute over whether Yablo's paradox is "really" self-referential looks less like a dispute about the sequence than about which of two concepts — circularity or ungroundedness — the word "self-reference" was tracking all along.

The unity question

Is the family one? Four answers are on the table.

Ramsey's partition denies it: the logical and the semantic paradoxes involve different notions and require different cures. Its best argument is the record — ZFC and Tarskian semantics each dispatched their half with no help from the other — and Curry, which fits neither half, has been treated on its own. Its weakness is that the partition was drawn by inspection, not proof, and that the diagonal lemma, which Ramsey did not have, runs identically through both halves.

The diagonal answer, the mathematician's, finds the unity in Cantor's argument. Lawvere's theorem of 1969, popularized by Yanofsky (2003), derives Cantor's theorem, Russell's paradox, Tarski's indefinability, Gödel's incompleteness, and the fixed-point theorems of computability from one lemma about surjections: paradoxes and limitative theorems are one argument with different bridge principles. Its best argument is that it is a theorem. Its limitation is that it explains the paradoxes' form without saying what to do about them, and that the sorites is not among its instances.

Priest's inclosure schema — a totality, an operation that carries any subset of it to an object outside that subset yet inside the totality, contradiction at the limit — was proposed in 1994, developed in Beyond the Limits of Thought, and extended to the sorites in 2010. Its best argument is the principle of uniform solution: structurally identical paradoxes deserve one cure, the set-theoretic and semantic paradoxes share the schema, so the century's two cures are ad hoc and only dialetheism, which accepts the contradiction at the limit, treats them uniformly. The criticisms are three: Badici (2008) and Zhong (2012) argue that the fit is loose — Curry, containing no negation, is no inclosure at all; Beall's "End of Inclosure" (2014) argues that the schema, run with the dialetheist's own tools, yields inclosure paradoxes dialetheism cannot solve; and "same form" underdetermines "same cure."

The ungroundedness answer, descending from Kripke, Yablo, and Leitgeb and now supported by the graph-theoretic results, finds the unity in reference structure: the semantic paradoxes are cases of a dependence relation that fails to be well-founded, and the set-theoretic paradoxes, on the iterative picture, are cases of membership that would. Its best argument is that it explains the truth-teller and Yablo's sequence, which the inclosure schema handles awkwardly, and that its prediction — well-founded structures are safe — is a theorem. Its limitation is the sorites, whose premises refer to nothing and whose pathology is tolerance, not dependence. Here the substructural theorists make the last unity claim: the sorites, Curry, and the liar all fail at a structural rule — cut for ST, contraction for Zardini — and the paradoxes are one phenomenon because consequence is one thing.

Unity claims have thus multiplied while Ramsey's partition has quietly lost its authority: no one now thinks the semantic paradoxes belong to linguistics, and the axiomatic theories of truth are arithmetic. Whether the paradoxes deserve a uniform solution remains open, and so does the prior question of what a solution owes — consistency, expressive completeness, mathematical strength, fidelity to speakers — since the theories differ on the currency as much as on the price.

Assessment

Editorial. The most defensible reading of the last half-century is that the paradoxes are not problems awaiting solution but measuring instruments: each theory of truth is a record of what its author was willing to pay, and revenge is the observation that the price is never zero. On that reading the substructural turn is the field's most important recent development — not because non-transitive or non-contractive logics are correct, but because they have shown that "classical logic" was two things, operational rules and structural rules, and that the paradoxes had been charged to the wrong account. Whether the metainferential hierarchy is ST's revenge or its vindication will decide whether that turn changed the subject or only its address.
