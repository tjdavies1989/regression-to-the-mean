What Is Philosophy?

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.14

A chemist who cannot define chemistry has a public-relations problem; a philosopher who cannot say what philosophy is has a philosophical problem, and every available answer is itself a contested philosophical position. The question is not idle self-regard: each self-conception licenses some methods and delegitimizes others, so what hangs on the answer is nothing less than what counts as doing the work well.

The rival conceptions divide roughly as follows. Quinean naturalism holds that philosophy is continuous with science — there is no first philosophy, no tribunal outside the web of belief; philosophy is the abstract, reflective end of the same inquiry. Its strength is economy: it needs no special faculty of a priori insight and explains why philosophy of physics or biology is done best by those who know the science. Conceptual analysis, given its most explicit modern defense by Frank Jackson, replies that even the naturalist needs an account of what her terms mean before empirical findings can bear on philosophical claims; analysis of our folk concepts is the unavoidable first step of "serious metaphysics," and it vindicates the armchair. Phenomenological description locates philosophy's subject matter in the structures of lived experience — a domain every science presupposes (someone observes, measures, judges) but none can describe from within its third-person stance. Wittgensteinian therapy denies that philosophy has a subject matter at all: its problems are bewitchments of language, and its proper product is not theory but dissolution; its strength is the track record of "deep problems" that evaporated under grammatical scrutiny. Sellars's synoptic conception — philosophy studies "how things in the broadest possible sense of the term hang together in the broadest possible sense of the term" — explains what remains for philosophy after the sciences have divided the world: the reconciliation of the manifest image of persons and reasons with the scientific image of particles and causes. Newest is conceptual engineering (Cappelen; Haslanger's ameliorative projects), which converts a threat into a program: if experimental philosophy shows our intuitions parochial and our concepts defective, the task is not to describe the concepts we have but to build the ones we need — as Haslanger asks not what race and gender mean but what they should mean.

Two boundary disputes are themselves division-defining. First, philosophy's relation to its own history: whether reading Aristotle is philosophy or scholarship. One side treats past texts as quarries of arguments to be assessed in current terms; the other (Bernard Williams's "history of philosophy" against mere "history of ideas") holds that philosophy without history mistakes its own parochial present for the space of possibilities. Where a department stands on this predicts much of the analytic–continental divide. Second, demarcation: fields repeatedly mature out of philosophy — physics, psychology, formal logic, linguistics — which suggests either that philosophy is the incubator of sciences (its progress measured in successful secessions) or that it is what remains when the empirically tractable has left, a permanent residue of questions that outrun method. On the other border lie religion and wisdom literature: Hadot's argument that ancient philosophy was a way of life, not a doctrine, presses the question of whether the modern academic conception is the discovery of philosophy's essence or a contingent narrowing.

Bridges

The naturalism dispute bridges to epistemology (the a priori, intuitions as evidence) and to science (what makes a question empirically tractable). Conceptual analysis and engineering bridge to language (meaning, reference, semantic externalism, metalinguistic negotiation). The history question bridges to the traditions register, whose entries are its evidence; the phenomenological self-conception bridges there too.
