Moral Responsibility

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.10

A friend betrays a confidence, and you resent it; a stranger returns your dropped wallet, and you are grateful. These responses do not merely register that something bad or good happened — they address the person, as author of what she did, and they claim to be fitting: she deserves the resentment, he deserves the thanks. Moral responsibility studies that claim. By editorial decision the question is split: whether anyone could ever have done otherwise — determinism and the metaphysical apparatus — is homed at metaphysics.free-will. This node owns what remains once that is bracketed, which turns out to be most of the subject: what blame and praise are, what makes an agent an eligible target of them, and when they are deserved. Responsibility here is a normative status, not a causal fact.

The modern field is founded on Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962). Strawson inverted the traditional order: instead of settling the metaphysics and then licensing our practices, he began with the practices — the "reactive attitudes" of resentment, indignation, gratitude, guilt — and argued that they constitute participation in interpersonal life itself. Excuses are telling: we withdraw resentment not upon learning that an act was determined, but upon learning that it did not mean what it seemed ("she didn't know," "he was pushed") or that the agent is not a full participant (childhood, psychosis). The practice answers to the quality of will an act expresses, and needs no external metaphysical warrant. Nearly every position since develops or resists this program.

Quality-of-will theorists take the hint literally: blameworthiness is the ill will or objectionable indifference an action manifests. Arpaly's version cuts against intellectualism — Huckleberry Finn, who helps Jim while sincerely judging he ought not, is praiseworthy, because his act responds to the moral reasons whatever his avowed principles say. Frankfurt's hierarchy locates responsibility in identification: an agent is responsible for the will she endorses at a higher order — hence the unwilling addict is his desire's victim while the willing addict owns it — though the regress question (what makes the higher order authoritative?) remains its standing tax. Watson's distinction organizes the field: attributability (the act expresses her ends, is hers to be judged by) is one thing, accountability (she may be sanctioned, called to answer) another — a wrongdoer with a brutalizing history may be fully attributable yet doubtfully accountable. McKenna's conversational model develops accountability's structure: blame is a move in a moral conversation, a demand for acknowledgment that the wrongdoer can answer, deflect, or repair.

If blame is something we do to people, it has its own ethics. The hypocrite's blame misfires though its target is guilty; blame can be true but not the blamer's business; proportionality constrains it as it constrains punishment — standing is now a literature in itself. Beneath the practice lies the justificatory question: is blame's warrant desert, backward-looking and basic, or its forward-looking effects — protection, formation, repaired relationships? Pereboom's responsibility skepticism denies basic desert altogether and then, instructively, rebuilds: moral protest, quarantine-model prevention, love, and meaning survive, he argues, without retribution. Whether what survives is recognizably our practice, or a successor to it, is where the debate now stands.
