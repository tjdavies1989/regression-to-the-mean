Expressivism

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-09-09.18

Expressivism holds that a moral judgment expresses a conative state — disapproval, a prescription, acceptance of a norm, a plan — rather than a belief, and that the declarative grammar of moral sentences disguises this. The parent set the view against its rivals; this entry follows its internal history, which is the story of one debt paid in successive currencies and then a worry that it has been paid too well.

Emotivism and prescriptivism. Ayer's version was a corollary of verificationism: "stealing is wrong" adds nothing verifiable to "stealing," so its moral surplus is an expression of feeling. Stevenson gave the view a theory of disagreement: moral terms carry emotive meaning, a standing power to influence attitudes, so that two parties agreed on every fact may still disagree in attitude — which is why moral argument continues where factual argument ends. Hare replaced feeling with prescription. A moral judgment is a universalizable imperative: to call an act wrong is to prescribe against it and against anything relevantly like it, and universalizability supplies a discipline emotivism lacked. All three rest on the same master argument, motivational internalism plus the Humean theory of motivation: sincere moral judgment moves the judge, beliefs alone move no one, so moral judgments are not beliefs.

The Frege–Geach problem. Geach observed that expression happens only when a sentence is asserted. In "if stealing is wrong, then getting your brother to steal is wrong," the antecedent expresses no disapproval, yet "stealing is wrong" must mean the same there as when asserted, else the modus ponens through it equivocates. Either moral sentences have a meaning independent of the attitudes they express — which is cognitivism — or moral inference is fallacious. The problem generalizes to negation, disjunction, and attitude reports, and demands an account of validity and inconsistency that does not help itself to truth.

Blackburn's logic of attitudes reads the conditional as a higher-order attitude — endorsement of the combination "disapproving of stealing while disapproving of getting others to steal" — so that one who disapproves of stealing but not of the abetting has a fractured sensibility. Critics (Schueler, Hale) reply that a fractured sensibility is a defect of character, not a contradiction: the wrong kind of wrongness. Gibbard's norm-expressivism builds a genuine semantics: a normative judgment expresses acceptance of a system of norms, and a sentence's content is the set of factual–normative worlds it rules out, so entailment and consistency fall out set-theoretically. His later plan-expressivism replaces norms with contingency plans: to judge that one ought to φ is to settle on φ-ing. Unwin's negation problem exposed the remaining gap: "stealing is not wrong," "not: stealing is wrong," and "not stealing is wrong" must express three different states, which a single attitude of disapproval cannot generate. Schroeder's being-for gives every moral state one shape — wrongness judgments are being for blaming — so that negation operates on content rather than attitude: to judge stealing not wrong is to be for not blaming for it. Schroeder's own audit: the construction works only if extended to all discourse, and grows implausible as it does.

Quasi-realism and creeping minimalism. Blackburn's program starts from projection and earns the realist idiom. Moral truth is available deflationarily; "cruelty would be wrong even if I approved of it" expresses a first-order attitude toward a counterfactual change in one's attitudes, not a metaphysical thesis; error and moral improvement become attitudes toward one's own sensibility. Dreier's creeping minimalism is the price of success: if deflationism about truth, facts, properties, and belief is right, the realist's talk of moral facts commits her to no more than the quasi-realist has earned, and the positions collapse into one. What could still separate them is disputed. Dreier and Simpson locate the difference in the order of explanation — the realist explains moral judgment by its representing moral facts, the expressivist by its psychological function without invoking such facts — while Gibbard takes it to be preserved in what the theorist says the state fundamentally is, and Köhler and Golub recast expressivism as a thesis in the psychology of judgment rather than in semantics.

Hybrid theories. Hybrids keep a belief for Frege–Geach and an attitude for internalism. Copp's realist-expressivism holds that moral assertions express beliefs while conventionally implicating attitudes; Ridge's ecumenical expressivism makes the belief's content depend on the attitude; Boisvert models the pairing on pejoratives, which describe and disparage at once. Schroeder's objection is that the belief does all the compositional work, leaving the attitude semantically idle, and that the pejorative model licenses only a narrow, cancellable hybrid.
