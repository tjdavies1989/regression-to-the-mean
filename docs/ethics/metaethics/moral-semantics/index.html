Moral Semantics

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-09-09.18

The parent treats descriptivism, expressivism, and their hybrids as metaphysical rivals. This entry treats them as competing entries in a single semantic theory: what value does "wrong" contribute to the sentences it occurs in, and what must that value do? The data any answer must fit are fixed and jointly awkward — moral sentences embed under every operator, disagreement survives agreement on the facts, sincere judgment reliably moves the judge, and "ought" is not even a distinctively moral word but a modal auxiliary that also ranks chess moves and umbrellas.

Descriptivist semantics. Descriptivists assign moral predicates properties as contents and divide over how the assignment is fixed. Cornell semantics borrows Putnam's causal regulation: "good" refers to whatever natural property regulates competent use, so the term's meaning is a matter of discovery, as "water" was. Jackson's analytic naturalism instead reads reference off the network of platitudes that competent speakers accept — the Canberra plan applied to morality — and holds that the mature folk theory's Ramsey sentence implicitly defines each moral term. Non-naturalists cannot use either, since no natural property is on offer, and so tend toward conceptual-role accounts: Wedgwood holds that the meaning of "ought" is given by its regulative role in practical reasoning, and that this role suffices to fix a reference that is nonetheless irreducible. The pressure point for all three is what makes moral concepts moral rather than merely coextensive with some property, and it is here that Moral Twin Earth becomes a semantic rather than a metaphysical datum: if speakers whose terms are regulated by different properties still count as disagreeing, then meaning is not fixed by regulation, and the descriptivist must build the disagreement-preserving element — Copp's normative role, Brink's shared conceptual role — into the semantics itself.

Expressivist semantics and its compositional programs. The expressivism entry follows the Frege–Geach tax through Blackburn, Gibbard, and Schroeder; what matters here is the methodological shift those programs mark. Gibbard's factual–normative worlds and Schroeder's being-for both abandon the idea that expressivism is a thesis about what sentences do not have (truth conditions) in favor of assigning every sentence a semantic value that composes. Yalcin generalizes this into a redescription of the whole descriptivist/non-descriptivist divide: the question is not whether moral sentences have contents but what role their contents play in a theory of communication — whether accepting one updates an agent's picture of the world or her plans. On that recasting, expressivism becomes a species of dynamic semantics, and Charlow, Starr, and Willer build it as such, modeling deontic and imperative meaning as operations on a state that pairs information with preferences or plans. The gain is a compositional semantics that owes nothing to Blackburn's psychology; the cost is that the resulting theory looks like ordinary formal semantics with an unusual index, prompting the creeping-minimalism worry in a new key.

Hybrids. Schroeder sorts hybrids by where the attitude enters: as part of the asserted content (Ridge's ecumenical expressivism, later relativized to a speaker's standard), as a conventional implicature (Copp), or as presupposition or generalized implicature (Strandberg, Finlay's later view). The classification exposes the trade: the more semantic the attitude, the more it must survive embedding — and it demonstrably does not survive "if stealing is wrong" — while the more pragmatic it is, the less it explains internalism, which was the point.

Relativist and contextualist semantics. Harman's "inner judgments" relativize "ought" to an agreement between speaker, agent, and audience, so that the judgment that Hitler ought not to have acted as he did is false if no shared motivation exists — relativism as a claim about logical form. Dreier's speaker relativism and Björnsson and Finlay's contextualism instead treat moral terms as indexed to contextually supplied standards, explaining disagreement pragmatically. MacFarlane's assessment relativism, developed for taste and epistemic modals, transfers to "ought" the retraction datum contextualism handles worst. The deepest version of the strategy comes from the semantics of modals: on Kratzer's account "ought" quantifies over worlds ranked by a contextually supplied ordering source, so moral "ought" is already contextualist unless something fixes the ordering source, and Finlay's end-relational theory claims nothing does.

Inferentialism. Chrisman's inferentialist expressivism identifies the meaning of "ought" with its role in inference — what licenses it, what it licenses — and holds that this dissolves the descriptivism question, since inferential role is neither a represented property nor an expressed attitude. Whether it inherits Brandom's difficulties about incompatibility and objectivity is its open question.
