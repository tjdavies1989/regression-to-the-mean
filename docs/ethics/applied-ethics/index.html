Applied Ethics

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.10

A physician asks whether to withdraw the ventilator; a regulator asks whether an algorithm whose error rates differ by race may sentence anyone; a commander asks whom he may bomb; a diner asks what died for lunch. None of them can wait for metaethics to settle. Applied ethics is where the division meets such questions on their own schedule — and its first discovery is that "apply" is the wrong verb. How ethics reaches cases is itself a contested problem, prior to every field below.

The naive picture is the theory-application model: identify the true normative theory, deduce the verdict. Its honesty is its strength — if consequentialism is true, it settles triage. But the theories disagree, command least confidence exactly where cases are hardest, and practitioners who agree on a verdict rarely share a theory. Principlism (Beauchamp and Childress) responds with mid-level principles — respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice — specified and balanced case by case; it became the working method of institutional bioethics precisely because it lets rival theorists cooperate, at the cost of offering no procedure when principles collide. Casuistry (Jonsen and Toulmin) drops principles for paradigms: reason analogically from settled cases outward, as common law does. Its strength is fidelity to how moral knowledge actually behaves; its weakness is that cases do not classify themselves, and paradigms encode the prejudices of whoever settled them. Traffic also runs upward: Foot's trolley cases were built for abortion and double effect before they became normative theory's laboratory. The fields are not clients of theory but test-beds for it.

The fields themselves have grown genuine literatures — journals, canons, internal debates — and this node is accordingly a hub. Bioethics, the oldest and most institutionalized, owns informed consent and its limits, human enhancement, end-of-life decisions, research ethics after Tuskegee, and the allocation of scarce care. AI ethics inherits bioethics' ambitions at speed: algorithmic fairness (where impossibility results show intuitive fairness metrics mutually unsatisfiable), opacity and the right to explanation, value alignment, and the question of machine moral status — argued jointly with mind.artificial-minds. Environmental ethics asks whether nature matters only for us: anthropocentrism against biocentric and ecocentric rivals, wilderness and restoration, and climate change as the field's forcing case — an essentially intergenerational, collective-action problem no traditional theory was built for. Animal ethics runs the normative triad on nonhuman subjects: Singer's equal consideration of interests, Regan's rights for subjects-of-a-life, Korsgaard's Kantian argument that animals are ends in themselves. The ethics of war holds just war theory's two tribunals — resort and conduct — with Walzer's conventionalism defending the moral equality of combatants and McMahan's revisionism denying that fighting for an unjust cause can be fought justly. Effective altruism is the newest arrival and the strangest: a movement rather than a field, applying maximizing beneficence to cause selection — global health, animal welfare, existential risk — and drawing critiques (demandingness, institutional blindness, the hubris of expected-value reasoning about the far future) that have themselves become philosophy.
