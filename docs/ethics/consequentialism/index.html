Consequentialism

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.10

Suppose you could make the world better — fewer deaths, less misery, more of whatever genuinely matters — and you refuse. What could justify the refusal? Consequentialism is the theory that nothing could: an act is right if and only if it produces the best available outcome. The idea has a disarming simplicity. Morality is not a list of taboos or a report of our sentiments; it is the project of making things go well, and anyone who cares about anything already grants that outcomes matter. The whole debate is over whether outcomes are all that matters.

The profoundest version is classical utilitarianism: the best outcome is the greatest balance of happiness over suffering, each person's welfare counting equally. Bentham gave it its radical edge — the felicific calculus dissolved rank, custom, and eventually species as moral boundaries. Mill argued that it could honor liberty, character, and the "higher pleasures" without abandoning the hedonist core. Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics remains the deepest statement: utilitarianism as what impartial practical reason demands, shadowed by his "dualism" — egoism seems equally rational, and reason alone may not adjudicate.

The objections deserve their full force. The theory is crushingly demanding: if every dollar and hour must go where it does most good, ordinary lives are continuous wrongdoing. Williams pressed the integrity objection: an agent required to abandon her ground projects whenever the impersonal sums shift is not a moral agent but a conduit for the universal calculus — utilitarianism attacks the very thing that makes her a person with reasons. Rawls charged that aggregation ignores the separateness of persons: it treats trade-offs across lives as if they were trade-offs within one life, where no one exists for whom the greater good is good. And Taurek asked whether the numbers should count at all — if I can save one stranger or five, is the five-fold claim really weightier, or are there only individuals, each with everything to lose?

A refinement industry answers. Rule consequentialism (Hooker its best contemporary defender) evaluates codes rather than acts: right action is conformity to the rules whose general internalization would be best — capturing constraints and permissions, at the risk of rule-worship. Railton's sophisticated consequentialism distinguishes criterion from decision procedure: the best agent by consequentialist lights may be one who loves particular people non-instrumentally and rarely calculates. Satisficing versions require only good enough; scalar versions drop "right" and "wrong" altogether for better and worse. Each concession purchases plausibility at the price of the original simplicity.

Then there is the consequentializing maneuver: any purported counterexample can be absorbed by enriching the theory of value — build agent-relativity or the badness of killings-by-me into the outcomes, and every plausible ethic has a consequentialist representation. Whether this shows consequentialism's truth or its vacuity — a theory unfalsifiable by absorption — is a live structural question. Meanwhile the theory's native instrument, expected-value reasoning under uncertainty, has quietly conquered practical ethics: risk, policy, effective altruism, and the moral weight of future generations are all argued in its idiom, even by its official opponents.
