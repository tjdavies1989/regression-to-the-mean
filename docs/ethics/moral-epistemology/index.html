Moral Epistemology

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.10

Everyone knows that gratuitous cruelty is wrong — more securely, probably, than they know most of what they learned in school. Yet nothing in the standard inventory of knowledge-sources obviously delivers this. No observation shows wrongness; no experiment tests it; moral facts, if there are any, do not reflect light or leave traces. Moral epistemology asks how, then, moral claims are known — and whether the striking confidence of our moral convictions can survive an honest account of where they came from.

The working answer of most moral philosophy is intuition: considered judgments about cases (do not push the man off the footbridge) are treated as evidence, the data against which theories are tested. Sidgwick tried to state conditions under which such seemings carry weight; the contemporary field mostly practices intuition-mongering while arguing about its license. The practice is disciplined by reflective equilibrium (Rawls, widened by Daniels to include background theories): adjust judgments and principles against one another until they cohere, and the surviving system is justified. Its strength is that it is arguably the only method anyone actually uses, and the only one that does not beg the question by privileging some inputs a priori. Its standing objection is garbage in, garbage out: coherence launders whatever it starts with, and the reflective equilibrium of a slaveholding society ratifies slavery. A bolder alternative, moral perception (Audi, McGrath), holds that we can literally see the wrongness in an act of cruelty — phenomenologically apt, but owing an account of how perception reaches facts with no causal powers.

The origins worry has hardened into the debunking arguments. Street's Darwinian dilemma: evolution shaped our evaluative dispositions for fitness, not truth, so the realist must either concede that selective pressure tracked mind-independent moral facts (scientifically incredible) or admit that our matching them would be a cosmic coincidence. Joyce runs the genealogy toward error theory; Greene's neurally-based version targets deontological intuitions specifically as emotional heritage. The replies define the current front. Third-factor accounts (Enoch, Wielenberg) interpose a link: survival-conduciveness correlates with goodness, so faculties selected to track the former are thereby reliable about the latter — vindicating reliability without magical tracking, at the price of assuming a moral premise the debunker will call question-begging. Others go companions-in-guilt: the debunker's own epistemic norms have the same genealogy, so the argument proves too much or nothing.

Two social puzzles round out the field. Moral testimony: deferring to a chemist about valences is epistemic hygiene, but deferring to a neighbor about whether eating meat is wrong seems defective even if the neighbor is reliable. Pessimists (Hills, McGrath) diagnose the defect — moral worth or moral understanding requires grasping the reasons oneself, not borrowing conclusions; optimists (Sliwa) answer that knowledge is knowledge, and that scrupulous people rightly defer where their own judgment is compromised. The dispute opens onto moral expertise and its Platonic identification problem — picking out a moral expert seems to require the very expertise sought. And moral disagreement gives the peer-disagreement debate its hardest instance: on abortion or capital punishment, people as informed and careful as you have reached the opposite verdict, and conciliationism applied consistently threatens wholesale moral skepticism. The machinery lives at epistemology.disagreement; the damage is assessed here.
