Moral Psychology

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.10

Normative theory tells agents what to do; moral psychology asks what kind of creature is being addressed. Its founding quarrel is the oldest in ethics — Hume's claim that reason is the slave of the passions against the rationalist insistence that moral judgment is reason's own work — and its distinctive modern feature is that the quarrel is now conducted partly with experiments. The field sits at the seam between philosophy and empirical psychology, and its recurring methodological question is what, if anything, data about how we in fact judge and feel can show about how we ought to.

The seam shows first in moral motivation. Motivational internalists hold that sincerely judging an act wrong necessarily carries some motivation to avoid it; externalists deny the necessity, treating the connection as contingent on desires the agent happens to have. The stakes are metaethical: internalism plus the Humean theory of motivation — beliefs alone never motivate; only belief–desire pairs do — seems to entail that moral judgments are not pure beliefs, handing expressivism its master argument. Michael Smith's "moral problem" states the trilemma exactly: moral judgments look objective (cognitivism), look practical (internalism), and motivation looks Humean — yet the three appear jointly inconsistent, and every metaethical position can be read as a choice of which to abandon. The amoralist, who judges without caring, is the contested figure; psychopathy is the field's attempt to settle by observation whether he exists. Psychopaths use moral language fluently while remaining unmoved, which looks like externalism's witness — unless, as internalists reply citing their weak performance on the moral/conventional distinction, they never make genuine moral judgments at all.

On moral judgment itself, the empirical turn has two landmarks. Haidt's social intuitionism holds that judgment is driven by fast affective intuition, with reasoning mostly post-hoc advocacy — supported by "moral dumbfounding," where subjects cling to verdicts after every reason is stripped away. Greene's dual-process account maps the divide onto trolley cases: characteristically deontological verdicts (don't push the man off the footbridge) correlate with fast emotional processing, characteristically consequentialist ones with controlled cognition — and Greene argues the emotional responses are unreliable heritage, debunking deontology. Berker's reply is the field's standard caution: the neural and psychological findings do no normative work by themselves; any debunking conclusion is carried entirely by a philosophical premise (that factors like personal force are morally irrelevant) that must be argued the old way.

The moral emotions carry their own literatures: guilt as the self-directed face of accountability, shame as a global self-assessment with a disputed moral status, and empathy as the sentimentalist tradition's favored engine of concern — now under attack from Bloom, who argues that empathy is biased, innumerate, and inflammable, and that diffuse rational compassion serves morality better. Finally, the field's oldest empirical question — whether genuine altruism exists or all motivation is covertly egoistic — has moved from armchair regress arguments to Batson's experimental program, whose manipulations of easy escape and mood-fixing are widely read as disconfirming egoistic reinterpretations of empathic helping, though the hedonist always has one more epicycle.

What is strongest in each camp is complementary: the sentimentalists own most of the data; the rationalists own the normative question the data keep failing to answer.
