Semantic Responses

◔ sketch  ·  satellite  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-09-09.17

Other responses to the Cartesian argument grant the skeptic her hypothesis and dispute what follows. Putnam's, in the first chapter of Reason, Truth and History (1981), disputes whether the hypothesis can be entertained by the person it is about. A word refers to a kind of thing only if its users stand in some causal connection to that kind: an ant's accidental trace of Churchill's profile does not depict Churchill, and a lifelong envatted brain, whose every experience a computer supplies, has never been connected to vats — only to the images and program-states that produce vat-experience. Its word "vat" therefore refers to vats-in-the-image, or to whatever electronic features generate them. So when the brain thinks "I am a brain in a vat," it says something false: it is not a brain-in-a-vat-in-the-image. And if it is not envatted, the sentence is false in plain English. Like "I do not exist," the sentence is false whoever thinks it — and a hypothesis that cannot be true of its own victim, Putnam concludes, is not a possibility at all.

Brueckner (1986) laid the argument out as a disjunction. Either I am a brain in a vat or I am not. If I am, my sentence "I am a brain in a vat" is true only if I am a brain-in-a-vat-in-the-image, which I am not; if I am not, the sentence is false in English. Either way the sentence is false. What that establishes, Brueckner observed, is a fact about a sentence, and the skeptic's question was about me. To pass from "my sentence 'I am a BIV' is false" to "I am not a BIV" I need the disquotational premise that my sentence is true just in case I am a BIV — and I know that only if I know I am speaking English rather than vat-English, which is to know already that I am not envatted. Putnam had proved something weaker than he claimed, or assumed what he set out to show. Warfield (1998) and Wright (1992) answered by routing the argument through self-knowledge: I know from the armchair that I am thinking that water is wet, and I know from the externalist theory of content that no brain in a vat could think that, so I know I am not one. Whether that reasoning is licit is the compatibility problem of externalism and privileged access, worked out at mind.self; McKinsey's charge that it delivers a priori knowledge of the world by stipulation is aimed at exactly this inference.

The loophole everyone concedes is recent envatment. Putnam's brain must have been envatted always, in a universe of vats and automatic machinery with no scientists in it, since a brain envatted last night keeps last week's semantics, and its "vat" refers to vats. Nor does it reach a vat maintained by scientists, whose contact with vats may enter the brain's causal history through the program they wrote. Nagel put the general version: perhaps I cannot truly say "I am a brain in a vat," but I may be in a predicament I have no words to describe, and the argument proves only that I cannot describe it. Chalmers's reinterpretation, in "The Matrix as Metaphysics," turns the loophole into a doctrine: if the hypothesis is true, it is a hypothesis about what underlies physics, not a skeptical one — my beliefs about tables come out largely true, since "table" refers to whatever computational structure has been playing table. Bouwsma's evil genius of 1949, who leaves no trace and so deceives no one, was the same thought.

The standing verdict is that the argument, where it works, refutes a formulation. The skeptic wanted "you cannot rule out that your experience is not caused as you take it to be," and she can restate that with any hypothesis that keeps ordinary reference — dreaming, the demon, last night's envatment — or with Nagel's indescribable one. Brueckner named a second cost: if I cannot tell whether I speak English or vat-English, I do not know what my words mean, and a semantic skepticism has replaced the metaphysical one. What survives is narrower. The argument shows that semantic externalism, if true, closes off the skeptic's favorite picture of a mind whose contents are fixed independently of the world it doubts; Putnam himself wanted it as a lemma against metaphysical realism, the position on which a total, undetectable error is coherent, and Button (2013) has argued that this, not the reply to Descartes, is where the argument does its real work.
