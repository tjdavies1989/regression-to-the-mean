Closure and Its Deniers

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-09-09.17

Stated naively — if you know p and p entails q, you know q — closure is false, and trivially: you may never have considered q, or may not know that the entailment holds. The principle worth defending is the one Hawthorne formulates: if you know p, competently deduce q from it, and come to believe q on that basis while retaining your knowledge of p, then you know q. Williamson puts the same thought as a truism about method: deduction is a way of extending knowledge, and any theory on which it is not is a theory of something other than knowledge. So formulated, closure looks less like a premise the skeptic smuggled in than a constraint any account of knowledge must respect. The denial has nonetheless been defended by serious people, for one reason — it dissolves the skeptical argument without dissolving anything else.

Dretske's zebras made the case first. You see striped equines in the zoo enclosure and know they are zebras; you do not know they are not mules cleverly painted, since nothing you can see distinguishes the two. "Knows," Dretske argued, is not a fully penetrating operator: like "explains why" and "is surprised that," it fails to pass through every known entailment. His positive account — knowledge requires conclusive reasons, reasons that would not obtain unless the belief were true — delivers exactly this pattern, as does the relevant-alternatives idea that knowing requires ruling out only the alternatives that are relevant, and painted mules are not. Stine noticed at once that relevant alternatives could keep closure by contextualizing relevance instead, so that the alternatives one must exclude shift with the conversation: contextualism's seed.

Nozick generalized the maneuver. Knowledge is belief that tracks truth: you would not believe p if it were false (sensitivity), and would believe it if true (adherence), the conditionals relativized to the method of belief. Your belief that you have hands is sensitive — in the nearest handless worlds you notice. Your belief that you are not a brain in a vat is not: in the nearest vat worlds you believe exactly what you believe now. Closure fails, and Nozick treats the failure as skepticism's grain of truth: the skeptic is right that you do not know you are unenvatted, wrong that this matters for hands.

DeRose named the cost. "I know I have hands, but I do not know I am not a handless brain in a vat" is an abominable conjunction — an assertion no theory should license. Kripke's red barn sharpened it: in a county whose fake barns are never painted red, sensitivity has you knowing there is a red barn before you while not knowing there is a barn. DeRose's own proposal absorbs sensitivity into contextualism: mentioning an insensitive proposition raises the standard for "know," so the skeptic's conclusion holds within her context, closure holds within every context, and the conjunction is never assertable in one.

Safety is the closure-friendly modal condition, and the mainstream successor to tracking. Sosa, Williamson, and Pritchard require not that you would disbelieve p were it false, but that your belief could not easily have been false — in nearby worlds where you believe p by the same method, p. Since no vat world is nearby, "I am not a brain in a vat" comes out safe, and the Moorean verdict follows with closure intact. Safety pays elsewhere: it has no account of why the skeptic's argument grips, and whether safety itself is closed under competent deduction has been questioned (Murphy, Alspector-Kelly), so it may only relocate the problem.

Whether denying closure is a cure worse than the disease is the standing question. Feldman called the denial among the least plausible ideas ever to come down the philosophical pike; Hawthorne holds that no counterexample could be more secure than the principle. Dretske answered that every theory pays, and the closure-keeper's bill — either skepticism or an unexplained knowledge of heavyweight implications — is no smaller. Two complications keep the debate from settling. Wright's distinction between closure and transmission lets knowledge close while warrant fails to transmit across the inference, a diplomatic settlement several parties accept. And multi-premise closure faces Hawthorne's lottery: you know you will not afford a safari this year, which entails your ticket will lose, which you do not know — with Lasonen-Aarnio arguing that risk accumulates across premises until closure must fail for long deductions even if it holds for short ones.
