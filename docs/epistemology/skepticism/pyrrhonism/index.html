Pyrrhonian Skepticism

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-09-09.17

Agrippa's Five Modes, as Sextus reports them, are questions for anyone who claims a ground. Philosophers disagree about it, with no agreed umpire. It appears so only relative to a perceiver and a condition. Her ground is another ground, and so on without end (regress), or simply posited (hypothesis), or supported by what it was meant to support (reciprocity). Barnes's The Toils of Scepticism (1990) showed that these are not five arguments but one net: disagreement and relativity summon the demand for a ground, and the last three modes close every exit. It catches reasoning as such, which is why none of the Cartesian responses treated by this node's siblings reaches it: safety, dogmatism, and shifting standards all offer grounds, and grounds are what the modes eat. What a ground could be is homed at epistemology.justification; here the modes are a machine for producing equipollence, the condition in which opposed considerations weigh the same.

Equipollence is where Sextus locates the practice. The skeptic does not deny that anything is known — that "negative dogmatism" he attributes to the Academy — but has an ability to set appearances and arguments against each other so that, weighing equal, they induce epochē, suspension. Tranquility follows as a shadow follows a body — as the painter Apelles got the foam on a horse's mouth by flinging his sponge at the picture in despair. The skeptic's phrases — "no more this than that," "everything is undetermined" — are not assertions but reports of how he is affected, and they apply to themselves: like purgatives, they expel themselves along with the humors. Whether sentences can consistently do this is the first modern question.

The second is older, posed by the Stoics to the Academy: apraxia, inaction. Action requires assent; whoever suspends all assent will neither eat nor dodge a cart. Sextus replies that the skeptic follows the appearances without opinion, along four guides — nature, which makes him perceive and think; feeling, which makes him hungry; the laws and customs of his country; and instruction in crafts. The reply generates the quarrel of the last half-century, organized around Galen's distinction between rustic and urbane skeptics. Frede's "The Sceptic's Beliefs" (1979) holds that the Pyrrhonist has beliefs in a perfectly good sense — passive acquiescence in how things strike him — and withholds only dogmata, beliefs held on the authority of reason and theory. Burnyeat's "Can the Sceptic Live His Scepticism?" (1980) answers that appearances, for Sextus, are explicitly not assessed for truth, that belief is precisely such assessment, and that the insulation of appearance from truth which buys ataraxia is one no human being can maintain. Barnes's "The Beliefs of a Pyrrhonist" (1982) declined to choose: Sextus's scope varies with his target, rustic when disagreement runs against everyday judgments, urbane when the Outlines limit suspension to what is "unclear"; the text underdetermines the man. The three papers, collected in The Original Sceptics (1997), frame the debate.

Fogelin's Pyrrhonian Reflections on Knowledge and Justification (1994) revived the position rather than the text. The claim is double: the Agrippan modes remain unanswered by foundationalism and coherentism alike, and theories of justification discredit themselves by trying; yet ordinary knowledge claims, judged by the standards in force when made, are mostly true. The neo-Pyrrhonist holds both and declines to theorize their reconciliation. Williams replies that the modes presuppose a "Prior Grounding" requirement — a belief is justified only if the believer has already grounded it — itself a smuggled foundationalism; Lammenranta counters that disagreement alone regenerates the problematic without it.

Comparison with Indian and Buddhist thought is standard and hazardous. Pyrrho traveled with Alexander to India and, Diogenes Laertius says, met the gymnosophists. Beckwith's Greek Buddha (2015) and Kuzminski make the debt substantive, while Bett's Pyrrho, His Antecedents, and His Successors (2000) reads the Aristocles passage as attributing to Pyrrho a metaphysical claim that things are indeterminate — not Sextus's epistemic suspension, and not Buddhist either. Nāgārjuna's "I have no thesis" and Sextus's self-expelling phrases share a form, and Garfield has argued both are non-dogmatic in the same sense; but emptiness is a soteriological teaching within a tradition that keeps its four truths, whereas tranquility for Sextus is a byproduct one stops seeking. The closest structural kin is Jayarāśi's Tattvopaplavasiṃha, which dismantles every pramāṇa in turn — toward not ataraxia but the enjoyment of a life freed from doctrine. Zhuangzi's problem, as figures.zhuangzi argues, is the criterion rather than the regress. The traditions converge on suspension and diverge on what it is for; the register's rule is to let them.
