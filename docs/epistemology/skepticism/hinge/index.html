Hinge Epistemology

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-09-09.17

Moore said he knew he had hands. Wittgenstein's reply, in the notebooks published as On Certainty, was not that Moore was wrong but that "know" was the wrong word. Knowledge lives where checking has a place; but "I have hands," "the earth existed long before my birth," "there are physical objects" are what any checking would use, not what it could deliver. Doubt them and you have not become more rigorous; you have removed the ground on which doubt itself stands. These are hinges: exempt from doubt so that inquiry can turn, and in the same movement exempt from knowledge, because a claim nothing could confirm is not a claim to knowledge. Skeptic and Moore share an error: both treat a hinge as an ordinary proposition that happens to sit at the bottom of the pile. Everything since has been an argument over what a hinge is instead, and the readings fall along one axis, from not propositional at all to propositional but warranted otherwise.

The framework reading. Moyal-Sharrock takes the strongest line: hinges are not propositions but enacted, animal certainties — a way of acting, not a way of believing — and putting one into words yields a grammatical remark rather than an assertion. This explains why hinges seem both obviously true and somehow unsayable: what looks like a truth is a rule of description. The reading's difficulty is that hinges do serve as premises, can be denied, and in some cases (the earth's age, the existence of the Americas) came to be believed on evidence — features of the propositional, not the merely enacted.

The entitlement reading. Wright keeps hinges propositional and gives them a distinctive warrant. A cornerstone of a cognitive project — that there is an external world, that memory is roughly reliable — cannot be earned within the project it makes possible; but if we have no reason to doubt it and inquiry is impossible without accepting something of the kind, we are rationally entitled to it, without evidence. This concedes the skeptic's premise and denies the conclusion its sting: we do not know the hinge, but we are not irrational to trust it. The cost is the charge that entitlement is a pragmatic license wearing epistemic dress, and the leaching problem: if what I have for "I have hands" is knowledge and for "I am not envatted" only entitlement, closure threatens to drain the former down to the latter.

The non-belief reading. Pritchard argues that hinge commitments are not beliefs. Belief is responsive to reasons and aims at truth; one's commitment to not being radically in error — the über hinge, of which the particular hinges are local codifications — is visceral, arational, and unrevisable by evidence. Closure, on this view, governs the rational transmission of belief, and since hinges cannot be believed, it never engages them: the skeptic's second premise is not false but inapplicable. Critics reply that a commitment held true, acted upon, and expressible in a that-clause is a belief by any working criterion, and that the category has been carved to fit the need.

The constitutivist reading. Coliva's hinges are propositions, but neither warranted nor unwarranted: they are assumptions constitutive of epistemic rationality itself, since without them nothing counts as evidence for anything. They are rationally mandated the way the rules of a game are mandated for anyone playing. Coliva accepts the consequence that closure for warrant fails across the hinge: perceptual warrant for "I have hands" presupposes the hinge and so cannot transmit to it.

Closure. The common cost is visible. The vat argument (closure) needs hinges to be ordinary propositions in the field of knowledge and closure. Every hinge theory buys immunity by taking hinges out of that field — as non-propositional, as entitled rather than known, as non-beliefs, or as constitutive — and each must then explain why "I have hands" and "I am not a handless brain," which are related by simple entailment, receive standing so different that the principle governing entailment loses its grip between them.

Religious hinges. The extension is older than the program (Malcolm and Phillips, whose Wittgensteinian fideism Nielsen attacked) and now sharper. Pritchard's quasi-fideism holds that religious belief, like everything else, rests on arational commitments that are not thereby irrational; the parity argument runs that the theist's hinges are no worse off than anyone's. The objection is that genuine hinges are universal and unrevisable, while religious ones are local, contested, and lost in conversion — either they are not hinges, or hinge status does no evaluative work.
