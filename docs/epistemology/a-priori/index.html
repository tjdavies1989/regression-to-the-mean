A Priori Knowledge

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.6

You know that 7 + 5 = 12, that nothing is red and green all over, that if all whales are mammals and Moby is a whale then Moby is a mammal. You did not learn these the way you learned that water boils at lower temperatures at altitude. No observation seems relevant to them; no observation seems capable of overturning them. The a priori is the standing puzzle this raises: how could a creature whose information channels are all sensory know anything independently of experience — and know it, apparently, with a necessity and certainty that experience never delivers?

The rationalist tradition (Plato, Descartes, Leibniz) took the phenomenon at face value: reason is itself a source of knowledge, grasping necessary truths directly, and such knowledge is the model to which the rest should aspire. Its strength is the data — mathematics exists, and no empiricist account of it has ever been comfortable. Empiricists from Hume to Mill countered that all substantive knowledge comes from experience; the apparent exceptions are either disguised generalizations from experience (Mill on arithmetic) or empty — "relations of ideas" that report only how we have connected our concepts. The logical positivists systematized the second option: all a priori knowledge is analytic, true in virtue of meaning alone, and hence tells us nothing about the world; the mystery dissolves because there was nothing substantive to know. This elegantly explains the necessity (we would not count anything as a counterexample) at the price of deflating mathematics to convention.

Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) demolished the settlement by arguing that the analytic/synthetic distinction cannot be drawn without circularity — analyticity gets explained by synonymy, synonymy by necessity, necessity by analyticity — and that in the web of belief any statement, logic included, is in principle revisable under recalcitrant experience. If nothing is immune to revision, nothing is a priori in the traditional sense. The strongest part is the holism; critics (Grice and Strawson, later Boghossian) reply that the distinction's resistance to reductive definition does not show it is unreal.

Kripke's Naming and Necessity (1972) then uncoupled what everyone had equated. Apriority is epistemic, necessity metaphysical, and they come apart in both directions: the stipulator of the standard meter knows a priori that stick S is one meter long, though the fact is contingent; that water is H2O is necessary, though only chemistry could discover it. The old debate had assumed one distinction where there are two, and the necessary a posteriori in particular handed metaphysics an empirical method.

The contemporary field divides over what remains. BonJour defends full-blooded rationalism: rational insight into necessity is indispensable, since without some a priori entitlement even reasoning from experience to anything beyond it would be impossible — empiricism, consistently applied, self-destructs into skepticism. Williamson argues the whole a priori/a posteriori distinction is shallow: in actual cognition — his example is knowing that all crimson things are red — experience plays a role that is more than enabling but less than evidential, and the binary classification cuts across the epistemically significant joints. Experimental philosophy presses from below: if the intuitions that supposedly manifest rational insight vary with culture, framing, and order of presentation, their evidential credentials need earning, not assuming. The defender's best reply is a companions-in-guilt argument: perception is variable and fallible too, and no one concludes it is not evidence.

Bridges

  • To logic: logical knowledge is the a priori's hardest test case — any argument for or against apriority already uses logic, so the entitlement cannot be evidential in the ordinary way.
  • To language: analyticity is jointly owned — whether meaning alone can make truths is as much semantics as epistemology.
  • To metaphysics.modality: Kripke's uncoupling routes here; modal epistemology (conceivability, counterfactual competence) is homed there.
  • To meta.methods: the standing of intuitions decides whether armchair philosophy has any evidence at all; experimental philosophy's challenge is prosecuted there.
