Art and Morality

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.12

Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will is, by common consent, both masterfully made and morally repugnant. The question this node owns is whether those two verdicts touch: is the film's viciousness a flaw in it as art, or a flaw in it that art criticism should pass over? Everyone agrees a work can be morally bad; the dispute is whether moral badness ever counts against — or for — artistic merit.

Radical autonomism says never: aesthetic and moral value are separate currencies, and to dock a work for its morals is to change the subject, as if faulting a proof for being rude. Its strength is that it explains the critic's actual practice of admiring what appalls, and it protects art from moralizing capture. Moderate moralism (Carroll) replies that some moral defects are aesthetic ones — namely those that interfere with the work's own project. A thriller that asks sympathy for a character we cannot but despise fails on its own terms; the moral flaw is an artistic malfunction. Ethicism (Gaut) generalizes this through the merited-response argument: works prescribe responses (amusement, sympathy, admiration), a response is merited only if it is appropriate to its object, and a prescribed response that is unethical is thereby unmerited — so a work whose moral perspective is corrupt fails to merit the responses it seeks, and that failure is aesthetic. Immoralism (Jacobson) presses the countercase: some works are good because they are morally dangerous — a brilliant satire or a seductive villain achieves its distinctive value by getting us to feel what we should not, and sanitizing it would make it worse art. The flaw, if it is one, can be the point.

Beneath the position-taking lies a phenomenon: imaginative resistance. Readers who will imagine dragons and time travel without protest balk when a fiction asks them to imagine that a massacre was right. Whether this is inability or refusal, and why moral propositions resist fictional truth when physical ones do not, is evidence about how fictions prescribe attitudes at all — and each side of the ethical-criticism debate reads the evidence its own way. Propaganda sharpens the stakes: formally accomplished art enlisted for evil seems to make its audience complicit in responses they would disown, which is the ethicist's best case and the autonomist's hardest one. The debate's current public face is the immoral-artist problem — whether the maker's crimes reach the work, whether biographical knowledge legitimately alters aesthetic response, and what audiences may still enjoy. And behind all of it stands the oldest answer: Plato expelled the poets precisely because he thought artistic power over feeling is moral power, making censorship not a philistine intrusion on aesthetics but a position within it.

The node's home is aesthetics, but its spine runs into ethics: merited response is a moral notion, complicity a moral charge, and the autonomist's separation of value spheres is itself a substantive ethical thesis.
