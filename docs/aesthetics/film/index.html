Philosophy of Film

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.12

Point a camera at a play and you have recorded a performance, not made a work. So ran the earliest philosophical verdict on cinema: a machine that merely registers what stands before it leaves no room for the shaping activity that art requires. The first film theorists answered by locating that activity in what the camera does not faithfully deliver — Münsterberg in the mind-like operations of close-up and flashback, Arnheim in the medium's departures from ordinary vision (flatness, framing, the absence of color and sound). Film became art, on this defense, precisely where it stopped being a recording. The argument succeeded — no one now doubts that film is an art — but it bequeathed the discipline its longest-running commitment: medium specificity, the thesis that each art should do what its material basis does best, and that film's aesthetics must be derived from its distinctive means. Noël Carroll's sustained attack on this thesis — media have no essence that dictates their proper use, and "film" itself names a shifting family of moving-image technologies — is a rare case of a founding doctrine largely abandoned under argument.

The deepest divide concerns photography itself. For the formalist tradition, film's photographic basis was an embarrassment to be overcome by montage and manipulation. André Bazin inverted the valuation: photography's automatic, mechanical genesis gives film a unique realism — the image is not a likeness of the world but a trace of it — and the styles that honor this (the long take, deep focus) are truest to the medium. Kendall Walton radicalized the idea into the transparency thesis: photographs are perceptual aids through which we literally see the photographed things, as we see through mirrors and telescopes. Critics reply that seeing requires the counterfactual dependence and egocentric information that pictures lack; the debate continues, and matters, because on it hangs what documentary footage can be evidence of.

Film is made by crowds — writers, cinematographers, editors, actors — yet criticism wants an artist. Auteurism answered that the director is the author whose sensibility unifies the work; its strongest ground is critical practice, which finds robust cross-film styles in Hitchcock or Ozu. Its critics answer that attributing a collective product to one mind is mythology, and propose multiple or collective authorship, or (with Berys Gaut) abandoning the single-author model altogether.

Spectatorship generates the field's most active current debates. What is it to follow a fiction film — do we imagine seeing the events depicted, as George Wilson argues, or does the imagined-observer model collapse into absurdity (are we imagining ourselves teleported among the characters, invisible?), as Gregory Currie objects? What is "identification" with a character, and is it one relation or several — sympathy, empathy, alignment? Cognitive film theory pursues these questions with philosophy of mind against an older psychoanalytic tradition. Stanley Cavell's ontology stands somewhat apart: film is "a succession of automatic world projections," a medium that satisfies the modern skeptical wish to view the world unseen — a treatment more inherited as provocation than doctrine.

The frontier case is interactive: videogames are moving images whose viewers act in them. Whether they are a new art form, how interactivity changes authorship, spectatorship, and fiction (the player is both audience and character), and whether "film" theory extends or breaks here is the live edge of the field.
