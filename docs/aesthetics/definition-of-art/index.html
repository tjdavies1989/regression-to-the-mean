The Definition of Art

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.12

A term that covers Chartres cathedral, a Beethoven quartet, Ulysses, and a signed urinal looks like it should have a definition — necessary and sufficient conditions for arthood. The search for one is aesthetics' oldest project, and its history is a graveyard with instructive epitaphs. Art as representation (Plato's mimesis) died of abstraction: a Rothko imitates nothing. Art as expression of emotion (Tolstoy, Collingwood) died twice over — much great art is coolly impersonal, and much sincere emoting is not art. Formalism (Bell's "significant form") rescued abstraction but could not say what significant form is without circularity, and it excluded the conceptual art that was about to arrive. Each theory canonized one movement's achievements and was then falsified by the next movement — often deliberately, since crossing the current boundary is a standing avant-garde strategy.

Weitz (1956) drew the skeptical moral: "art," like Wittgenstein's "game," is an open concept, held together by family resemblances rather than an essence, and necessarily so — a closed definition would foreclose the creativity that is art's point. This is the position every later definition must answer, and its strength is its track record: it predicted the failure of the classical theories and explains why artists keep breaking fences.

The field's turning point came from an artwork. Warhol's Brillo Boxes (1964) are perceptually near-indistinguishable from the supermarket cartons they replicate, yet the one is art and the other not — so, Danto argued, arthood cannot be a manifest property at all. What separates indiscernibles is something the eye cannot descry: a surrounding atmosphere of theory and art history, an "artworld," within which the work embodies a meaning. Dickie stripped the idea of its intellectual atmosphere and hardened it into the institutional theory: an artwork is an artifact on which standing has been conferred by someone acting on behalf of the artworld's practices. Its strength is coverage — it accommodates Fountain, readymades, and whatever comes next; its notorious cost is that it makes conferral look arbitrary, silent on why anything is conferred on.

Two major successors keep definition alive while honoring Weitz's data. Levinson's historical definition ties arthood backward rather than downward: something is art if it is intended for regard in ways prior artworks were correctly regarded — the concept unfolds recursively through history, open at the front, anchored at the back (leaving first art as its hard case). Gaut's cluster account concedes that no single condition is necessary: a list of criteria — aesthetic properties, expressiveness, formal complexity, intention, institutional position — counts toward arthood, with enough of them sufficient. It trades the elegance of a definition for fidelity to actual classificatory practice.

Behind the contest sits the question of what hangs on it. Something does: "but is it art?" governs museum walls, public funding, customs law (Brancusi's Bird in Space was litigated as raw metal), and the admissibility of each avant-garde. Deflationists reply that classification and evaluation come apart — settling that something is art settles nothing about whether it is any good — and that the real work lies with the concept's function, not its boundary. Whether "art" needs defining, or only understanding historically and institutionally, is itself now a position on the board.
