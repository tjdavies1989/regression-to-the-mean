Beauty

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.12

Nothing in aesthetics is more familiar or more elusive. We call faces, coastlines, proofs, and sonatas beautiful, and two facts about the practice pull in opposite directions. Responses vary — across cultures, eras, and neighbors — which suggests that beauty lies in the beholder. Yet the practice behaves as if there were something to be right about: we point to features, train our perception, and experience beauty as a discovery, not a decision. The question of this node is not how judgments of taste claim validity — that machinery is homed under aesthetic experience — but what beauty itself is.

Three families of answers divide the field. Objectivists hold that beauty is a real feature of things: the classical tradition located it in proportion, harmony, and unity in variety, from the Pythagoreans through Hutcheson's "uniformity amidst variety." Its strength is that it takes the phenomenology at face value — beauty presents itself as in the object, and only a real feature explains how it can be missed, argued for, and found. Subjectivists, from Hume to Santayana's "pleasure objectified," identify beauty with a certain pleasure projected onto its cause; their strength is intimacy — a beauty no one could ever feel seems idle, and no list of objective proportions has survived its counterexamples. Response-dependence accounts split the difference: beauty is a disposition to please suitably situated perceivers, on the model of secondary qualities like color. This keeps both data — beauty is constituted by response, yet a perceiver can get it wrong — at the price of specifying "suitably situated" without circularity.

Kant contributed a distinction that still organizes the debate: free beauty (a flower, an ornament) is judged without a concept of what the thing is supposed to be, while dependent beauty (a beautiful horse, a beautiful church) presupposes one. The sublime is beauty's structured contrast: the vast, formless, or overwhelming — the storm, the starry sky — first defeats the imagination and then elates. Burke traced the elation to terror at a safe distance; Kant to reason's discovery that the mind outscales anything sense presents. Either way, the sublime shows that aesthetic value is not exhausted by pleasing form.

The twentieth century exiled beauty from art and criticism. After the avant-garde, beauty looked like kitsch — the currency of advertising and propaganda, an anesthetic rather than an achievement — and serious critics stopped using the word. Its revival is recent. Danto's The Abuse of Beauty argues that beauty is one aesthetic quality among many, essential to some works' meaning and rightly refused by others, but never the definition of art. Nehamas recovers Stendhal's formula — beauty is a promise of happiness — recasting it not as static delight but as a summons to sustained engagement with an object one does not yet fully understand. Nehamas also presses the oldest connection: in Plato's Symposium, beauty is the object of eros, a ladder from beautiful bodies toward Beauty itself. Whether the beauty of artworks is continuous with erotic attractiveness, or must be quarantined from it by Kantian disinterestedness, remains a live and slightly embarrassed question. Finally, natural beauty claims priority: Kant took nature, not art, as beauty's paradigm, and environmental aesthetics argues that an artworld-centered theory distorts the phenomenon. That debate is homed under environmental and everyday aesthetics, but its pressure is felt here.
