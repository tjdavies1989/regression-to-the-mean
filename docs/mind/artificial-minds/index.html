Philosophy of Mind

Artificial Minds

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.8

Could a machine think, understand, or feel? And — the question that gives the first its bite — how would anyone tell? Between the thermostat and the human now sit systems that converse fluently about their own alleged experiences. The problem is method: every test for mind was calibrated on creatures, and machines are where behavior and constitution come apart.

Turing saw this from the start. His 1950 paper does not define thinking; it declares "Can machines think?" too meaningless to discuss and replaces it with the imitation game — could an interrogator, conversing by teletype, reliably distinguish machine from human? Folklore reads the test as a criterion of mind; Turing offered it as a fair sampling of intelligent capacities and a prediction about how the word "thinking" would come to be used. For broadly behaviorist and functionalist views, the substitution is the position: sufficiently rich performance is not evidence of thought but constitutive of it.

Searle's Chinese Room is the standing objection. A man in a room, following an English rulebook for manipulating Chinese symbols, passes the Turing test in Chinese while understanding nothing; syntax, Searle concludes, does not suffice for semantics, so no computer understands merely by running the right program. The systems reply, at full force: the man is only the processor, and it is the whole system — man, rulebook, ledgers — that understands, just as no neuron speaks English; Searle's rejoinder (let the man memorize the rulebook) arguably relocates the system without eliminating it. The robot reply, at full force: couple the program to the world through sensors and effectors, and the grounding Searle finds missing is exactly what has been added. Searle denies that causation supplies content; his opponents answer that on any naturalistic theory of intentionality it is precisely where content comes from — making the Room a referendum on those theories, not a free-standing refutation.

Computationalism — mind as the software of the brain — is the framework at stake, and its discontents go beyond Searle: triviality arguments that almost any physical system can be mapped onto almost any computation; Harnad's symbol-grounding problem; embodiment arguments that cognition is constitutively sensorimotor, so a disembodied program simulates a mind as it simulates a hurricane.

Large language models are the live case, and the classical arguments underdetermine it. They are not the hand-coded symbol systems Searle targeted, yet they are textbook candidates for ungrounded symbol manipulation — "stochastic parrots" on one reading, systems with emergent world-models on another. What is genuinely new is the assessment problem: fluent verbal report is exactly what such systems are trained to produce, so their introspective testimony — claims of experience or of its absence alike — cannot be taken at face value, and behavioral tests fail wherever the training data contains the test. Hence the turn to theory-driven indicators (global workspace, higher-order theories) and to the ethics of uncertainty: if the probability of machine sentience is nonzero, moral status cannot wait on proof, and false positives and false negatives both carry costs (bridge: ethics).
