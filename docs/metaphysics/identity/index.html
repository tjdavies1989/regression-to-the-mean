Identity and Persistence

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.3

Nothing seems easier than identity: everything is what it is and not another thing. The trouble begins with a distinction. Two copies of a book are qualitatively identical — alike in their properties — but numerically distinct: two things, not one. Numerical identity is the relation everything bears to itself and to nothing else, and its governing principle is Leibniz's Law: if a is identical to b, then whatever is true of a is true of b. The principle looks trivially secure, yet nearly every puzzle in this territory is an alleged counterexample to it. Lois believes Superman can fly and doubts Clark Kent can; the statue is badly made but the clay is not; the ship was once entirely oaken and is so no longer. Defenders of the Law answer that these cases confuse it with the false converse (indiscernibles need not be identical), or trade on intensional contexts where substitution fails for linguistic rather than metaphysical reasons, or misdescribe one thing as two. Which diagnosis fits which case is where the real work lies.

Persistence sharpens the problem. A candle is straight at noon and bent at midnight; if the noon candle is the midnight candle, one thing is both straight and bent — apparently contradicting Leibniz's Law. This is Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics, and the main theories of persistence are rival solutions. Endurantism holds that objects are wholly present whenever they exist, and relativizes property-possession to times: the candle is straight-at-noon. Its strength is fidelity to common sense — the thing before you now is not a mere slice of something longer. Perdurantism holds that objects are four-dimensional wholes with temporal parts; the noon part is straight, the midnight part bent, and no one thing is both simpliciter. Its strength is that it dissolves the contradiction cleanly and handles fission cases with ease. Stage theory keeps the temporal parts but identifies ordinary objects with the momentary stages themselves, related by a counterpart relation across time — preserving perdurantist resources while making "the candle" refer to something wholly present after all.

The ship of Theseus is the stress test. A ship's planks are replaced one by one; the removed planks are reassembled into a second ship. Continuity of form and function favors the repaired ship; sameness of matter favors the reassembled one; both cannot be the original, since they are two. Every theory of persistence owes an answer, and the case genuinely constrains theory: it forces a choice among continuity criteria, best-candidate accounts, and the concession that identity over time is partly a matter of convention.

Some philosophers respond by revising identity itself. Geach argued that identity is relative: a and b may be the same ship but not the same collection of planks, and there is no absolute relation beneath the sortal-relative ones — a view most now resist but which forced the role of sortal concepts into the open. Others locate the trouble in vagueness: could it be simply indeterminate whether a is b? Evans's celebrated one-page argument answers no — if it were indeterminate, a and b would differ in a property (being determinately identical to a), hence be distinct, hence determinately so. And Kripke, from modest modal premises, derived the necessity of identity: if a is b, a is necessarily b, since a is necessarily self-identical. True identities hold of necessity; contingency, where it appears, belongs to descriptions, not to things — a result whose machinery lives in logic.modal.
