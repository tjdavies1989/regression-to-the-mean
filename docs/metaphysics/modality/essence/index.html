Essence and Essentialism

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-08-26.7

For a generation, essentialism was not a position but a diagnosis. Quine's mathematical cyclist made the case: mathematicians are necessarily rational and accidentally two-legged; cyclists necessarily two-legged and accidentally rational. What, then, of a cycling mathematician? If necessity attaches to objects only under a description, the question has no answer — and quantified modal logic, which asks it of objects simpliciter, presupposes an "invidious Aristotelian essentialism" that Quine offered as its reductio. The mid-century consensus took the lesson: de re modality was confusion, necessity at best a feature of how we talk.

Kripke reversed the verdict by dissolving its premise. Names are rigid designators; once "Socrates" tracks one man across every possibility, asking what he could have been requires no privileged description. And with de re modality legitimate, Kripke argued for substantive essential truths. Origin: this table could not have come from a wholly different block of wood — a table so made would at best be a duplicate, since nothing would tie it to this one; a person could not have come from different gametes. Kind: the table could not have been ice, for an ice table would be a distinct object merely resembling it; if Socrates is human, he is necessarily so. Composition: a thing's original material constitution constrains what it could have been. Each is knowable only a posteriori — essence and the armchair came apart even as essence returned. The arguments have their own literature: Salmon showed the origin argument needs a sufficiency premise stronger than Kripke's text supplies, and Robertson's tolerance and recycling puzzles make the modest premises yield too little necessity or too much.

Kripke's rehabilitation still defined essence modally: an essential property is one a thing has necessarily if it exists. Fine's 1994 counterexamples inverted the order. Socrates necessarily belongs to {Socrates}, is necessarily distinct from the Eiffel Tower, necessarily exists while 2+2=4 — yet none of this is part of what Socrates is. The modal account overgenerates, Fine argued, because essence is definitional, not modal: the real definition of a thing, what its being consists in, with a constitutive core and merely consequential penumbra. Necessity then flows from essence — metaphysical necessity as truth in virtue of the nature of all things — rather than defining it. The essence-first program built on this: Lowe's "serious essentialism," on which essences are not further entities but the very identity of things; Hale's essentialist grounding of necessary beings; Correia's refinements of the logic and varieties of essence. Ontological dependence is recast in the same idiom — x depends on y when y figures in x's essence — cutting finer than modal-existential accounts, on which Socrates and {Socrates} depend symmetrically.

Against all this stand the deflations. Sidelle's conventionalism keeps Kripke's data while draining their metaphysics: the necessary a posteriori factors into an analytic frame ("whatever water's actual composition, it has it necessarily") plus empirical filling; essences are shadows of our conventions of individuation, not joints of the world. Thomasson's modal normativism goes further: metaphysical modal claims are not descriptions at all but object-language expressions of semantic rules — mandates in the material mode — which makes modal knowledge cheap (conceptual competence plus empirical fact) and modal metaphysics largely verbal. The live question is thus stark: is essence discovered or legislated? Deflationists must explain why essence seems to explain — why definitions look like findings; essentialists must explain how a faculty tuned to the actual detects natures at all, a bill presented at modal-epistemology.
