Modal Epistemology

◔ sketch  ·  anchor  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-08-26.7

Whatever possible worlds are, none but this one is ever observed. So every modal belief that outruns actuality — that there could have been talking donkeys, that you could not have had different parents — needs a source, and the candidates on offer are few and strained.

The oldest candidate is conceivability. Yablo gave it its careful modern form: to conceive that p is not merely to find no contradiction in p, nor to fail to see its impossibility, but to imagine a world one takes to verify p — an appearance of possibility, defeasible like perceptual appearance but evidence all the same. The pressure on any such principle comes from Kripke's a posteriori necessities. We can apparently conceive that water is not H₂O or that Hesperus is not Phosphorus, yet these are impossible; so either conceivability misleads or "conceive" needs disambiguating. Chalmers's refinement does the second, splitting the notion along two axes: ideal conceivability (surviving unlimited rational reflection) versus prima facie, and primary conceivability (a way the world might actually be, considered as such) versus secondary (a way it might counterfactually have been). What the water-thinker conceives is primarily but not secondarily possible — a world where the watery stuff is XYZ — so the Kripkean cases are absorbed, and the surviving thesis is that ideal primary positive conceivability entails primary possibility. That thesis is the engine of the zombie argument; whether it is a discovery or a definition of "ideal" is the live dispute.

Van Inwagen's modal skepticism concedes the everyday and doubts the exotic. We know that the table could have stood elsewhere and cannot know that there could have been transparent iron or a maximally great being: our imaginative verdicts are trained on cases close to actuality, and philosophy's remote possibility claims are precisely where the training gives out — as Yablo's own account predicts, since imagining a verifying world requires detail the remote cases never supply.

Williamson relocates the whole capacity. Evaluating counterfactuals is an ordinary, evolutionarily unmysterious skill; since "necessarily p" is equivalent to "if not-p were the case, a contradiction would be," modal knowledge falls out of counterfactual competence with no special faculty. This deflates the how possible question but vindicates less than it seems to: the imaginative simulations that ground counterfactual judgment are reliable where they are constrained by experience — roughly van Inwagen's safe zone — and philosophy's cases are exactly where the constraints lapse. Critics (Roca-Royes among them) add that the equivalence makes knowledge of necessities rest on counterpossibles the simulation heuristic handles worst.

Two further programs bypass conceivability altogether. Lowe, following the Finean inversion, holds that we grasp essences simply in understanding what things are — no essence, no reference — and derive modal truth from real definition; the epistemology is only as secure as our claim to know essences non-modally. Modal empiricists ground modal knowledge in actuality itself: similarity (this glass can break, because a like one did), induction, and extrapolation from experienced variation — safe for nearby possibility, silent about zombies, and content to be so.

The stakes are out of proportion to the security. The zombie argument against physicalism, Descartes's conceived disembodiment, and the Gödelian ontological argument — where S5 converts mere possibility of a perfect being into actuality — each run through a single remote-possibility premise. Philosophy's biggest conclusions hang on its shakiest epistemology, and each account above draws the line between trustworthy and untrustworthy modal judgment just where its proponents' other commitments need it.
