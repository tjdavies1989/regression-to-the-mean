Counterpart Theory and Transworld Identity

◔ sketch  ·  standard  ·  layer 2  ·  pass 2026-08-26.7

Humphrey could have won in 1968: at some world, Humphrey wins. But which inhabitant of that world is Humphrey? For a mid-century moment this looked like a deep problem — philosophers demanded "criteria of transworld identity," qualitative marks by which to locate one individual across possibilities. Kripke's response was deflation. The demand rests on a bad picture, of worlds as distant planets viewed through a telescope, their inhabitants awaiting identification. Worlds are not discovered but stipulated: when we say "Humphrey might have won," we specify the possibility as one concerning Humphrey, and no qualitative criterion is needed, any more than one is needed to say which of two dice might have landed six. For anyone whose worlds are abstract representations, transworld identity is trivial: the representation simply mentions the man himself.

Lewis cannot say this. His worlds are concrete and disjoint, so nothing is part of two of them — and he argued independently that overlap would founder on accidental intrinsics: if Humphrey himself inhabited a world where he has six fingers, would he have five fingers or six? So individuals are worldbound, and represented elsewhere by counterparts: inhabitants of other worlds that resemble them closely, in relevant respects, more than anything else there does. Because counterparthood is similarity, it inherits similarity's disorder — it need not be transitive or symmetric, a thing may have several counterparts at a world or none, and, crucially, it is inconstant: which respects of similarity matter shifts with how the object is picked out.

Kripke's footnote 13 pressed the intuitive objection: Humphrey "could not care less" whether someone else, however much resembling him, wins in another possible world. Lewis's reply distinguishes analysis from analysandum. The modal property — possibly winning — belongs to Humphrey himself; he has it in virtue of his counterpart's victory, just as, on ersatzism, he has it in virtue of a set of sentences, which contains him even less. Every actualist treatment makes de re modality turn on something other than the man's own victory; if that is an offense, all parties give it.

Inconstancy is the theory's genuine selling point. Lumpl the clay and Goliath the statue, created and destroyed together, seem one thing — yet Lumpl could have survived squashing and Goliath could not, and Leibniz's Law then forces two coincident objects. Counterpart theory dissolves the puzzle: one object, two names evoking different counterpart relations — trace it by lump-similarity and the squashed thing is a counterpart, by statue-similarity and it is not. De re modal predication becomes covertly relative, like "big." Admirers call this constitution solved for free; critics reply that it relocates modality from things to our ways of describing them — an irony, since it half-concedes the Quinean suspicion Kripke had discredited.

The deepest fault line is haecceitism: can worlds differ non-qualitatively, in which individuals fill the roles? Chisholm's paradox builds the case as a sorites — world by world, Adam and Noah swap properties until a final world qualitatively just like ours with the two men interchanged; tolerant essences plus S4 deliver either that conclusion or bare, arbitrary essentialist cutoffs. Responses map the field: Chisholm and Salmon abandon the transitivity of accessibility; Forbes blurs essences; Lewis exploits intransitive counterparthood to halt the slide without touching the logic, and offers "cheap haecceitism" — one world representing distinct de re possibilities under different counterpart mappings — while officially holding, as anti-haecceitist, that worlds differ only qualitatively. Adams's "Primitive Thisness and Primitive Identity" (1979) argues the other side: thisnesses — being this very thing — are primitive, irreducible to qualitative suchness, as a world of two indiscernible spheres shows; identity across worlds then needs no qualitative grounding at all, vindicating Kripke's stipulation from beneath.
