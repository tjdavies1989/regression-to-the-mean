René Descartes

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.3

1596–1650 · French philosopher, mathematician, and natural scientist; by convention the founder of rationalism and of modern philosophy

Why they matter

Descartes reset philosophy's starting point. Where the scholastic curriculum began with the world — being, substance, the categories — Descartes began with the knower, asking what a single mind, trusting nothing on authority, could establish for itself; epistemology sits at the front of the modern canon because he put it there. The instruments he forged became permanent fixtures: methodical doubt as a test of foundations, the cogito as the residue doubt cannot dissolve, and the real distinction between mind and body, which gave the mind–body problem the shape it still has. He was simultaneously a working scientist — co-inventor of analytic geometry, author of a complete mechanist physics — and the two projects are one: the metaphysics was built, he said, to be the roots of a tree whose trunk is physics. Much of what he concluded is now rejected; almost none of it can be ignored, because an unusual number of contemporary positions — in epistemology, philosophy of mind, cognitive science — are defined by their opposition to something Descartes said first and most clearly.

Life

Born in 1596 at La Haye en Touraine (now simply "Descartes"), he was educated at the Jesuit college of La Flèche, whose scholastic curriculum he later said left him convinced only of his own ignorance, and took a law degree at Poitiers. He then chose the world over the library: military service in the Dutch and Bavarian armies, travel, and — in a stove-heated room in Germany in November 1619 — the night of dreams that persuaded him he was called to rebuild the sciences on a single method. From 1628 he lived in the Dutch Republic, moving often and guarding his solitude, connected to Paris through the correspondence network of Marin Mersenne. Galileo's condemnation in 1633 led him to suppress The World, his completed physics, since it assumed the Earth's motion. A daughter, Francine, died at five in 1640 — by his own account the greatest sorrow of his life. From 1643 he corresponded with Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, his most penetrating critic and the addressee of his moral philosophy. In 1649 he accepted Queen Christina's invitation to Stockholm, where the five a.m. tutorials of a Swedish winter helped kill him; he died of pneumonia in February 1650.

Works

  • Rules for the Direction of the Mind (c. 1628, unfinished, posthumous) — the early methodology: knowledge as intuition plus deduction, on the model of mathematics.
  • Discourse on the Method (1637) — the intellectual autobiography and manifesto, prefacing three scientific essays (Optics, Meteorology, and the Geometry that founded analytic geometry).
  • Meditations on First Philosophy (1641) — the metaphysical core, published with seven sets of Objections and Replies from critics including Hobbes, Arnauld, and Gassendi — a built-in adversarial process without real precedent.
  • Principles of Philosophy (1644) — the system in textbook form: metaphysics, then the full mechanist physics.
  • The Passions of the Soul (1649) — the psychology and ethics of the mind–body union, written for Elisabeth.
  • The correspondence — especially with Elisabeth (1643–49), now part of the canon rather than an appendix to it.

Thought

The method of doubt

Cartesian doubt is an instrument, not a temperament. Descartes was no skeptic; he designed the most powerful skeptical arguments in the literature in order to break them, on the principle that a foundation is secure only if it survives the worst attack constructible. The First Meditation escalates deliberately: the senses sometimes deceive (which impugns only distant and small things); I cannot rule out that I am dreaming (which impugns the senses wholesale but leaves arithmetic standing); and finally, for all I know my creator — or, in the methodological fiction, a malicious demon of utmost power — has made me err even in the simplest judgments. The doubt is hyperbolic by design: it treats a belief's merely possible falsehood as grounds for setting it aside — a standard applied only here, once in a life, to find what survives it. This distinguishes his project from Pyrrhonism, whose doubt aims at suspension of judgment and tranquility; Descartes borrows the skeptics' weapons to end skepticism. The legacy is double: the dreaming and demon arguments became the canonical statements of external-world skepticism, detached from the constructive project they were built to serve — modern epistemology inherited the acid without the foundation it was meant to test.

The cogito

One thing survives the demon: I am, I exist is true whenever I think it, because doubting is itself thinking, and even an all-powerful deceiver needs a victim who exists. What exactly the cogito is remains disputed. Read as an inference ("whatever thinks exists; I think; so I exist") it seems to presuppose a general premise the doubt has not yet certified; Descartes insisted it is rather a direct intuition, and later commentators (notably Hintikka) have read it as performative — the attempt to think "I do not exist" defeats itself in the performance. What it establishes is deliberately thin: my existence, for as long as I am thinking, as a thing that thinks — doubts, understands, affirms, denies, wills, imagines, senses. It does not yet establish that I am an immaterial soul (that argument comes later and separately, a point Descartes stressed against hasty readers), nor that I persist between thoughts; and critics from Gassendi to Lichtenberg have pressed whether even the "I" is earned — whether the evidence licenses more than "there is thinking." But as a fixed point it did its work: it relocated certainty from the world to the first person, and made self-consciousness the paradigm of knowledge — the most consequential reorientation in modern philosophy.

Clear and distinct perception, God, and the circle

From the cogito Descartes extracts a general rule: what made that first certainty certain was that I perceived it with complete clarity and distinctness; so whatever I perceive clearly and distinctly is true. But the demon hypothesis still stands against the rule — perhaps I am so made that even my clearest perceptions deceive. Removing it is God's job. The Third Meditation argues that my idea of an infinite, perfect being has more representational content than a finite mind could manufacture, so only God can be its cause (the "trademark" argument); the Fifth adds an ontological argument: existence belongs to God's essence as the angle-sum belongs to the triangle's. A perfect being cannot be a deceiver — deception is a defect — so my clearest faculties, properly used, are trustworthy; error is traced (Fourth Meditation) to the will's assenting beyond what the understanding clearly perceives. Arnauld, in the Fourth Objections, stated the standing problem: clear and distinct perception is used to prove the God who is then invoked to validate clear and distinct perception — the Cartesian circle. Descartes' reply distinguishes present perception from recollection: while actually attending to a clear and distinct perception I cannot doubt it, and God is needed only to secure remembered demonstrations and defeat the hyperbolic doubt wholesale. Whether this — or the bolder reading on which the goal is stable, irreversible certainty rather than an external guarantee — escapes the circle is still argued; the circle remains the textbook case of the problem facing any attempt to validate one's faculties without using them.

The real distinction and Elisabeth's objection

The Sixth Meditation argues that mind and body are really distinct substances. I have a clear and distinct idea of myself as a thinking, non-extended thing, and of body as an extended, non-thinking thing; whatever I can clearly and distinctly conceive apart, God can create apart; so I could exist without my body. The argument's form — from conceivability, via essences, to a modal and then an ontological conclusion — made it the template for every later conceivability argument in philosophy of mind, and its premises mark the classic pressure points: whether my grasp of my own essence is complete enough to license the inference (Arnauld: a man ignorant of geometry can conceive a right triangle without the Pythagorean property), and whether conceivability proves possibility. Yet Descartes equally insisted that I am not a pilot in a ship: mind and body form a genuine union, "intermingled," as pain and hunger attest. Princess Elisabeth, in May 1643, put the sharpest contemporary question: how can an immaterial, unextended soul move the body, when moving something seems to require contact and extension? Descartes answered that we possess three primitive notions — thought, extension, and the union — each understood through itself; the union is known not by metaphysics but in ordinary life, and the error is to model soul–body interaction on body–body contact. Elisabeth replied that she found it easier to grant the soul extension than to accept causation she could not conceive — and Descartes conceded, remarkably, that the mind cannot distinctly conceive the soul's distinctness and its union with the body at the same time. The exchange — the first and sharpest statement of the interaction problem, pressed harder than in any of the published Objections — set the agenda for the next half-century: occasionalism, pre-established harmony, and Spinoza's monism are all attempts to keep what Descartes put asunder without the interaction he could not explain. The pineal gland, his proposed seat of interaction, answered where but never how.

Mechanist physics and the animal machine

Descartes' physics identifies matter with geometrical extension: body just is what has length, breadth, and depth, from which it follows that there is no vacuum, that matter is indefinitely divisible, and that all natural change is matter in motion governed by laws — God conserves a constant quantity of motion, and the laws (including the first published statement of rectilinear inertia) follow from His immutability. This was a declaration of war on scholastic natural philosophy: substantial forms, real qualities, and natural teleology are banished as projections of the mind onto matter; the color is in us, the corpuscles are in the world. The program's ambition was total — magnetism, the tides, the planets, the animal body — and if its detailed mechanisms (the vortices, the subtle matter) died with Newton, the meta-level conquest was permanent: nature as law-governed mechanism, explanation as reduction to configuration and motion. Its most notorious corollary is the bête-machine: animals, lacking souls, are automata — exquisitely engineered, feeling nothing, their cries no more evidence of pain than a clock's chime is of thought. Descartes' criterion for the difference is striking: no machine, and no animal, could use language flexibly or reason across arbitrary subject matters — general-purpose reason, he argued, cannot arise from any arrangement of organs. The claim is an ancestor of the Turing test with the predicted verdict reversed, and it fixed a question that has never closed: what behavior, if any, would show that a mechanism thinks or feels?

The Passions of the Soul

Elisabeth's questions about the union pushed Descartes from metaphysics to psychology, and his last book is the payoff. The passions — wonder, love, hatred, desire, joy, sadness, and their compounds — are perceptions in the soul caused and sustained by motions of the body's animal spirits; they are not diseases of the mind but functional states, instituted by nature to incline the soul toward what is (usually) beneficial to the union. Because they are caused in us, they cannot be switched off by fiat; because the soul can act, they can be managed — indirectly, by redirecting attention, and durably, by retraining the associations between bodily states and thoughts. The resulting ethics is a program of self-mastery culminating in générosité: the settled, legitimate self-esteem of one who knows that nothing truly belongs to her but the free use of her will, and who resolves to use it well — at once the remedy for disordered passion and the basis of regard for others. The work is the corrective to the caricature Descartes: the philosopher of disembodied intellect spent his final book on embodiment, emotion, and their cultivation, and modern affective science's picture of emotions as embodied appraisals serving action is closer to the Passions than its authors usually acknowledge.

Influence and reception

The immediate reception was combative and formative. Cartesianism spread through the Dutch and French universities against theological resistance (his works went on the Index in 1663), and the great rationalist systems are reworkings of his: Spinoza began as his expositor and collapsed the two substances into one; Malebranche turned the interaction problem into occasionalism — God as the only true cause; Leibniz, rejecting the physics and the interactionism alike, offered pre-established harmony. The empiricists defined themselves against the innate ideas and the armchair physics, which is how the rationalist–empiricist frame — a nineteenth-century textbook construction more than a seventeenth-century reality, but too useful to die — made him "founder of rationalism" by convention.

The "father of modern philosophy" title has attracted a standing scholarly correction: Gilson and, later, Ariew and Secada documented how much Descartes owes to scholasticism — the vocabulary of substance, the doctrine of God's concurrence, arguments with close ancestors in Suárez — and how carefully the Meditations is modeled on the genre of religious meditative exercises. On this view the revolution was real but the materials were inherited; the discontinuity is partly retrospective, manufactured by a tradition that needed a founding.

His deepest influence, arguably, is as the figure modern philosophy defines itself against. Ryle's The Concept of Mind (1949) made "Descartes' myth" — the ghost in the machine, the mind as a private inner theater — the official error of the tradition; behaviorism, functionalism, and eliminativism all position themselves as exits from Cartesian dualism. Embodied and extended approaches to cognition target the picture of mind as an inner arena only contingently housed in a body; externalism about mental content targets the assumption that the mind's contents are self-intimating and world-independent; Damasio's Descartes' Error made him the named antagonist of affective neuroscience — ironically, given the Passions. Feminist historians (Bordo, Lloyd) read the ideal of pure detached reason as gendered; Heidegger traced modern "subjectivism" to the cogito. That so many mutually incompatible programs need him as their foil is the surest measure of how completely he framed the problems.

Reading path

Start with the Discourse on the Method, Parts I–IV — short, autobiographical, the gentlest entry to the doubt and the cogito. Then the Meditations, one meditation at a time, followed by selections from the Objections and Replies — at minimum Hobbes (Third), Arnauld (Fourth), and Gassendi (Fifth), where the circle and the real distinction meet their first and best critics. Then the Elisabeth correspondence (Shapiro's edition), which belongs with the Meditations rather than after it, and the Passions of the Soul; the Principles and The World are for readers pursuing the physics. Secondary: Cottingham's Descartes as the accessible introduction; Bernard Williams' Descartes: The Project of Pure Enquiry as the philosophical classic; Garber's Descartes' Metaphysical Physics for the science; Gaukroger's biography for the life. The Cottingham–Stoothoff–Murdoch(–Kenny) translation (CSM/CSMK) is standard.

Assessment

Editorially: Descartes is the clearest case in the canon of a philosopher whose questions outlived his answers. The circle is probably unbreakable, the real distinction unproved, the physics dead; yet the demand behind them all — that knowledge be something a single mind can audit from the inside — created modern epistemology, and the interaction problem Elisabeth forced him to face is still the mind–body problem, renamed. He is also the canon's best argument for reading correspondence: the Meditations shows Descartes at his most confident, the letters to Elisabeth at his most honest, and the second is where the live philosophy is.
