Nāgārjuna

◑ survey  ·  standard  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-09-09.15

c. 2nd–3rd century CE · Indian Buddhist philosopher; founder of the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school of Mahāyāna Buddhism

Why they matter

Nāgārjuna is the most consequential Buddhist philosopher after the Buddha, and the reason is a single reversal of direction. The Abhidharma schools had analyzed the world down to its ultimate constituents — the dharmas, momentary event-particulars that alone exist in their own right. Nāgārjuna argued that the analysis had stopped one step short: whatever arises dependently is empty (śūnya) of intrinsic nature (svabhāva), and that includes the dharmas. Emptiness, on his account, is not a denial of dependent origination but its meaning — nothing exists from its own side, and that is exactly what allows things to arise, change, and cease. Everything that followed in Mahāyāna philosophy elaborates this claim, defends it against the charge of nihilism, or tries to state it without falling into the trap it sets for any statement. His methods — the four-cornered analysis, refutation by unwanted consequence, the declaration that he holds no thesis — made him the tradition's model of how philosophy can dismantle metaphysics without proposing a rival one, and the question of what such philosophy positively says has kept his readers arguing for eighteen centuries.

Life

Almost nothing is known. The traditions agree that Nāgārjuna was a South Indian Brahmin who converted, flourished under a Sātavāhana king, and was connected with the Andhra coast; modern scholarship places him roughly between 150 and 250 CE. Everything more specific is hagiography: Kumārajīva's fifth-century biography and the Tibetan histories have him recovering the Perfection of Wisdom sūtras from the undersea realm of the nāgas (hence the name) and living for centuries — legends that secure his rank as the "second Buddha" of the Mahāyāna and tell us nothing checkable. A tantric author, an alchemist, and a physician later shared the name, and their works were absorbed into the single figure. The philosopher of this article is the author of the analytic corpus below, whoever he was.

Works

  • Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way, MMK) — twenty-seven chapters, some 450 verses, each examining a category (conditions, motion, self, the Tathāgata, nirvāṇa) and showing it cannot be what it would have to be if it had intrinsic nature. The foundational text of Madhyamaka.
  • Vigrahavyāvartanī (Dispeller of Disputes) — seventy verses with autocommentary, answering an objector who charges that emptiness is self-refuting and that a critic of the means of knowledge can establish nothing. The key text on method.
  • Śūnyatāsaptati, Yuktiṣaṣṭikā, Vaidalyaprakaraṇa — shorter treatises on emptiness, on reasoning, and against Nyāya's categories of debate. With the two above, the "reasoning corpus" the Tibetan tradition treats as securely authentic.
  • Ratnāvalī (Precious Garland) and Suhṛllekha (Letter to a Friend) — counsel to a king on ethics and the bodhisattva path; emptiness lived rather than argued.
  • Contested: the Akutobhayā, an early MMK commentary sometimes taken as his own; the vast Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa, preserved only in Chinese; hymns and tantric works.

Thought

Emptiness as the meaning of dependent origination

Nāgārjuna's target is precise. Abhidharma metaphysics grants that persons and chariots are conceptual constructions but holds that the dharmas beneath them exist ultimately, each bearing its own svabhāva — an intrinsic nature that is what it is independently of anything else, unfabricated, borrowed from nothing. Nāgārjuna argues that nothing answering that description could participate in dependent origination, and that dependent origination is the one thing a Buddhist cannot surrender. The MMK's first chapter opens the campaign: nothing arises from itself (it would already exist), from another (with no intrinsic connection, anything could arise from anything), from both, or from neither; a condition is a condition only relative to what it conditions, which does not yet exist. The second chapter dismantles motion: no moving on the path already traversed, none on the path not yet traversed, and no "being-traversed" locatable without a mover distinct from the moving. Chapter after chapter repeats the pattern — agent and action, fire and fuel, seer and seen, self and aggregates — each pair defined by mutual dependence, so that neither member has the self-standing nature that would make it ultimately real.

The positive doctrine is stated in the fifteenth and twenty-fourth chapters. Intrinsic nature would be unproduced and unchanging, so if anything had it nothing could arise or cease; conversely, whatever arises dependently must lack it. Emptiness is thus not a further fact behind dependent origination but the same fact in the mode of negation: to be empty is to be dependently originated, and conversely. This is the "middle way" of the school's name — between eternalism, which posits enduring intrinsic natures, and annihilationism, which holds that things do not exist at all. Both extremes share the presupposition that to exist is to exist intrinsically; one affirms it, the other despairs of it. In the encyclopedia's own terms the position is a thoroughgoing anti-foundationalism: no fundamental level, no ungrounded ground, dependence without terminus in every direction.

The two truths

The doctrine that makes emptiness livable is the distinction between two truths. Conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya; the term also connotes concealment) is the domain of ordinary assertion: chariots carry people, fire burns, actions have consequences. Ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya) is what is found when the conventional is analyzed for intrinsic nature: nothing. Nāgārjuna insists on both: the ultimate cannot be taught without the conventional, and without the ultimate nirvāṇa is not attained. What the distinction is not is a two-tier ontology. The Abhidharma had also used two truths, with the dharmas as the real substrate beneath conventional fictions; Nāgārjuna keeps the vocabulary and removes the substrate. The ultimate truth about anything is that it lacks an ultimate nature — that there is nothing over and above the conventional for the conventional to conceal. Hence the twenty-fifth chapter's famous consequence: not the slightest difference between saṃsāra and nirvāṇa, since neither is a place or state with a nature of its own. Interpretation divides between readings on which the ultimate is an ineffable reality beyond conceptuality and readings on which the ultimate truth is a truth about the conventional — that it is all there is — with no further object. The deflationary reading dominates contemporary exegesis; the other has deep roots among the tradition's own commentators.

The catuṣkoṭi and refutation by consequence

Two instruments run through the corpus, to be understood as Indian dialectical practice before any Western logic is laid over them. The catuṣkoṭi, or four-cornered analysis, is a schema for exhausting the positions available on a question: a thing is F, is not F, is both, is neither. It predates Nāgārjuna; the Buddha is said to have declined all four answers to whether an enlightened one exists after death. Nāgārjuna's use is overwhelmingly negative — on nirvāṇa, the Tathāgata, the self, all four corners are rejected — and it is intelligible on one premise, which the text supplies: each corner is a claim made with the presupposition of intrinsic nature. To deny that a thing intrinsically exists and to deny that it intrinsically does not exist is no contradiction, because the denials remove a shared presupposition rather than affirming incompatible predicates. The commentators later fixed this as a distinction between non-implicative (prasajya) negation, which merely rejects, and implicative (paryudāsa) negation, which rejects by asserting the complement. Nāgārjuna's are of the first kind: rejecting all four corners commits him to no fifth.

The second instrument is prasaṅga, refutation by drawing out an unwanted consequence. Nāgārjuna does not, in the main, argue for emptiness from premises of his own; he takes the opponent's commitment to intrinsic natures and shows it entails what the opponent must deny — that nothing changes, that the path cannot be walked. The argument leaves its author with nothing to defend. The two instruments belong together: the catuṣkoṭi maps the space of svabhāva-committed positions, and prasaṅga clears every cell of it. Whether what remains is a position, a method, a therapy, or a silence is the question the tradition has never settled — and the reason the school later split over whether a Mādhyamika may use ordinary inference at all.

The nihilism charge and the emptiness of emptiness

The twenty-fourth chapter stages the objection that has followed the school through its history. If everything is empty, the objector says, nothing arises or ceases; the four noble truths, the path, and the Buddha himself are abolished; emptiness is the ruin of Buddhism. Nāgārjuna turns the charge around. It is the theorist of intrinsic natures who cannot accommodate the four truths: if suffering had intrinsic nature it could neither have arisen nor be ended; if the path existed from its own side it could not be cultivated; if the Buddha had a nature he could not have become one. Only empty things can be produced, transformed, and extinguished; emptiness is not the negation of the conventional world but the condition of its working.

The deeper move is that emptiness is itself empty. It is not a property things possess intrinsically, nor a hidden essence that survives the analysis; it is, in Nāgārjuna's phrase, a dependent designation, as conventional as anything it describes. The thirteenth chapter warns that those who make emptiness into a view are incurable: it was taught as the relinquishment of all views, and to hold it as one more reinstates the reification it was meant to cure. This is what separates Madhyamaka from nihilism. The nihilist holds a thesis — that nothing exists — which is one more claim about how things ultimately are. Nāgārjuna's claim is that there is no way things ultimately are, and that this claim, too, is empty. The cost is real: a doctrine that refuses to be a doctrine invites the reply that it says nothing, and the Vigrahavyāvartanī exists because the reply was made.

The Vigrahavyāvartanī: can the critic of the pramāṇas have a thesis?

The opponent of the Vigrahavyāvartanī — a Naiyāyika in all but name — presses two charges. First, self-refutation: if all things are empty, the statement "all things are empty" is empty, and an empty statement can negate nothing; if it is not empty, the thesis is false by its own counterexample. Second, the means of knowledge: if Nāgārjuna denies that the pramāṇas establish their objects, by what means does he establish his denial? To the first Nāgārjuna replies that emptiness does not deprive things of their function: an empty statement still communicates, as a conjured person may prevent another conjured person's deed. Then, famously, he declares that he has no thesis (pratijñā): had he one, the fault would attach to it; since he has none, there is no fault. To the second he turns the regress. If the pramāṇas establish their objects, what establishes the pramāṇas? If nothing, the demand for establishment is abandoned; if other pramāṇas, there is a regress; if they establish themselves, the opponent's lamp analogy fails, since a lamp does not illuminate itself in any sense in which it dispels darkness; and if the objects establish the pramāṇas, the relation is circular and neither side established.

What "I have no thesis" means is the interpretive crux of the whole corpus. On the strongest reading, Nāgārjuna asserts nothing at all — his sentences are instruments for removing the opponent's convictions, without truth-value of their own. On the weaker and now more common reading, he has no thesis of the kind the opponent requires: none backed by intrinsic natures and established by foundational means of knowledge. He can assert, conventionally, that things are empty, as one asserts anything — the only kind of assertion anyone ever makes. Read this way the text is an early and radical argument that the epistemic regress has no foundationalist stopping point: the pramāṇas and their objects are established together, if at all, and mutual establishment is what establishment is.

Influence and reception

Āryadeva extended the method in the Catuḥśataka, and by the sixth century the MMK commentaries had produced the school's defining quarrel. Buddhapālita read Nāgārjuna as a pure practitioner of prasaṅga: every argument exposes a consequence the opponent must reject, and the Mādhyamika asserts nothing on his own account. Bhāviveka objected that this is not argument: a refutation must be cast as a formal inference of the kind Dignāga had just codified, and the Mādhyamika owes independent (svatantra) inferences on grounds both parties accept. Candrakīrti, in the Prasannapadā and Madhyamakāvatāra, defended Buddhapālita on principle: there are no grounds both parties accept, since the opponent's presuppose intrinsic natures. Prāsaṅgika and Svātantrika are Tibetan labels for this division, but the division is real: it concerns how far the emptiness of emptiness reaches into the logic of one's own speech.

Push-back from outside was immediate: the Nyāyasūtra itself answers arguments recognizably Nāgārjuna's about the pramāṇa regress, and the two schools argued the foundations of knowledge for a millennium; Śaṅkara declined to argue, dismissing the Mādhyamika as a nihilist unworthy of refutation.

In Tibet Candrakīrti's reading became orthodoxy, but an orthodoxy at war with itself. Tsongkhapa's Geluk Prāsaṅgika made emptiness compatible with a robust conventional world: the object of refutation is precisely and only intrinsic nature, the negation is non-implicative, and conventional existence — causation, ethics, the path — stands untouched by the analysis. The Sakya master Gorampa answered that this domesticates Nāgārjuna: the ultimate is freedom from all four corners, beyond conceptual elaboration altogether. That quarrel, still conducted in monastic debate, is the sharpest available commentary on the corpus. In China Kumārajīva's translations founded the Sanlun ("Three Treatise") school, whose master Jizang made the two truths a ladder of successively emptied dichotomies; it passed to Japan as Sanron. Tiantai's Zhiyi drew from a single MMK verse the three truths — empty, provisional, middle — that organize his system.

Modern reception has been a history of mirrors: Stcherbatsky's Kantian, Murti's absolutist, Kalupahana's empiricist, the Wittgensteinian and deconstructionist Nāgārjunas of later decades. The current philosophical reading, associated with Jay Garfield and Mark Siderits, is deflationary and anti-foundationalist: emptiness rejects any ultimate nature, including a nature of ultimate reality; the two truths are two ways of taking one world; and the catuṣkoṭi respects classical logic once the presupposition of intrinsic nature is made explicit. Against it stands Graham Priest's dialetheist reading, developed with Garfield, on which the ultimate truth that there is no ultimate truth is a genuine contradiction Nāgārjuna embraces, and rejecting all four corners requires a fifth, ineffable value best modeled in a paraconsistent many-valued logic. This is contested on philological and philosophical grounds alike: Tom Tillemans argues that Mādhyamikas reason with an ordinary commitment to consistency and that the apparent contradictions dissolve under the two-negation analysis; Siderits holds that presupposition failure leaves nothing for a dialetheist to be right about. The register's warning applies: the comparison illuminates the structure of the limit-contradictions, if they are such, and flattens Nāgārjuna into a precursor if taken as more.

Reading path

Begin with the MMK in the Siderits and Katsura translation (2013), from the Sanskrit with commentary keyed to the Indian commentators; Garfield's The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way (1995), from the Tibetan, is the alternative and the more widely read. Take chapters 1, 2, 15, 18, 24, and 25 first. Then the Vigrahavyāvartanī in Westerhoff's translation and commentary (2010): short, and where the method explains itself. For the commentators, Sprung's partial Prasannapadā and Tsongkhapa's Ocean of Reasoning (Garfield and Ngawang Samten). Secondary: Westerhoff's Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka (2009) is the standard analytic study; the Cowherds' Moonshadows (2011) is the state of the debate on conventional truth. For the dialetheist reading and its critics, read Garfield and Priest's "Nāgārjuna and the Limits of Thought" alongside Tillemans's "How Do Mādhyamikas Think?"

Assessment

Editorially: Nāgārjuna is the purest case in this encyclopedia of a philosopher whose position is a method; the question "what does he actually hold?" resists answer by design, since the Vigrahavyāvartanī is built to make it misfire. The deflationary reading is the best available, because it lets the corpus be argument rather than gesture, but it attributes to Nāgārjuna a doctrine of presupposition he never states. The safest verdict is the tradition's own: the quarrel between Tsongkhapa and Gorampa is not a failure of interpretation but the doctrine working as intended.
