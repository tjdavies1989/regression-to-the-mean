Aristotle

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-21.1

384–322 BCE. Classical Greek philosopher; student of Plato, founder of the Lyceum and of the Peripatetic tradition.

Why they matter

Aristotle is the figure this encyclopedia's tree cites most often because he did not merely contribute to philosophy's divisions — he drew many of the divisions themselves. Logic as a formal discipline begins with him and no one earlier; his syllogistic remained the working theory of inference for over two thousand years. Metaphysics as the study of being, ethics organized around character and flourishing, political science as the comparative study of constitutions, systematic biology, literary theory: each exists as a distinct subject partly because Aristotle wrote the treatise that made it one. Where Plato pulls philosophy toward a single vision, Aristotle divides, sorts, and asks of each domain what precision it can bear. For long stretches of Islamic and Latin intellectual history he was cited simply as "the Philosopher," and much of modernity — mechanistic science, Fregean logic, Kantian ethics — defined itself by the specific ways it broke with him.

Life

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE at Stagira in northern Greece; his father was physician to the Macedonian court, a plausible source of his lifelong biological bent. At seventeen he entered Plato's Academy in Athens and remained twenty years, until Plato's death in 347. He then worked in Asia Minor and on Lesbos — the period of his most intensive zoological research — and around 343 tutored the young Alexander at the Macedonian court. Returning to Athens in 335, he founded his own school, the Lyceum, whose members were called Peripatetics. When Alexander died in 323 and anti-Macedonian feeling surged, Aristotle faced an impiety charge and withdrew to Chalcis, where he died in 322. The works we possess are not his polished publications, which are lost, but lecture texts and drafts edited into corpus form centuries later: dense, elliptical, and occasionally at odds with one another — one reason his interpretation remains a live scholarly industry.

Works

  • The Organon — the six logical treatises: Categories (kinds of predication; the primacy of substance), De Interpretatione (assertion, truth, future contingents), Prior Analytics (the formal syllogistic), Posterior Analytics (demonstration and scientific knowledge), Topics (dialectical argument), Sophistical Refutations (the first taxonomy of fallacies).
  • Physics — nature, change, the four causes, place, time, and the unmoved mover.
  • Metaphysics — being qua being, substance, form, actuality, the divine intellect; its accidental title named the discipline.
  • De Anima — the soul as form and first actuality of a living body; perception and intellect.
  • The biological works (History, Parts, and Generation of Animals) — the corpus's largest portion: teleological, comparative life science.
  • Nicomachean Ethics — flourishing, virtue of character, practical wisdom, friendship, and the contemplative life. (The Eudemian Ethics covers parallel ground.)
  • Politics — the polis, citizenship, and the pathology of constitutions.
  • Rhetoric — persuasion through argument, character, and emotion.
  • Poetics — tragedy, plot, and katharsis; the founding text of Western aesthetics.

Thought

The Organon: logic invented, and completed within its fragment

Aristotle's most singular achievement is that formal logic has a definite historical beginning, and it is the Prior Analytics. Others before him argued and even reflected on argument; Aristotle was the first to study validity as a matter of form. His device was the schematic letter — "if A belongs to every B, and B to every C, then A belongs to every C" — which detaches an inference from its subject matter and lets one ask, in full generality, which patterns guarantee their conclusions. The variable, the formal schema, and validity in virtue of form all enter the human record here.

The system built with these tools is the syllogistic: a theory of inferences among four sentence forms (every B is A; no B is A; some B is A; some B is not A) combined through a shared middle term. Aristotle organizes the valid patterns into figures, derives the rest from a privileged "perfect" first figure, and — remarkably — establishes the invalidity of the remaining combinations by the method still standard today: producing counterexamples, triples of terms that make the premises true and the conclusion false. He thus practices, avant la lettre, both proof theory and a rudimentary model theory. Twentieth-century reconstruction (Łukasiewicz reading the syllogistic as an axiomatic theory, Corcoran and Smiley as a natural-deduction system) established a striking result: within its own fragment of logic, the syllogistic is sound and complete — every valid argument expressible in its forms is derivable. Its famous two-millennium reign — Kant judged in 1787 that logic had not advanced a step since Aristotle and seemed complete — was therefore not mere ancestor-worship; the theory really was finished, as far as it went. Its limits were equally real: it cannot represent relations ("every horse is an animal, so every head of a horse is a head of an animal" lies beyond it) or multiple generality — precisely the gaps Frege's quantifier-variable notation opened in 1879.

Around the formal core stand the rest of the Organon's bequests: the Posterior Analytics' picture of a science as truths demonstrated from first principles — the ancestor of the axiomatic ideal, with its regress argument that demonstration must terminate in indemonstrable starting points; the Topics' theory of dialectic, which informal logic and argumentation theory still claim as their charter; the Sophistical Refutations' catalogue of fallacies; and De Interpretatione's sea-battle argument about tomorrow's contingents, fountainhead of debates over future truth, determinism, and temporal and many-valued logics.

Substance, form, and matter

The Categories asks what is most fundamentally real and answers: primary substances — this man, this horse — the individual subjects of which everything else (qualities, quantities, relations) is predicated. This deliberately inverts Plato: the particular concrete thing, not the separated universal, is ontologically basic; universals are real but exist only as instantiated. The Metaphysics deepens the picture through hylomorphism, the analysis of ordinary things as compounds of matter (hylē) and form (morphē). A thing's form is not its shape but its organizing principle — what makes this flesh and bone a human being — and the central books argue that form has the best claim to be substance in the primary sense, as what makes a thing definable, unified, and what it is. Hylomorphism also answers the Presocratic puzzles of change: not something coming from nothing, but a persisting substratum exchanging one form for another. The framework remains a live research program: contemporary hylomorphists deploy it against both reductive atomism and Platonism in debates over material constitution, persistence, and natural kinds.

The four causes and natural teleology

To understand a thing scientifically, the Physics argues, is to grasp its four "causes" — better, four kinds of explanatory factor: the material (what it is made of), the formal (what it is to be it), the efficient (what brought it about), and the final (what it is for). The doctrine is a thesis about explanation before it is one about metaphysics: explanation is irreducibly plural, and a science giving only the material and efficient story has not finished its work. The contested element is the final cause. Aristotle holds that natural things — above all organisms — have ends immanent in their natures, without any designing mind: sharp teeth are for cutting, the embryo develops toward the mature form. The mechanistic seventeenth century made rejecting final causes its manifesto, and Darwin is often said to have finished the job; yet philosophy of biology has found teleological notions — function, adaptation, what a trait is selected for — impossible to purge and has instead sought to domesticate them, a debate in which Aristotle remains the reference point.

Actuality and potentiality

Cutting across the form–matter analysis is the distinction between potentiality (dunamis) and actuality (energeia): the bronze is potentially a statue; the sleeping geometer potentially theorizes; matter stands to form as potentiality to actuality. The distinction lets Aristotle define change (the actuality of the potential as potential), rebut Megarian arguments that only the actual is possible, and articulate the priority of the actual: potentialities are understood through the actualities they are for, and the universe requires at its explanatory summit a being of pure actuality — the unmoved mover of Metaphysics XII, a divine intellect thinking itself, moving the heavens as an object of desire moves a lover. Here begins philosophical theology, and here too a modal metaphysics grounded in the powers of things, revived in contemporary dispositional theories of modality against the possible-worlds orthodoxy.

Soul as the form of the body

De Anima defines the soul as the first actuality of a natural body having life potentially — the form of a living body, the organized set of capacities (nutrition, perception, desire, locomotion, intellect) that make it alive. The position splits the difference between materialism, which reduces soul to bodily stuff, and Platonic dualism, which makes it a separable substance: the soul is no more separable from the body than sight from the eye, yet not identical with the matter it informs. Perception is the reception of form without matter; the status of intellect (nous), which Aristotle cryptically suggests may alone be separable, generated a millennium of controversy. Modern functionalists claim Aristotle as an ancestor — mind as organization, multiply realizable in matter — while others read hylomorphism as a third option that dissolves rather than answers the Cartesian mind–body problem; both readings keep De Anima on the syllabus of current philosophy of mind.

Ethics: virtue, practical wisdom, and flourishing

The Nicomachean Ethics begins not with duty or law but with a question about a life: what is the good for a human being? Its answer — eudaimonia, flourishing — is defended by the function argument: a thing's good lies in the excellent exercise of its characteristic activity, and the characteristic activity of humans is rational activity; hence the human good is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, in a complete life. Virtues of character (courage, temperance, justice, generosity) are stable dispositions acquired by habituation, each a mean between excess and deficiency fixed not by rule but by the judgment of the person of practical wisdom (phronēsis) — a virtue that cannot be codified, because ethical truth holds only "for the most part" and situations outrun any rulebook. Feeling matters as much as action: the virtuous person desires and enjoys the right things, which is why moral education is training of emotion, not merely behavior. The theory embeds accounts of voluntariness, weakness of will (akrasia), friendship, and a closing argument for contemplation as the highest happiness whose tension with the political life is still debated. When Anscombe in 1958 pronounced modern moral philosophy bankrupt and pointed back to Aristotle, she launched the revival that made virtue ethics the third major position in normative theory; his insistence on the uncodifiability of ethical judgment likewise anchors contemporary particularism.

The Politics: the polis and its constitutions

Ethics flows into politics because, for Aristotle, the human being is by nature a political animal: the capacities for speech and justice mature only in a polis, and the city exists not for mere life but for the good life. The Politics combines this naturalism with an empirical program — his school collected over 150 constitutional histories — yielding the classic sixfold typology of constitutions (rule by one, few, or many, each in a correct and a deviant form) and a proto-science of revolution and stability. His distributive principle is proportional: political justice assigns office by relevant merit, and rival constitutions err by absolutizing one partial claim (wealth, freedom, birth). He offers an early epistemic argument for collective judgment — the many, pooling perspectives, may judge better than the excellent few — while notoriously defending natural slavery and the exclusion of women from rule, positions his own principles have been turned against ever since. The Politics remains a founding text for communitarian critiques of liberal neutrality, republican citizenship, and the capabilities approach to justice.

The Poetics: tragedy and katharsis

Against Plato's expulsion of the poets, the Poetics treats imitation (mimēsis) as natural, cognitively valuable, and analyzable. Tragedy is the imitation of a serious and complete action, achieving through pity and fear the katharsis of such emotions — a famously compressed clause whose interpretation (purgation, purification, clarification) is its own literature. Aristotle ranks plot above character, dissects reversal and recognition, and argues that poetry is "more philosophical" than history because it presents universals — what a certain kind of person would probably or necessarily do. The treatise founded genre theory, ruled neoclassical criticism for centuries, and still frames debates over why audiences seek painful art and what fiction teaches.

Influence and reception

No philosopher's afterlife is longer or stranger. The Lyceum produced Theophrastus and, in the first centuries CE, the great commentators (Alexander of Aphrodisias, later the Neoplatonists), who transmitted an Aristotle already partially harmonized with Plato. The decisive medieval channel ran through the Islamic world: the falsafa tradition — al-Fārābī, Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna), Ibn Rushd (Averroes) — translated, systematized, and transformed the corpus, Avicenna recasting its metaphysics around essence and existence, Averroes earning the title of "the Commentator"; al-Ghazālī's attack on the philosophers shows the reception was contested from within. Latin Europe recovered Aristotle largely through these Arabic intermediaries in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries; after initial condemnations, Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas built the scholastic synthesis on him, and Aristotelian logic, physics, and psychology became Europe's university curriculum for four centuries. The scientific revolution defined itself against that curriculum: Galileo and Descartes made "Aristotelian" a term of abuse, mechanism expelled final causes, empiricism mocked substantial forms. Kant still took the syllogistic to be logic entire; Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) ended its reign by subsuming it within a vastly more expressive system. Yet the twentieth century staged multiple returns: Łukasiewicz and Corcoran vindicated the syllogistic's rigor; Anscombe, Foot, and MacIntyre rebuilt ethics on his foundations; Nussbaum and Sen drew the capabilities approach from his account of flourishing; hylomorphism and powers-based metaphysics re-entered analytic debate. The pushback matters as much as the inheritance: much of modernity is intelligible precisely as anti-Aristotelianism.

Reading path

Start with the Nicomachean Ethics (Books I–III and VI), the most self-standing and immediately rewarding entry point. Then Physics II — the four causes and teleology in one compact book — followed by the Categories and De Anima II.1–3 for substance and soul. For the logic, read Prior Analytics I.1–7 with a modern guide (Smith's or Striker's commentary), and Posterior Analytics I.1–3 for the theory of science. Continue with Metaphysics I, VII (hard but central), and XII; Politics I and III; and the Poetics entire, which is short. A newcomer should not read the corpus cold: Ackrill's Aristotle the Philosopher, Barnes's very short introduction, or Shields's survey supply the scaffolding the lecture texts assume. The Revised Oxford Translation (ed. Barnes) is the standard English corpus.

Assessment

Editorially: Aristotle is the best single argument that philosophy makes progress, and the best that it does not. The syllogistic was genuinely superseded — Frege's logic proves more, provably — yet the Ethics has not been superseded but only rejoined, and hylomorphism returns whenever reductionism overreaches. His deepest legacy may be methodological: begin from the appearances and the reputable opinions, honor the precision each subject admits and no more, and trust the world's articulations, patiently traced, to show where philosophy's divisions belong.
