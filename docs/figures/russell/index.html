Bertrand Russell

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-09-09.15

1872–1970 · British logician, philosopher, and public moralist; with Frege and Moore a founder of the analytic tradition, and its first exemplar of philosophy as logical analysis

Why they matter

Russell is the figure through whom Frege's logic became a philosophical method. Frege built quantificational logic to ground arithmetic; Russell found the contradiction in that grounding, rebuilt the foundations in Principia Mathematica, and then turned the instrument outward, arguing that a sentence's grammatical form systematically misleads about its logical form and that many classical metaphysical puzzles are artifacts of the mismatch. The theory of descriptions was the demonstration piece — Ramsey called it a paradigm of philosophy — and its habit of trading inferred entities for logical constructions ran through everything Russell later wrote on perception, matter, and mind. He was also one of the century's public consciences: jailed twice, dismissed from Cambridge, barred from a New York chair, and a Nobel laureate in literature, all for holding in public the view of belief and evidence that his epistemology held in private.

Life

Born in 1872 into the Whig aristocracy — his grandfather Lord John Russell had twice been prime minister — Russell was orphaned by four and raised at Pembroke Lodge by a devout, formidable grandmother. He read mathematics and then moral sciences at Trinity College, Cambridge, joined Moore's revolt against British idealism, and won a fellowship in 1895. The turning point was the Paris congress of 1900, where he met Peano and grasped what symbolic logic could do; within a year he had found his paradox, within three had published The Principles of Mathematics. The decade of Principia (with Whitehead, 1910–13) nearly broke him; Wittgenstein's arrival in 1911 finished the job.

The First World War made him a public figure: he organized for the No-Conscription Fellowship, lost his Trinity lectureship in 1916, and in 1918 served six months in Brixton for an article on the war, writing Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy in his cell. In 1940 a New York court annulled his appointment at City College as morally unfit. He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950. His last two decades were given to the nuclear question — the Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955), the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, the Committee of 100's civil disobedience, and a second week in Brixton in 1961, at eighty-nine. He died in Wales in 1970.

Works

  • The Principles of Mathematics (1903) — logicism announced for all of mathematics; the paradox confessed in an appendix.
  • "On Denoting" (1905) — the theory of descriptions; the most influential twenty pages in the philosophy of language.
  • Principia Mathematica (with Whitehead, 1910–13) — ramified types, the no-class theory, mathematics from logic plus three contested axioms.
  • The Problems of Philosophy (1912) — acquaintance and description, sense-data, induction, universals, in prose anyone can read.
  • Our Knowledge of the External World (1914) and "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism" (1918) — facts, simples, and the method of logical construction.
  • Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy (1919) — Principia without symbols.
  • The Analysis of Mind (1921) and The Analysis of Matter (1927) — neutral monism and structuralism about physics.
  • Human Knowledge: Its Scope and Limits (1948) — the late epistemology.
  • Why I Am Not a Christian (1927/1957), A History of Western Philosophy (1945), the Autobiography (1967–69) — the public Russell.

Thought

The theory of descriptions: the paradigm of philosophy

The problem Russell inherited was ontological inflation. If every grammatical subject stands for something — and the Principles had held that whatever can be mentioned has being — then "the golden mountain" names an entity of some diminished sort, as Meinong maintained. "On Denoting" dissolves the commitment by denying that definite descriptions are names at all. "The F is G" is analyzed as a quantified claim: there is at least one F, at most one F, and whatever is F is G. The description vanishes under analysis; it is an incomplete symbol, meaningful only in the sentences it occurs in. Three puzzles are solved at once. "The present King of France is bald" is false rather than truth-valueless, and its negation ambiguous between a primary and a secondary occurrence of the description — the scope distinction that now runs through all of formal semantics. George IV could wonder whether Scott was the author of Waverley without wondering whether Scott was Scott. And "the golden mountain does not exist" is true without a golden mountain to be true of. Russell took Frege's sense–reference distinction to be an unnecessary detour: quantificational structure alone handles informativeness and empty terms, and the theory remains Frege's standing rival in the theory of reference.

Ramsey's phrase "paradigm of philosophy" named what the theory taught by example: that surface grammar hides logical form, that a metaphysical puzzle can be a symptom of misreading it, and that analysis is philosophy's characteristic act. The paradigm has costs. Strawson objected in 1950 that a description presupposes rather than asserts its object's existence, so the King of France sentence lacks a truth-value; Donnellan distinguished referential from attributive uses. Kripke defended Russell against both by relocating their phenomena in pragmatics, and generalized quantifier theory absorbed descriptions as one determiner among many — a vindication in form, if not in ontological ambition.

Logicism, the paradox, and the theory of types

Russell's logicism was more ambitious than Frege's — all of pure mathematics was to be derived from logic — and it was Russell who broke it. In 1901 he asked whether the class of all classes not members of themselves is a member of itself, and found that it is if and only if it is not. His letter to Frege of June 1902 showed that Basic Law V licenses exactly this class. Russell's cure was the theory of types, motivated by Poincaré's vicious-circle principle: nothing may be defined in terms of a totality to which it would belong. Propositional functions are stratified so that a function takes as argument only entities of the type below it, and the paradoxical class is not false but ungrammatical. Classes themselves are eliminated as logical fictions — the no-class theory is the theory of descriptions applied to mathematics.

The cost is the story of Principia. Because the principle stratifies not only types but orders within a type — the ramified hierarchy — the definitions real analysis needs, above all the least upper bound, become inexpressible. Russell's remedy, the axiom of reducibility, asserts that every higher-order function has an equivalent at the lowest order; it does the work, but nobody, Russell included, could see it as a truth of logic. The axiom of infinity and the multiplicative axiom (choice) compounded the embarrassment. Logicism thus survived in hedged form — mathematics follows from logic plus hypotheses logic cannot certify — and Russell candidly stated the theorems as conditionals. Ramsey showed in 1925 that the simple theory of types suffices once the semantic paradoxes are separated from the set-theoretic ones; Gödel showed in 1931 that no system of Principia's kind can be both complete and consistent. Zermelo's set theory, restricting comprehension rather than stratifying it, won the working mathematician.

Logical atomism

The 1918 lectures give the metaphysics the method presupposed. The world consists of facts, not things: an atomic fact is a particular's having a quality or particulars' standing in a relation, and an atomic proposition — in a logically perfect language mirroring the world's structure — is true just if such a fact obtains. Molecular propositions are truth-functions of atomic ones. Russell, unlike the Wittgenstein of the Tractatus, argued that negative and general facts must be admitted too, since no collection of atomic facts entails that there are no others. Analysis terminates in simples, known by acquaintance, and the supreme maxim of scientific philosophizing — replace inferred entities with logical constructions out of what is given — is the program's engine: numbers, classes, instants, and physical objects are reconstructed rather than posited. Russell's atomism was empiricist, an inventory of what we could know; Wittgenstein's was an a priori argument about what any language must presuppose. The first is refutable by finding that nothing in experience is simple; the second, Wittgenstein later concluded, by looking at how language actually works.

Knowledge by acquaintance and by description

The epistemology behind the atomism turns on one relation. Acquaintance is direct presentation of an object to a mind — of sense-data, universals, one's own mental states, and (Russell wavered) the self — and it is knowledge of things, not of truths. Everything else is knowledge by description: we know Bismarck only as whatever satisfies some description. The principle of acquaintance — every proposition we understand must be composed wholly of constituents we are acquainted with — links the two and makes ordinary proper names abbreviated descriptions. The Problems of Philosophy (1912) turns the picture into the century's most-read introduction to epistemology. Physical objects are inferred from sense-data as the simplest hypothesis, never a certainty. The chicken fed daily until the day its neck is wrung states the problem of induction with memorable savagery; Russell's tentative answer, that the inductive principle must be accepted on its intrinsic evidence or all science falls, prefigures the postulates of 1948. And the closing chapter's claim that philosophy's value lies in the uncertainty it enlarges and the impartiality it cultivates is the ethics of the public life stated as epistemology. Acquaintance was the sense-datum tradition's founding concept and the target of its great critics — Austin's phenomenology of ordinary perception and Sellars's Myth of the Given, on which nothing given without conceptual articulation can justify a belief; recent philosophy of perception has revived it under Russell's own name.

Neutral monism and the analysis of matter

The Analysis of Mind (1921) abandons the acquaintance relation, and with it the subject at one end of it. Following James, Russell now held that mind and matter are not two stuffs but two groupings of a single neutral material — events — organized by physical laws into the series we call objects and by mnemic causation into the series we call minds. Introspection reveals no ego; the self is a construction like the table. The Analysis of Matter (1927) extends the view to physics with a structuralist thesis: we know the intrinsic character of percepts, which are events in our own brains, but of the external world only its structure. Max Newman objected in 1928 that structure so construed is trivial — any collection of the right cardinality carries any structure whatever — and Russell conceded without repairing. Russellian monism in the current philosophy of consciousness — the intrinsic properties physics leaves unspecified are or ground the phenomenal — takes its name and central move from these books.

Bridges: mind.mind-body, mind.perception, metaphysics.ontology

The later epistemology: Human Knowledge (1948)

Russell's last major work asks how scientific knowledge is possible given that its content outruns any possible experience. Hume's problem is taken at full strength: no deductive argument reaches the unobserved, and probabilistic induction presupposes the uniformity it is meant to establish. Russell's answer is five postulates of scientific inference — quasi-permanence, separable causal lines, spatiotemporal continuity, common causal origin for similar structures, and analogy — which are neither self-evident nor provable nor known a priori, but are presupposed by every inference from percept to object and are, he argued, the animal's habits made explicit. The conclusion is deliberately uncomfortable: either we know something independently of experience, or strict empiricism cannot underwrite science. The reception was cold — the postwar profession had turned to the later Wittgenstein, and an epistemology anchored in animal inference looked like a relic — but it now reads as an early statement of the view that justification may rest on reliable dispositions the believer cannot vindicate from the inside.

Russell and Wittgenstein

The relation ran hard in both directions. Wittgenstein arrived from Frege in 1911 and within two years had convinced Russell that the multiple-relation theory of judgment — belief as a relation among a mind and the constituents of a would-be fact — could not explain why those constituents formed a possible proposition rather than a list. Russell abandoned the 1913 Theory of Knowledge manuscript, and his 1918 lectures open by crediting the ideas to his former pupil. Russell in turn gave Wittgenstein his problems, his logic, and his publisher: the introduction to the Tractatus (1922) secured its printing while, on Wittgenstein's view, misunderstanding it — Russell's proposal of a hierarchy of languages to escape the saying–showing doctrine was the theory of types re-applied. Thereafter the divergence was total: Russell judged the later philosophy an abandonment of serious thinking, and Wittgenstein held Russell's popular writing in contempt. The estrangement is not merely personal: the two fixed the tradition's poles — philosophy as a science continuous with logic and physics, and philosophy as therapy for the confusions such ambitions breed — and every analytic philosopher since has had to choose.

The public philosopher

Russell's politics were not a sideline but the same conviction differently applied: that belief should be proportioned to evidence, and that the refusal to do so — in war, religion, and sexual morality — is the root of most avoidable suffering. His First World War pacifism was consequentialist rather than absolute (he supported the war against Hitler) and rested on a judgment that the war's costs exceeded any attainable good. Why I Am Not a Christian and Marriage and Morals apply the evidential ethic to faith and to sex, and cost him the New York chair. The Nobel citation of 1950 named his championing of humanitarian ideals and freedom of thought — it rewarded the epistemology's public face. The nuclear campaigns of his last years, from the Manifesto with Einstein to the Committee of 100's sit-downs, took the same shape: a philosopher's insistence that the calculation be done in public. There is a tension he acknowledged. His metaethics moved from Moore's objectivism to a subjectivism on which moral judgments express desire, yet he confessed he could not believe his loathing of cruelty was only a preference. He never resolved the difficulty; he acted as though objectivism were true.

Influence and reception

Russell's influence is diffuse because it became the tradition's ambient method. Carnap's Aufbau (1928) is Our Knowledge of the External World carried out in detail, and the Vienna Circle took logical construction as its charter. Quine learned logic from Principia, used descriptions to eliminate names from ontology, and inherited the naturalism of Human Knowledge without acknowledging it. Ramsey simplified the types; Gödel closed the logicist road. The pushback was equally formative. Strawson's "On Referring" launched the ordinary-language critique of the whole analytic style. Kripke rescued Russell on descriptions while destroying the descriptivism about names that the principle of acquaintance had made compulsory. Recent scholarship — Hylton on the escape from idealism, Landini and Linsky on Principia — has restored the working philosopher behind the popularizer; Monk's biography restored the man, and did not flatter him.

Reading path

Begin with The Problems of Philosophy, which is short and presupposes nothing; then "On Denoting" (in Logic and Knowledge, ed. Marsh), read with Strawson's "On Referring" and Kripke's "Speaker's Reference and Semantic Reference". Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy gives the logicism and the types without symbols. "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism" (also in Marsh) is the metaphysics; read it against the Tractatus. Human Knowledge, Part VI, contains the postulates. For the public Russell, Why I Am Not a Christian and the Autobiography. Secondary: Hylton's Russell, Idealism, and the Emergence of Analytic Philosophy for the origins; Sainsbury's Russell for the doctrines; Monk's two-volume life for the man.

Assessment

Editorially: Russell's technical reputation rests on two achievements he did not rank highest, "On Denoting" and the paradox, while his own favorite, the epistemology of constructions, has aged worst. His genius was diagnostic — finding the contradiction, finding the misleading form — and diagnosis is what analytic philosophy was for; the systems he built from the results were provisional by design, and he abandoned them with a candor his critics rarely matched. The public life belongs in the same ledger: often wrong, sometimes badly, he submitted his errors to the standard he asked of everyone else, and the demand that belief answer to evidence outlasts every doctrine it produced.
