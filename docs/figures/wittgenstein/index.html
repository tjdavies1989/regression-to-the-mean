Ludwig Wittgenstein

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-21.2

1889–1951 · Austrian-British philosopher; author of two complete and opposed philosophies, each foundational for a school of the analytic tradition

Why they matter

Wittgenstein is the only modern figure who produced two finished philosophies, repudiated the first in building the second, and saw each become the charter of a movement. The Tractatus — the picture theory, logic as tautology, the boundary between saying and showing — was taken by the Vienna Circle as the founding document of logical empiricism. The Philosophical Investigations — meaning as use, language-games, rule-following, the private language argument — became the charter of ordinary-language philosophy and remains the most cited philosophical work of the twentieth century. Beneath both runs one conviction: philosophical problems are not discoveries awaiting solution but confusions bred by misunderstood language, and the aim of philosophy is not theory but clarity — a conception that indicts most of what philosophers do, including most of what has been done with him.

Life

Born in Vienna in 1889, youngest child of one of Europe's richest steel magnates, into high musical culture and catastrophe — three of his four brothers died by suicide. Aeronautical engineering at Manchester led him via the foundations of mathematics to Frege, who sent him to Russell at Cambridge in 1911. He enlisted in the Austro-Hungarian army in 1914, sought front-line postings, was decorated, and finished the Tractatus as a prisoner of war. Convinced it had solved philosophy's problems, he gave away his entire fortune — partly, anonymously, to poets including Rilke and Trakl — and left the subject: six unhappy years as a village schoolteacher, a monastery gardener's post, the design of his sister's Vienna house. Drawn back by Ramsey, Sraffa, and Brouwer's 1928 Vienna lecture, he returned to Cambridge in 1929 with the Tractatus as his doctoral thesis and spent two decades dismantling it, succeeding Moore in the chair in 1939 and serving as a hospital porter through the second war. He resigned the professorship in 1947 — he thought academic philosophy a kind of living death and urged his best students out of it — and died of cancer in Cambridge in 1951; his last words were that he had had a wonderful life. The biography is not ornament: for Wittgenstein philosophy was an ethical demand on the person, and the fortune renounced, the wars volunteered for, the chair resigned are that conception enacted.

Works

  • Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921) — the only book published in his lifetime: seven numbered propositions and their ramifying comments, ending in the injunction to silence.
  • The Blue and Brown Books (dictated 1933–35) — the middle period in transition: language-games introduced, the Tractatus under demolition.
  • Philosophical Investigations (posthumous, 1953) — the second philosophy: an album of remarks on meaning, rules, and sensation, in running argument with his earlier self.
  • Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics (1956) — rule-following applied to proof and necessity; the most contested part of the corpus.
  • On Certainty (1969) — the final notebooks: Moore's "I know I have hands" and the hinges on which doubt turns.

Thought

The Tractatus: picture theory and the limits of language

The Tractatus asks how language can represent the world at all, and answers with the picture theory: a proposition is a picture of a possible state of affairs. Names stand for objects; their arrangement mirrors a possible arrangement of objects; the proposition is true if that arrangement obtains. What picture and world must share — logical form — cannot itself be pictured. The world, correspondingly, is the totality of facts, not of things: atomic facts are configurations of absolutely simple objects. Here lies the real relation to Russell's logical atomism. Russell's atoms were epistemological — sense-data, where acquaintance bottoms out. Wittgenstein's simples are demanded a priori: if sense is to be determinate, analysis must terminate, so there must be simples — and he pointedly gives no example. His atomism is a requirement of logic, not an inventory of experience; the Tractatus contains no epistemology, and Wittgenstein thought Russell's admiring introduction had missed the point. The account of logic follows: the logical constants do not represent ("my fundamental idea"); complex propositions are truth-functions of elementary ones; and the propositions of logic are tautologies, true under every assignment, saying nothing. Logic's necessity is thereby demystified — not the deepest truths about reality, as Frege and Russell held, but the by-product of what any representing sign-system must be.

Saying, showing, and the ladder

What can be said is exactly what can be pictured: contingent facts — the propositions of natural science. Everything that gives life its significance falls outside: logical form shows itself but cannot be stated, and the same holds for ethics, aesthetics, the self as limit of the world, God. These are not dismissed — the book calls them the mystical, and Wittgenstein told a publisher its real point was the ethical part left unwritten. But the doctrine devours the book itself: its own propositions, being about logical form, are by its lights nonsense, and the penultimate remark instructs the reader to throw away the ladder after climbing it. What that means is the central scholarly dispute: the traditional reading takes the nonsense to be illuminating — gesturing at ineffable truths — while the "resolute" reading (Diamond, Conant) takes Wittgenstein at his word: nonsense is mere nonsense, and the book is a therapeutic exercise curing the reader of the illusion that its sentences meant anything — continuous, then, with the later therapy rather than its target. Either way, the final proposition — whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent — is at once a logical thesis and an ethical act: the refusal to chatter about what matters most.

Meaning as use, language-games, family resemblance

The Investigations opens with Augustine describing how he learned to speak — words name objects — and spends its length showing what this picture conceals. Language is not one thing but a motley of language-games: ordering, describing, joking, thanking, praying — activities woven into the "forms of life" of their players. For a large class of cases, the meaning of a word is its use: not an object it stands for, not an idea it evokes, but its role in practice; the builder's assistant who fetches a slab at the call "Slab!" understands the word, and his understanding is nothing beyond mastery of the technique. Against the demand — the Tractatus's own — that a concept have an essence fixing its every application, Wittgenstein offers family resemblance: the things called "games" share no one common feature, only overlapping similarities, and the concept works without an essence; the demand for one was the philosopher's imposition. The reversal is exact: where the early work legislated a priori what language must be, the later work looks at what language is, and finds the determinacy demand itself the source of the puzzlement. Use-based, pragmatist, and inferentialist theories of meaning descend from these sections; so does the standing objection that "use" is too coarse to explain how finite speakers understand unbounded novel sentences.

Rule-following and Kripke's skeptic

The deepest stretch of the Investigations (§§138–242) asks what it is to follow a rule. A pupil taught to add 2 continues correctly to 1000, then writes 1004, 1008. What fact makes him wrong? Any finite run of examples is compatible with deviant continuations; a rule in words is more signs needing interpretation; a formula before the mind can be applied variously. Hence the paradox of §201: if every course of action can be made to accord with the rule on some interpretation, none is determined by it. Wittgenstein's resolution: the paradox assumes that grasping a rule is always an interpretation, but there is a grasp that is practice, not interpretation — acting as trained, within a custom, where agreement in judgments is not the abolition of objectivity but its background condition. Kripke's 1982 reading made these sections an industry. His Wittgenstein is a skeptic conceding there is no fact — about past usage, dispositions, or inner life — in virtue of which I mean addition rather than the deviant "quaddition," who then offers a "skeptical solution": meaning-attributions are not descriptions of facts but moves licensed by communal practice. Whether "Kripkenstein" is Wittgenstein is widely doubted — Baker and Hacker held that the paradox is stated only to be dissolved — but Kripke's skeptic set decades of agenda for the metaphysics of meaning. In the Remarks the same considerations yield the claim that proof forges necessity rather than tracking it — Dummett's "full-blooded conventionalism," easier to dismiss than refute.

The private language argument

Could there be a language only its speaker could understand — its words referring to immediate private sensations, the things only he can know? Sections 243–315 argue no. Suppose I write "S" in a diary whenever a certain sensation recurs, with no outward criterion. What makes a later use of "S" correct? Only my impression that this is the same again — but then whatever seems right will be right, which only means that here there is no "right." A sign with no standard of correct use is not a word, and inner ostension — concentrating on the sensation while saying the sign — fixes no technique of application. The moral is not behaviorism: Wittgenstein denies not sensations but the assimilation of their grammar to object and designation — "not a something, but not a nothing either." Sensation-language lives off natural expression and shared circumstance: "pain" is taught to the child crying over a scraped knee, and the beetle in everyone's private box — the famous image — drops out of the language-game as irrelevant to how the word works. The consequences radiate: other minds becomes an attitude toward a soul rather than an inference from behavior; first-person authority becomes expressive rather than observational; every empiricism built on private givens loses its foundation. Whether it proves more than general memory-skepticism would is disputed; its transformation of the philosophy of mind is not.

Philosophy as therapy

Both philosophies issue in a metaphilosophy, and the later one is explicit: philosophical problems have the form "I don't know my way about." They arise when language "goes on holiday" — when forms of words are taken out of the language-games where they work and asked metaphysical questions they were never shaped to answer; we are held captive by pictures (the inner object, the crystalline rule, the name-and-object model) lying in our language itself. Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language: it advances no theses, but assembles reminders of how words are used until the problem does not get solved but disappears — the fly shown the way out of the fly-bottle. Steelmanned, this is a diagnosis with a mechanism: it predicts where problems cluster (wherever surface grammar assimilates unlike uses — "I have a pain" modeled on "I have a pin"), and it explains a datum other metaphilosophies must excuse, philosophy's millennia of unresolved disagreement. Critics answer that the diagnosis is itself a thesis defended by argument; that some problems survive every grammatical clarification; and that the therapeutic conception, consistently applied, would end the subject. Wittgenstein accepted the last consequence with equanimity.

On Certainty: hinge propositions

Moore had answered the skeptic by holding up his hands — here is one hand, here another, therefore an external world exists — claiming to know these things. Wittgenstein's last notebooks accept Moore's certainties while rejecting his account of them. "I know I have hands," in ordinary circumstances, is not knowledge at all: knowledge belongs where doubt, checking, and evidence have a place, and nothing is more certain than the propositions by which any checking would be done. These are hinges: propositions exempt from doubt so that inquiry can turn on them. The earth has existed for many years; there are physical objects — the inherited world-picture, held not by evidence but by everything we do: the riverbed of thought rather than the river. The skeptic's mistake is not asserting a falsehood but simulating a doubt: a doubt that doubted everything would dissolve the very practices that give "doubt" its sense. The position is neither foundationalism — hinges are not premises we reason from but the form of our reasoning — nor coherentism. "Hinge epistemology" — Wright, Pritchard, Moyal-Sharrock — is now a research program, and the notebooks, never revised, are the most influential unfinished book in modern epistemology.

Influence and reception

The first reception was a productive misreading. The Vienna Circle read the Tractatus line by line and took from it verificationism and the elimination of metaphysics — discarding as embarrassment the saying/showing doctrine and the mysticism its author considered the point. Through pupils and executors — Anscombe (whose Intention founded modern action theory), Malcolm, Rhees, von Wright — and through Ryle and Oxford ordinary-language philosophy, the Investigations dominated the Anglophone field into the 1960s. The pushback was proportionate: Russell declared the later philosophy an abdication that made philosophy at best a help to lexicographers; Popper treated the therapeutic conception as a betrayal of philosophy's real problems. The deeper eclipse came from practice, not polemic: Quinean naturalism, Chomskyan linguistics, and the systematic semantics and metaphysics of Kripke, Montague, and Lewis simply resumed the theory-building Wittgenstein had declared misconceived; by the 1980s he was cited everywhere and followed almost nowhere. The revival has three strands: the resolute rewriting of the Tractatus; hinge epistemology's colonization of On Certainty; and the use of the rule-following and private-language material by philosophers (McDowell, Wright, Brandom) who accept the arguments while declining the therapeutic frame. Beyond philosophy his mark runs through anthropology, social theory, theology, and aesthetics. He remains the discipline's standing self-accusation: a thinker who held that professionalized philosophy was a disease of the intellect, canonized by the profession he diagnosed.

Reading path

Do not start with the Tractatus. Start with the Blue Book: plain English, the later method with the least apparatus. Then the Philosophical Investigations (Anscombe's translation, revised by Hacker and Schulte): §§1–133 on meaning and philosophy, §§138–242 on rules, §§243–315 on private language carry the core; it is an album, and rereading is the intended mode. Then the Tractatus (Pears–McGuinness translation), beside Russell's "Philosophy of Logical Atomism" for contrast, and On Certainty last — short and self-standing. Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language is essential, read as argument rather than exegesis. Secondary: Monk's biography The Duty of Genius, itself an interpretation; Baker and Hacker's commentaries for the traditional reading; Crary and Read's The New Wittgenstein for the resolute one; McGinn's Routledge guidebook as the accessible companion.

Assessment

Editorially: the question of early versus late matters less than the continuity both books enforce: the limits of sense as philosophy's real subject. The rule-following considerations and the private language argument seem to this encyclopedia the strongest sustained arguments in twentieth-century philosophy of mind and language, largely unanswered rather than answered. The therapeutic metaphilosophy is another matter: defended by arguments, it is a thesis in the genre it condemns, and the discipline's decision to mine Wittgenstein for doctrines while shelving his verdict on doctrine may be self-serving — or the correct judgment on the one place his diagnosis outran the evidence. He would have regarded this article as a symptom. That the symptom is the only way to transmit him is the joke built into his legacy.
