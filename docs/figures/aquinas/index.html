Thomas Aquinas

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-09-09.15

1225–1274 · Dominican friar and master of theology at Paris; the Latin scholastic tradition's central synthesis of Aristotle, Avicenna, and Christian doctrine

Why they matter

Aquinas is the philosopher through whom the Latin West made its peace with Aristotle, and the terms of that peace still govern much of philosophy of religion, ethics, and metaphysics. The full Aristotelian corpus reached Paris wrapped in an Arabic commentary tradition that read it as a closed naturalism: an eternal world, one shared intellect, a God ignorant of particulars. Aquinas showed that Aristotle's instruments — form and matter, act and potency, the four causes, the virtues — could be taken over entire, corrected at a few decisive points, and made to carry a doctrine of creation, providence, and grace that Aristotle never imagined. The corrections came largely from Avicenna, above all the distinction between what a thing is and that it is, which became the axis of a new metaphysics of being. The result was a system in which reason and revelation have distinct domains and one source, grace perfects nature rather than replacing it, and a human being is neither a soul imprisoned in a body nor a body that happens to think. Nearly every later position on God's existence and nature, religious language, natural law, and the virtues is either an elaboration of Aquinas or a considered departure from him.

Life

Thomas was born around 1225 at Roccasecca, near Aquino in the Kingdom of Sicily, youngest son of a minor noble house. An oblate at Monte Cassino from about five, he was sent in his teens to the university at Naples, where he first read Aristotle and first met the Dominicans. His decision in 1244 to join a mendicant order so alarmed his family that they detained him for a year. Released, he studied at Paris and Cologne under Albert the Great. In Paris from 1252 he lectured on Lombard's Sentences and became a master of theology in 1256. A decade in Italy followed — Orvieto, Rome, where the Summa theologiae was begun, and Viterbo. His second Paris regency (1268–72) was fought on two fronts: against the radical Aristotelians of the arts faculty led by Siger of Brabant, and against Franciscan theologians who thought his own Aristotelianism had gone too far. He returned to Naples in 1272. On 6 December 1273, after an experience during Mass he never explained, he stopped writing, saying that beside what he had seen his work seemed like straw. He died on the road to the Council of Lyon, at Fossanova, on 7 March 1274, aged about forty-nine.

Works

  • On Being and Essence (c. 1252–56) — a short early treatise laying down the metaphysics of essence and existence on which everything later rests.
  • Commentary on the Sentences (1252–56) — the vast apprentice work, later quarried to complete the unfinished Summa.
  • Disputed Questions — On Truth, On the Power of God, On the Soul, On Evil (1256–72): scholastic argument at its most searching, often fuller than the Summa.
  • Summa contra Gentiles (1259–65) — four books on what reason can establish about God, creation, and providence, with revealed doctrine reserved to the last.
  • Summa theologiae (1265/66–73) — the masterwork, written for beginners and left incomplete: the Prima Pars on God and creation, the two-part Secunda on human action, law, and virtue, the Tertia on Christ.
  • The Aristotle commentaries (1268–73) — line-by-line expositions of the Physics, Metaphysics, De anima, Nicomachean Ethics, and others.
  • Polemical opuscula — On the Unity of the Intellect against the Averroists (1270) and On the Eternity of the World (1271), fixing his position between the arts masters and the Franciscans.

Thought

Being and essence: the Avicennian inheritance

Aristotle's analysis of substances into matter and form explains change, individuation, and the difference between a living body and a corpse. It does not explain why there are any substances at all, and it has no place for beings that are form without matter yet are not God. Aquinas found the missing distinction in Avicenna: in any creature, what the thing is (its essence) is really other than the act by which it is (esse). The early argument is simple — one can understand what a human being or a phoenix is without knowing whether any exists, so existence is no part of what is understood — and its conclusion is that essence stands to existence as potency to act, a second composition layered over that of matter and form. This lets angels be pure forms yet creatures: they lack matter but not the composition that marks everything dependent. Aquinas altered his source at one decisive point. Avicenna's Latin readers, following Averroes's polemic, took him to treat existence as an accident that befalls essence; Aquinas insisted that esse is the actuality of every form, the act of all acts, which essence limits and receives. Being is thus the most intimate thing in each thing, participated in degrees, and creation is ordered by how much of it a nature can hold. Whether the distinction is real, as Giles of Rome argued, or merely conceptual, as Henry of Ghent and later Suárez held, was the first great dispute about Thomism and remains open.

God as subsistent being and the five ways

The distinction yields a theology by subtraction. If in every creature essence and existence differ, a being whose essence is simply to be would be unique, uncaused, and the source of everything else's existence: God is ipsum esse subsistens, subsistent being itself, not a being among beings but the act of being unreceived by any limiting nature. The five ways (Summa theologiae Ia q.2 a.3) are best read as five routes into this conclusion rather than freestanding proofs. Each begins from an Aristotelian feature of the world — things change, cause, come to be and pass away, are better and worse, act toward ends without thinking — and argues that the feature is intelligible only if the series of its explanations terminates in something that has the relevant perfection without receiving it: pure act, an uncaused cause, a being necessary through itself, a maximal being in which others participate, an intelligence directing the unintelligent. The regress forbidden is not temporal — Aquinas held that reason cannot show the world had a beginning, and the ways would run in an eternal world; what cannot regress infinitely is a series in which each member causes only because a prior member is acting through it now, as a hand moves a stick moving a stone. The questions that follow (qq.3–11) derive the attributes negatively, removing from the first cause everything composition would entail — no matter, no accidents, no difference of essence and existence — hence simplicity, and from simplicity perfection, immutability, eternity, and unity. This tells us, Aquinas insists, what God is not rather than what he is.

Analogy against univocity and equivocation

A simple, uncaused being belongs to no genus, so "wise" cannot mean in "God is wise" exactly what it means of Solomon; but if it meant something wholly unrelated, the sentence would be empty and every argument from creatures to God would trade on four terms. Aquinas's answer (Ia q.13) is that perfection-terms are said of God and creatures analogically: the perfection signified is really and pre-eminently in God, its cause, but the mode in which our words signify it — as a quality possessed by a subject — is drawn from creatures and fails of him. The distinction between the thing signified and the mode of signifying is the whole doctrine, and it lets the earlier negations coexist with true affirmations. Scotus objected a generation later that unless some concept of being applies univocally to God and creatures no inference about God is valid, and the argument has never closed: Cajetan systematized analogy into a metaphysics of proportionality, McInerny argued it was never more than a logical doctrine about names, and Barth's rejection of the analogia entis made it a fault line between Catholic and Protestant theology in the twentieth century.

The soul as form of the body

Against the Platonic and Augustinian picture of a soul using a body as a sailor uses a ship, Aquinas took Aristotle's definition without reserve: the soul is the substantial form of the living body, the principle by which it lives, senses, and thinks, and a human being is one substance, not two. He went further than his contemporaries in holding that each substance has exactly one substantial form, so that the intellectual soul is also what makes the body a body — the thesis Kilwardby condemned at Oxford. Yet Aquinas also held that the soul survives death, and the argument (Ia q.75) is the heart of his psychology. Understanding grasps the natures of all bodies; an organ with a determinate bodily nature would obstruct that universal receptivity, as a tongue coated with bitterness tastes everything bitter; so the intellect's proper operation is not the operation of any organ, and what operates through itself subsists through itself. The soul is a subsistent form, incorruptible because a form cannot be parted from itself. Aquinas did not soften the discomfort: the separated soul is not the human person, its condition is unnatural, and the resurrection of the body is required for a complete human being to exist again. Against Averroes he argued that the intellect cannot be one separate substance shared by all, since it is this man who understands. Modern readers divide over whether the view is dualism or hylomorphic materialism, and the "survivalist" and "corruptionist" readings of the separated soul divide current scholarship.

Natural law and the virtues

The Secunda Pars treats human action as a rational creature's movement back toward its source, through law and virtue. Law (IaIIae qq.90–108) is an ordinance of reason for the common good, promulgated by one who has care of the community. The eternal law is the divine reason ordering all things; the natural law is the rational creature's participation in it — the first principles of practical reason, self-evident to anyone who grasps the terms, the most basic being that good is to be done and pursued and evil avoided. Its content is filled in by the natural inclinations — to preserve one's being, to procreate and rear offspring, to know the truth about God and live in society — and human law is derived from it as conclusion from premises or as determination of what the precept leaves open, so that an enactment contrary to natural law is a corruption of law. Virtue (qq.49–67) is Aristotle's hexis rendered as habitus: a stable disposition of a power toward its good act. The four cardinal virtues — prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance — are acquired by practice, unified through prudence, and ordered to the imperfect happiness of this life; the three theological virtues — faith, hope, charity — are infused by grace and ordered to the vision of God, with charity as the form of all the rest. The Secunda Secundae then treats the virtues one by one, yielding the analysis of self-defense that became the doctrine of double effect. The whole is teleological: what one ought to do is fixed by what a being of this nature is for, and the theological completion shows what the natural account was reaching toward rather than cancelling it.

Faith and reason, and the method of the Summa

Aquinas distinguished two orders of truth about God. Some truths — that God exists, is one, is not a body — reason can demonstrate, and these are the preambles of faith; others — the Trinity, the Incarnation — exceed reason's reach and are held by faith, but cannot contradict what reason establishes, since both flow from one source. A demonstration that seems to prove something contrary to faith contains an error that can in principle be found; hence he refused both the fideist's retreat from argument and the "double truth" his contemporaries attributed to the arts masters. The same rule governs philosophy within theology: sacred doctrine is a science whose principles are received from God's own knowledge, and it uses philosophical argument as any science uses its subordinate disciplines. The Summa embodies this pedagogically. Each article poses a question, sets out the strongest objections to the answer to come, cites an authority on the other side, resolves the matter, and answers each objection in turn — no position dismissed before it has been stated at its best. The order of the whole, on Chenu's influential reading, traces the procession of creatures from God and their return to him, with Christ as the way. The form is the schools' disputed question; what Aquinas added was a discipline of proportion, treating each matter at the length its difficulty warrants and no more.

Influence and reception

The immediate reception was hostile. On 7 March 1277 Bishop Tempier of Paris condemned 219 propositions taught in the arts faculty, several touching Thomist theses; Kilwardby's Oxford condemnation followed within days, and the Franciscan William de la Mare's Correctorium catalogued over a hundred errors in the Summa. The Dominicans adopted his teaching in 1278–79, and John XXII's canonization of 1323 turned the tide; the Paris condemnation was withdrawn in what concerned him two years later. Scotus and Ockham built against him in the next generation, on univocity, the formal distinction, and the primacy of will.

Thomism's long career ran through the commentators: Capreolus in the fifteenth century; Cajetan and the Salamanca school in the sixteenth, when the Summa replaced the Sentences as the theological textbook and Aquinas was named Doctor of the Church in 1567; Báñez and John of St. Thomas in the seventeenth, with Suárez carrying scholastic vocabulary to Descartes and Leibniz. After an eighteenth-century eclipse, Leo XIII's Aeterni Patris (1879) made Aquinas the norm of Catholic philosophy and launched the Leonine edition. Neo-Thomism then fractured productively: Garrigou-Lagrange defended the commentators' strict system; Gilson and Fabro recovered the primacy of esse; Maritain applied natural law to democracy and human rights; Maréchal, Rahner, and Lonergan read Aquinas through Kant; and Chenu's historical school insisted he be read as a thirteenth-century theologian rather than a timeless system, a program Torrell and Pasnau have continued.

Analytic Thomism arose in Britain. Geach's essay on form and existence in Three Philosophers (1961, with Anscombe) recast the essence–existence distinction in Fregean terms; Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) reopened virtue and natural teleology for a profession that had forgotten them; Kenny's The Five Ways (1969) and Aquinas on Being were the critical wing, arguing that the proofs fail and that esse trades on confusions Frege exposed. Haldane named the movement in 1997; Kretzmann, Stump, and Davies made Aquinas a standard interlocutor in philosophy of religion and metaphysics, and MacIntyre and Finnis restored his ethics and jurisprudence to wide currency. Current pushback runs on three lines: that the five ways depend on a physics no one holds; that the real distinction is either trivial or unintelligible; and, from within theology, that the natural–supernatural division his commentators hardened is not his own.

Reading path

Begin with On Being and Essence, short, and the key to everything else. Then Summa theologiae Ia qq.2–13 (existence, simplicity, attributes, analogy), qq.75–76 (the soul), IaIIae qq.90–97 (law) and qq.49–67 (virtue), through McDermott's concise Summa or Pegis's Basic Writings. Summa contra Gentiles Book I gives the philosophical theology at fuller length. Secondary: Davies's The Thought of Thomas Aquinas as the reliable overview, Stump's Aquinas as the analytic reconstruction, Torrell's Saint Thomas Aquinas as the standard life, Kenny's Aquinas on Mind as the sympathetic critic, and Wippel's The Metaphysical Thought of Thomas Aquinas for being and essence.

Assessment

Editorially: Aquinas's distinctive strength is architectural — the capacity to give each part of a system exactly the weight it needs. That is also his limitation as a philosopher to argue with: a single doctrine, extracted, often looks either obvious or arbitrary, because the arguments gesture toward a structure rather than standing alone. The metaphysics of esse remains the deepest and least settled part of the inheritance; whether it is a discovery or an artefact of Latin grammar is a question Kenny and Gilson answered oppositely, and the corpus has not resolved.
