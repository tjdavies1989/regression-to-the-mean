Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna)

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.6

980–1037 · Persian philosopher and physician; the system-builder of falsafa and the most influential philosopher of the Islamic world

Why they matter

Ibn Sīnā is the rare figure who is the central reference point of two civilizations' philosophy at once. Within the Islamic world he did what Aristotle had done for the Greeks: he built a complete system — logic, natural philosophy, psychology, metaphysics — so commanding that for the next eight centuries philosophy in Arabic and Persian was conducted largely as commentary on, defense of, or attack upon him, not on the Greeks he had absorbed. His signature move, the distinction between essence and existence, reorganized metaphysics around a new question — not what things are but that they are, and what could account for it — and his proof of a Necessary Existent from that distinction remade rational theology in Islam, Judaism, and Christendom alike. Translated into Latin in the twelfth century, he taught the scholastics their metaphysical vocabulary before they could read Aristotle whole; Aquinas's esse and essentia are his terms naturalized. And in the "flying man" he produced one of philosophy's permanent thought experiments: the soul's immediate awareness of itself, prised apart from every awareness of body. That he also wrote the Canon of Medicine, the standard medical textbook of Eurasia for six hundred years, is almost a footnote.

Life

Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAbdallāh ibn Sīnā was born around 980 near Bukhara, in the Persian Samanid domains, and left an autobiography — rare among premodern philosophers — that is itself a philosophical document: a portrait of a mind that learned essentially alone. By his own account he had mastered jurisprudence, medicine, and the mathematical sciences in his teens, was treating patients at sixteen, and read Aristotle's Metaphysics forty times without understanding its point until a chance purchase of al-Fārābī's short treatise on its aims unlocked it. Access to the Samanid royal library, granted for a successful medical cure, completed his education; he claimed to have learned nothing new after eighteen, only judgment.

The rest of the life was politics endured for the sake of work. The Samanid collapse sent him westward through the courts of Khwārazm, Gurgān, and Rayy; at Hamadhān he served the Būyid emir as vizier, was ousted by a military mutiny, hid, was imprisoned in a fortress, and escaped in disguise to Isfahan, where under ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla he finally found fourteen relatively stable years. He wrote at a pace that astonished contemporaries — major works composed from memory while traveling, dictated in prison, drafted in the mornings before court duties — and lived, his students recorded, with equal appetite for work and pleasure. He died in 1037 at Hamadhān, on campaign with his patron, of a colic he characteristically insisted on treating himself.

Works

  • Al-Shifāʾ (The Cure, c. 1020–27) — the vast summa: logic, natural philosophy, mathematics, and metaphysics in one architecture. Its psychology (the De Anima) and its Metaphysics (Ilāhiyyāt) are the load-bearing books; the latter, in Latin, shaped scholasticism.
  • Al-Najāt (The Salvation) — his own compact abridgment of the system; the psychology section is the standard entry to his theory of soul.
  • Al-Ishārāt wa-l-tanbīhāt (Pointers and Reminders) — the late masterpiece: terse, allusive chains of argument, including the mature proof of the Necessary Existent; the most commented-upon philosophy book of the Islamic East.
  • Dānishnāma-yi ʿAlāʾī (Book of Knowledge for ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla) — the system in Persian, effectively founding Persian philosophical prose.
  • Al-Qānūn fī al-ṭibb (The Canon of Medicine) — the synthesis of Galenic medicine that governed medical education from Central Asia to Montpellier into the seventeenth century.
  • Lost or fragmentary: The Easterners and the Kitāb al-Inṣāf (Fair Judgment), whose surviving traces fuel debate over whether a distinct "Eastern philosophy" diverged from the Aristotelian corpus.

Thought

Essence and existence

Ibn Sīnā's metaphysics begins from a distinction that Aristotle had gestured at and never made load-bearing: what a thing is (its essence, māhiyya) is one question; that it is (its existence, wujūd) is another. Nothing in the definition of horse tells you whether any horses exist. From this he drew a doctrine of striking abstraction, the so-called indifference of essence: considered purely in itself, an essence is neither universal nor particular, neither one nor many, neither existent nor non-existent. Horseness, in his famous formula, is just horseness. Universality accrues to it as conceived in a mind; particularity as realized in matter; but the essence itself is indifferent to both careers, which is precisely why one and the same nature can be in many individuals and in many minds. Existence, then, is not a component of any finite essence but something that comes to it — Ibn Sīnā says it is an "accident," though not a quality among qualities: it is what must be added, from outside, for the essence to be realized at all. Every contingent thing is therefore metaphysically composite — essence plus existence — and its existence demands a cause. Averroes objected that this makes existence a strange accident of a subject that would have to exist already to receive it; the Latins inherited both the distinction and the quarrel, and the question of whether essence and existence are "really" distinct became a defining dispute of scholastic metaphysics. However the composition is parsed, the distinction gave metaphysics a new master question: what accounts not for the natures of things but for their being there at all.

The Necessary Existent and the proof from contingency

The essence–existence distinction yields a modal division of everything there is. Whatever's essence does not include existence is possible in itself (mumkin): it can exist or not, and if it exists, something else must have tipped the balance — through its cause it becomes necessary, but only borrowed necessity. If anything exists whose essence is its existence, it is necessary in itself (wājib al-wujūd bi-dhātihi): incapable of not existing, needing no cause. Ibn Sīnā's proof, given its finest form in the Ishārāt and named by him the burhān al-ṣiddīqīn, the demonstration of the truthful, argues that the totality of possible existents cannot account for itself: consider everything possible, as one aggregate; that aggregate is itself possible, and its existence must be explained by something outside it — which can only be the Necessary Existent. He was explicit and proud that this proof is metaphysical, not physical: it starts from existence and its modal structure, not — as Aristotle's argument for a prime mover had — from motion and the cosmos, so that reflection on being alone reaches God, whom the sciences of nature could reach only as a mover. From necessity he then derives the classical attributes: the Necessary Existent must be one (two necessary beings would need a differentiator, importing composition), simple, immaterial, pure intellect and pure good. From it the world proceeds not by choice at a moment but by eternal emanation — a hierarchy of intellects and celestial spheres, terminating in the Active Intellect that governs the sublunar world — since a cause complete from eternity has its effect from eternity. The proof, in kalām dress or scholastic dress, is the ancestor of every argument "from contingency" since; whether the aggregate of all possibles is itself one more possible thing remains the standard point of attack.

The flying man and the soul's self-awareness

In the psychology of the Shifāʾ, Ibn Sīnā asks the reader to imagine a person created at a stroke, fully mature and rational, but suspended in a void: limbs splayed so nothing touches, no sight, no sound, no sensation of any kind, no memory of a body. Such a person, he claims, would nonetheless affirm their own existence — while affirming nothing about limbs, organs, brain, or anything spatial. What is affirmed cannot be identical with what is not affirmed at all; so the self one is immediately aware of is not the body, and the soul's awareness of itself owes nothing to sensation. Ibn Sīnā is candid that this is a pointer (tanbīh) rather than a demonstration — a device to remind us of something continuously present but overlooked: that self-awareness is primitive, constant, and prior to all awareness of objects. The comparison with Descartes's cogito is irresistible and must be handled with care. Both find bedrock in the self's presence to itself; but Ibn Sīnā is not answering a skeptic, is not founding the sciences on certainty, and doubts nothing — the flying man serves a psychology, not an epistemology, and its conclusion is a thesis about the nature of soul (immaterial, individuated, self-subsistent, hence capable of surviving the body) rather than about the order of knowledge. On his account the rational soul is not, as in Aristotle, merely the form of an organic body: it uses the body, is individuated by its origin with a body, but is a substance in its own right, immortal by nature — though what philosophy promises the soul is intellectual felicity or misery, not the resurrection of flesh.

God's knowledge of particulars and the three condemned theses

A Necessary Existent that is pure intellect knows — but how, and what? Ibn Sīnā's answer is that God knows all things as their cause: knowing His own essence, He knows everything that flows from it, in an eternal, unchanging act. This entails that God knows particulars, but "in a universal way": He knows every eclipse through the complete network of astronomical causes that necessitates it, timelessly — not by watching the sky and updating, as we do, since perceptual, time-indexed knowledge would put change and passivity in the changeless. Whether such knowledge deserves the name — whether knowing the causal recipe for an eclipse is knowing this eclipse, or knowing that Zayd sins — is exactly where al-Ghazālī struck. In the Tahāfut al-falāsifa (The Incoherence of the Philosophers) he indicted twenty Avicennan doctrines and singled out three as not merely error but unbelief: the eternity of the world, the denial that God knows particulars as particulars, and the denial of bodily resurrection. The charges were not caricature; each names a real consequence Ibn Sīnā had accepted with open eyes, because on his principles an eternal cause has an eternal effect, changeless knowledge cannot be perspectival, and an immaterial soul has no philosophical use for restored flesh — the Qurʾānic imagery he read as necessary rhetoric for the many. The confrontation fixed the agenda of Islamic theology for centuries: not whether to engage Ibn Sīnā, but on which points he could be absorbed and on which he must be resisted.

Logic reformed

Ibn Sīnā is one of the few figures between the Stoics and Leibniz to alter logic itself rather than transmit it. He organized logic around the pair taṣawwur and taṣdīq — conception and assent — making it the universal instrument for acquiring both, and he treated Aristotle as a resource to be corrected, announcing that one should learn logic from the subject, not from books. His deepest innovations concern modality and time together. An assertion like "every B is A," he argued, is systematically ambiguous until one specifies when and how the predicate is supposed to hold: at some time, at all times, necessarily while the subject exists, or necessarily while the subject satisfies the description "B" (his readers codified the last two as the dhātī and waṣfī readings — necessity of the thing versus necessity under a description; a moving thing is necessarily changing only while it is moving). On this basis he rebuilt the modal syllogistic, rejecting Aristotelian conversions that fail under his readings, and developed a logic of conditional and disjunctive propositions with its own syllogistic, absorbing and extending what survived of Stoic themes. The result was not a commentary tradition but a rival system: within two centuries "logic" in the Islamic East meant Avicennan logic, studied through his texts and their commentaries, with Aristotle's Organon receding into history. Historians of logic now treat his temporal-modal propositions as a genuinely alternative development of formal logic — evidence that the discipline's path from Aristotle to Frege was not the only one available.

Influence and reception

No philosopher of the medieval world had a wider wake. In the Islamic East, al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut is remembered as the great refutation, but its effect was the opposite of destruction: al-Ghazālī himself certified Avicennan logic as harmless and necessary, and the theologians who followed — above all Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, Ibn Sīnā's sharpest critical commentator — rebuilt kalām on Avicennan foundations, so that post-classical Islamic theology argues in his vocabulary of essence, existence, and necessity even where it rejects his conclusions. Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī's counter-commentary on the Ishārāt answered al-Rāzī point by point, and the ensuing commentary tradition ran for centuries. Suhrawardī's Illuminationism defined itself against him (arguing, among other things, that the essence–existence distinction is merely conceptual); in Safavid Iran, Mullā Ṣadrā effected the profoundest reversal, arguing for the primacy of existence (aṣālat al-wujūd) over essence — a revolution conducted entirely within the problem-space Ibn Sīnā had built, and the living core of philosophy in Iran to this day.

In the Latin West, translations made at Toledo in the twelfth century put the Shifāʾ's psychology and metaphysics into scholastic hands a generation before the full Aristotle, so that the Latins first read Aristotle through Avicennan eyes. The debts are structural: Aquinas's real distinction between esse and essentia, and his proof of God from the composition of the two, are Avicennan inheritances turned to new use; Scotus took from him the common nature indifferent to universality and particularity, and the conviction that being is the first object of the intellect; the doctrine that God alone is His own existence became common scholastic property. The Latins also pushed back — against the eternal world, the emanation scheme, and the single Active Intellect — and "Avicennized Augustinianism" was an early casualty of the condemnations; but the pushback, as in the East, was conducted with his tools. In medicine the Canon was simply the curriculum, printed dozens of times in Renaissance Europe and lectured on at Padua into the 1600s — a second, parallel empire of influence that made "Avicenna" a byword for learned authority and, eventually, a target for medical reformers like Paracelsus, who burned it publicly.

Reading path

Begin with the psychology: the Najāt's section on the soul (F. Rahman, Avicenna's Psychology) is short and contains the flying man's neighborhood. Then the Ishārāt for the mature metaphysics in its most concentrated form (Shams Inati's translations cover the work; the metaphysics volume, Ibn Sīnā and Mysticism aside, is the essential part), and the Metaphysics of The Healing in Marmura's facing-page translation for the full system — Books I, VI, and VIII carry the essence–existence doctrine and the Necessary Existent. Al-Ghazālī's Incoherence of the Philosophers (also Marmura) is the indispensable hostile companion. Secondary: Jon McGinnis's Avicenna (Oxford) as the best single guide; Dimitri Gutas's Avicenna and the Aristotelian Tradition for the life, the corpus, and the demolition of the "Eastern mysticism" legend; Peter Adamson's edited Interpreting Avicenna for the state of scholarship.

Assessment

Editorially: Ibn Sīnā is the strongest counterexample to the story that philosophy's center of gravity passed directly from Athens to Paris. For systematic reach joined to originality his peers are Aristotle and Kant, and the Western survey's habit of calling him a "transmitter" of Greek thought inverts the truth: what he transmitted was a system mostly his own, which two civilizations spent centuries digesting. The essence–existence distinction looks, on merits, like a permanent acquisition: every later account of modality and ontological dependence either uses it or must explain it away.
