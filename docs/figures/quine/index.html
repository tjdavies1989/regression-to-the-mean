W. V. O. Quine

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-09-09.15

1908–2000 · American logician and philosopher; the analytic tradition's great naturalist, who dismantled logical empiricism from inside and rebuilt philosophy as a chapter of science

Why they matter

Quine took the program of Carnap and the Vienna Circle — philosophy as the logical analysis of science, resting on a sharp line between truths of meaning and truths of fact — and removed the line. "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) argued that no principled distinction separates analytic from synthetic truths, and that statements face experience as a body, not singly; with that, a priori knowledge, meanings as entities, and philosophy as a discipline prior to science all lost their footing at once. What replaced them was naturalism: philosophy is continuous with natural science, has no standpoint outside it, and asks its questions — what exists, what we know, what our words mean — with science's own methods and science's own fallibility. He also gave the tradition its working criterion of ontological commitment, its most disquieting argument about meaning (the indeterminacy of translation), its fiercest resistance to modal logic, and the ideal of a canonical notation from which a theory's ontology can be read. Nearly every major program in Anglophone philosophy since 1960 — the modal metaphysics he opposed no less than the naturalism he founded — defined itself by its relation to him.

Life

Willard Van Orman Quine was born in 1908 in Akron, Ohio; a boyhood passion for maps and etymology foreshadowed a lifelong taste for exactness and for travel. He studied mathematics at Oberlin, reading Russell on the side, and took a Harvard doctorate under Whitehead in 1932, in two years. A traveling fellowship carried him to Vienna, Prague, and Warsaw in 1932–33, where he attended the Vienna Circle, spent formative weeks with Carnap — the closest thing he had to a teacher — and learned the new logic at its most rigorous from Tarski and Leśniewski. He returned to Harvard as a Junior Fellow and never left, apart from naval intelligence work during the war, holding the Edgar Pierce chair from 1956 until retirement in 1978. Two marriages, four children, a hundred countries visited. He wrote a spare, witty prose that hid the difficulty of his ideas, stayed doctrinally consistent across six decades to a degree almost unmatched in the tradition, and died in Boston in 2000.

Works

  • "Truth by Convention" (1936) — the first shot at Carnap: if logic were true by convention, applying the conventions would already require logic.
  • "On What There Is" (1948) — the criterion of ontological commitment; the friends of possible objects and of meanings demolished.
  • "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) — the rejection of analyticity and of reductionism; confirmation holism; the most influential paper in twentieth-century analytic philosophy.
  • From a Logical Point of View (1953) — the essays above collected, with "Reference and Modality," against quantified modal logic.
  • Word and Object (1960) — the systematic statement: radical translation, indeterminacy, regimentation, the flight from intension.
  • Ontological Relativity and Other Essays (1969) — "Ontological Relativity" and "Epistemology Naturalized," which turned the arguments of Word and Object into positive programs.
  • Pursuit of Truth (1990) — the late summary, restating the system with new economy.

Thought

Ontological commitment: "On What There Is"

The old puzzle of non-being — how can we deny that Pegasus exists without there being something, Pegasus, that we deny it of? — had driven Meinong to populate the world with subsistent entities. Quine moved the question from names to quantifiers. Names can be paraphrased away by Russell's method: "Pegasus exists" becomes "something pegasizes," and the denial is a denial that anything does. What a theory is committed to is then not what its singular terms appear to name but what its bound variables must range over for its sentences to be true. The slogan is that to be is to be the value of a variable. This is not a theory of what exists — it tells you what a theory says exists — but a standard of clarity: state the theory in first-order notation and read the commitments off the quantifiers. It cuts two ways. Against the inflationist, talk of possible fat men in the doorway can be dropped without loss; against the deflationist, mathematics, ineliminably quantifying over numbers and classes, commits the scientist who uses it to abstract objects. Quine accepted the latter reluctantly — his early sympathies were nominalist, and a 1947 paper with Goodman tried to do without classes — and the indispensability argument for mathematical platonism is the enduring result: believe in whatever our best total theory of the world cannot do without, and it cannot do without sets.

"Two Dogmas" and the web of belief

Logical empiricism rested on two theses Quine called dogmas: that analytic truths (true in virtue of meaning alone) divide cleanly from synthetic truths (true in virtue of fact), and that each meaningful statement is equivalent to some construction out of immediate experience. The paper's first half is a circle-hunt. Analyticity is explained through synonymy, synonymy through definition or interchangeability salva veritate, interchangeability through necessity, necessity through analyticity; each explanation presupposes another member of the same family, and none is anchored in anything a behaviorally minded empiricist could observe. Carnap's semantical rules only relocate the problem: defining "analytic-in-L" for an artificial language does not explain "analytic" as such. The second half offers the positive alternative that carried the argument: confirmation holism. Following Duhem, Quine argued that individual statements have no isolable empirical content; only a large body of theory meets experience, and a recalcitrant observation can be accommodated by revising any part of it — the peripheral hypothesis, the auxiliary assumptions, or, in principle, the laws of logic. Knowledge is a web of belief, a field of force whose edges touch experience and whose interior, mathematics and logic included, meets observation only through the whole. Statements near the center are not analytic, merely those we are least willing to give up. The a priori collapses into the deeply entrenched, and the difference between the philosopher's questions about frameworks and the scientist's questions within them becomes one of degree, not kind.

Word and Object: indeterminacy, inscrutability, relativity

If meanings are not entities and analyticity has no empirical footing, what remains of meaning? Word and Object answers with a thought experiment. A field linguist confronts a language with no prior contact — radical translation — with only native behavior and the surrounding environment to go on. She can fix the translation of observation sentences by matching assent and dissent to stimulus conditions, and can fix the truth-functional connectives; but the totality of such evidence, Quine argued, is compatible with rival translation manuals that assign to the same native sentence non-equivalent English sentences. This is the indeterminacy of translation, and its bite is that no further fact — no meaning hidden in the native's head — settles which manual is correct. The most famous corollary concerns reference: when the native says "gavagai" as a rabbit passes, the evidence cannot distinguish reference to rabbits from reference to undetached rabbit-parts or rabbit-stages, since the apparatus of individuation is itself translated only relative to a scheme. This is the inscrutability of reference, and it holds of one's own language too, except that at home one acquiesces in the mother tongue and takes its terms at face value. Ontological relativity follows: it makes sense to ask what a theory's objects are only relative to a background theory and a chosen manual of translation into it; absolute questions about reference are empty, and the regress ends in practice, not in principle. This is not skepticism about the world but a claim that all possible evidence underdetermines semantics, which Quine took to show that the intensional idiom of meaning, synonymy, and proposition has no place in a scientific description of the world.

Naturalized epistemology

The traditional epistemologist sought a foundation for science outside science: Descartes in the cogito, the empiricists in sense-data, Carnap in a rational reconstruction of physical objects from experience. "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969) declares the project dead. The Cartesian quest for certainty failed; the reconstruction of body-talk from sense-talk was never carried out and, after holism, cannot be. The honest question left is scientific: how does a creature receiving a meager input at its sensory surfaces produce as output a torrential theory of the external world? That question belongs to psychology, and epistemology becomes a chapter of it, studying the relation between evidence and theory as one natural phenomenon among others. Quine did not think this viciously circular. Circularity would be a fault only if the aim were to validate science from without; once that aim is abandoned there is no better standpoint than science's own — Neurath's boat, rebuilt plank by plank at sea, is the governing image. Critics saw a change of subject: Kim argued that a descriptive psychology of belief-formation cannot replace a normative theory of justification, and Quine's later answer was that normativity survives as engineering — the technology of truth-seeking, judged by predictive success.

Extensionalism and the hostility to modality

Quine's canonical language is extensional: co-referring terms are everywhere interchangeable salva veritate, and a sentence's truth depends only on the extensions of its parts. Modal contexts violate this. "Nine is necessarily greater than seven" is true; "the number of planets is nine" was true; yet "the number of planets is necessarily greater than seven" is false. Substitution fails, and Quine's diagnosis is that necessity, so used, attaches to sentences under a description rather than to objects — a relic of analyticity, itself unexplained. Quantifying into such contexts, as quantified modal logic requires, would be to ask of an object whether it is necessarily thus-and-so however described, and that, Quine argued in "Reference and Modality," commits one to Aristotelian essentialism: the view that objects in themselves have some properties necessarily and others contingently. The illustration is the mathematical cyclist. Mathematicians are necessarily rational and only accidentally two-legged; cyclists are necessarily two-legged and only accidentally rational; a mathematician who cycles is then both necessarily rational and not — unless some fact fixes which of his descriptions gives his essence, and Quine saw nothing in a scientific worldview to supply one. The objection extended, more mildly, to propositional attitudes: belief contexts are opaque too, indispensable in practice but excluded from the austere language in which science's ontology is stated. Extensionalism was for Quine the price of clarity, held against the tide to the end.

Regimentation and canonical notation

Quine's positive method is regimentation: the paraphrase of scientific discourse into a canonical notation of first-order predicate logic with identity, whose variables range over physical objects and sets. The paraphrase is not a claim about the hidden meaning of ordinary sentences — that would reintroduce meanings — but a proposal, adopted for simplicity and clarity, keeping what the theory needs and discarding the rest. Names go by Russell's method; tense yields to four-dimensional objects; reference to properties, propositions, and possibilia disappears; belief and modality are set aside as foreign to the core. What survives is a language in which logical form is perspicuous, ontological commitment is read off directly, and first-order logic holds without exception. Quine's conservatism about logic follows. Classical first-order logic is uniquely recommended by its completeness, compactness, and intimacy with truth; second-order logic he called set theory in sheep's clothing, and deviant logics merely change the subject — whoever denies excluded middle has changed the meaning of "not." Yet holism keeps even logic revisable in principle, and Quine allowed that a revision for quantum mechanics could be entertained if overall simplicity warranted it; the tension between that revisability and the practical immunity of first-order logic is a standing puzzle in his system.

Influence and reception

Quine's deepest legacy is the naturalistic turn. That philosophy has no standpoint above science, that its problems are continuous with scientific ones, and that its methods are those of empirical inquiry, was a minority position in 1950 and is the mainstream of Anglophone philosophy today; the naturalism of most current philosophy of mind, epistemology, and metaphysics descends from him. Davidson turned radical translation into radical interpretation and built on truth and charity a theory of meaning Quine regarded as a friendly development; Putnam and Goodman absorbed the criticism of analyticity; Dennett, Fodor, and the Churchlands carried naturalized epistemology into philosophy of mind; and the indispensability argument set the terms of the philosophy of mathematics for fifty years, with Field's fictionalism written expressly to defeat it.

The pushback was as consequential as the influence. Grice and Strawson's "In Defense of a Dogma" (1956) argued that the analytic–synthetic distinction is shown genuine by the broad agreement with which competent speakers apply it to new cases — a distinction reliably drawn cannot be illusory — and that Quine's demand for a reductive definition sets an unreasonable standard. Carnap replied that for constructed languages the distinction was a matter of explication, not discovery. Chomsky, in "Quine's Empirical Assumptions" (1969), charged that the indeterminacy of translation either collapses into ordinary underdetermination of theory by evidence, which afflicts physics too and threatens nothing, or else presupposes a behaviorism about language that linguistics had discredited; Quine's answer — physics is underdetermined but true, translation has no fact of the matter to be true of — struck many as an unargued asymmetry. The most damaging reversal came from Kripke. Naming and Necessity (1970) separated necessity from analyticity and a priority, defended essentialism through rigid designation, and showed quantified modal logic coherent without the mathematical cyclist's dilemma. The result was the return of the modal metaphysics Quine had spent decades holding at bay, in a form — Lewis's possible-worlds realism — that ironically wields Quine's own criterion of commitment on behalf of possibilia. Kim's normativity objection and Boghossian's defense of analyticity kept alive the questions "Two Dogmas" seemed to close; Fodor and Lepore argued that meaning holism makes communication and belief-change unintelligible, turning the web of belief against itself. Quine answered nearly all of it, conceded on details, and revised nothing central.

Reading path

Begin with "On What There Is" and "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" in From a Logical Point of View: both are short, argue in the open, and remain the entry to the whole debate; read "Reference and Modality" from the same volume against the opening of Kripke's Naming and Necessity to hear the modal quarrel in both voices. Word and Object is the system: chapters 1–2 for radical translation and indeterminacy, chapters 5–6 for regimentation and the flight from intension. Then "Epistemology Naturalized" and "Ontological Relativity" from the 1969 collection. The Web of Belief (with Ullian) is an unforbidding textbook statement of holism; Pursuit of Truth the best late summary; Quintessence collects the essential essays. Secondary: Hylton's Quine as the most reliable systematic study; the Gibson Cambridge Companion; Grice and Strawson, Chomsky, and Kim as the indispensable opposition.

Assessment

Editorially: Quine's position is one of the few in the modern canon that can be held whole, and its wholeness is both its strength and its trap. Every doctrine supports every other — holism undoes analyticity, which undoes necessity, which undoes essentialism, leaving an extensional world of objects and sets described by first-order science — so a single rejected premise threatens the whole. Kripke's separation of necessity from analyticity was such a premise, and the tradition followed him; yet the naturalism survives, and on the question that mattered most to Quine — whether philosophy has any authority not borrowed from the sciences — his answer remains the one his opponents must still argue against.
