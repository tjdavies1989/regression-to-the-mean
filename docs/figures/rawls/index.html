John Rawls

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-09-09.15

1921–2002 · American political philosopher in the social-contract and Kantian tradition; author of A Theory of Justice and re-founder of normative political philosophy in the analytic world

Why they matter

By mid-century Anglophone political philosophy was widely thought to be finished: positivism had ruled evaluative claims empty, and what remained was analysis of words like "authority," or the history of dead systems. A Theory of Justice (1971) ended that by example. It set out a full, argued theory of a just society — principles, a procedure that generates them, a method for testing them, an account of their stability — in a form that invited replies at every joint. The replies became the field: libertarian, communitarian, feminist, Marxist, cosmopolitan, and racial-contract critiques are all, at their origin, responses to Rawls. The original position and the veil of ignorance are now common property; the difference principle is the most-discussed distributive principle since utility; reflective equilibrium is the method most moral philosophers admit to using. His later turn to stability under deep disagreement — Political Liberalism — set a second agenda, on legitimacy and public reason, still open.

Life

Rawls was born in Baltimore in 1921, the second of five sons of a successful lawyer and a mother active in the suffrage movement. Two brothers died in childhood of diseases contracted from him, a fact he never discussed and which biographers connect to his lifelong stammer and to the mature work's theme of undeserved fortune. At Princeton a senior thesis on sin and faith showed a devout Episcopalian considering the priesthood. He enlisted as an infantryman in 1943 and served in New Guinea, the Philippines, and occupied Japan, where he saw the aftermath of Hiroshima; a chaplain's sermon assuring the troops that God aimed their bullets at the Japanese, and news of the Holocaust, undid his faith. He declined a commission and left the army a private. He rarely wrote of these years — a late essay condemning the bombings, a posthumous note on his loss of faith — but the theory's gravity about moral arbitrariness bears their imprint.

He took a Princeton doctorate (1950), spent a Fulbright year at Oxford among Hart, Berlin, and Hampshire, and taught at Cornell and MIT before settling at Harvard in 1962 for good. The life was deliberately quiet: he refused most honors and nearly all interviews, and treated his students — many of whom became the field's leaders — with a deference they found almost excessive. He married Margaret Fox in 1949; they had four children. Strokes from 1995 slowed but did not stop his revising; he died at home in Lexington, Massachusetts, in 2002.

Works

  • The early papers (1951–58) — "Outline of a Decision Procedure for Ethics" (the germ of reflective equilibrium), "Two Concepts of Rules," and "Justice as Fairness" (1958), the first statement of the contract idea and the two principles.
  • A Theory of Justice (1971; revised 1999) — the system entire: original position, two principles, the contract argument, the stability of a well-ordered society.
  • "Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory" (the Dewey Lectures, 1980) — the method's self-understanding; the coinage named a metaethical position.
  • Political Liberalism (1993; expanded 1996, 2005) — the reframing: justice as fairness as a political conception, overlapping consensus, public reason. "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited" (1997) completes it.
  • The Law of Peoples (1999) — the theory extended, and pointedly not extended, to relations among societies.
  • Justice as Fairness: A Restatement (2001) — the compact late version, absorbing three decades of replies.
  • The lecture courses on the history of moral (2000) and political (2007) philosophy — Rawls reading Hume, Kant, Hegel, Rousseau, and Mill as his real interlocutors.

Thought

The original position and the veil of ignorance

Rawls's opening claim is that justice is the first virtue of institutions, as truth is of theories, and that its primary subject is the basic structure — constitution, economy, family: the institutions that fix people's life prospects before they have done anything. To find its principles he revives a device Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, and Kant had used and the nineteenth century discarded: agreement. Principles of justice are those free and rational persons would choose, in an initial situation of equality, to govern their common life. The situation is the original position, and its defining feature is the veil of ignorance: the parties know the general facts of social science but nothing of their own class, race, sex, talents, or conception of the good. Since no one can tailor principles to their advantage, the agreement is fair — hence justice as fairness.

The device does two kinds of work. As a model it turns a question about justice into one about rational choice under uncertainty. As a moral idea it enforces impartiality of a specific kind: not the utilitarian's sympathetic spectator, who sums everyone's interests into one, but a plurality of distinct persons each of whom must be able to accept the result. Here lies Rawls's case against utilitarianism, the reigning view he set out to displace. Utilitarianism extends the principle of rational choice for one person — accept losses now for greater gains later — to society as a whole, treating persons as channels through which utility flows rather than as separate lives with their own claims; it does not take seriously the distinction between persons. The original position is built so that it must: no party will gamble their basic liberties on the chance of belonging to the majority whose satisfaction would justify the sacrifice.

The two principles and the difference principle

The parties, Rawls argues, would choose two principles in strict order. The first requires an equal scheme of basic liberties — political liberty, conscience, association, the person, the rule of law — for each, compatible with the same for all. The second permits social and economic inequalities only if they attach to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity and work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged — the difference principle. Liberty has lexical priority over the second principle, and opportunity over the difference principle: no gain in wealth can purchase a loss of liberty. Advantage is measured in primary goods — liberties, opportunities, income, wealth, the social bases of self-respect: the all-purpose means any rational plan of life needs.

The difference principle is the theory's signature and its most contested argument. Its central thought is that the distribution of natural talents, and of the circumstances that develop them, is arbitrary from a moral point of view — no one deserves their starting position — and a society that lets the natural lottery fix shares merely ratifies luck. Meritocracy is not the remedy but a refinement of the problem: it corrects for class and leaves the lottery of talent untouched. The difference principle treats the distribution of talents as a common asset, letting the talented reap gains only on terms that benefit those who lost the draw. The formal argument is that parties behind the veil, unable to assign probabilities and facing outcomes they could not live with, would reason by maximin, choosing the scheme whose worst position is best. Harsanyi and others objected that maximin is an irrational rule; Rawls's restatements lean less on decision theory and more on publicity: principles must be justifiable to everyone, including the worst off, who cannot accept one that sacrifices them for aggregate gain. The institutional upshot, he insisted, was not the welfare state but a property-owning democracy in which capital and skills are dispersed in advance.

Reflective equilibrium and Kantian constructivism

How is such a theory justified, if there is no moral fact to discover and no axiom to derive from? By reflective equilibrium. We begin with considered judgments — convictions held with confidence under conditions favorable to judgment: that slavery is unjust, that religious persecution is wrong. We formulate principles that would account for them, and where the two conflict we revise in whichever direction seems, on reflection, more compelling — dropping a judgment the principle exposes as prejudice, or a principle whose implications we cannot accept — until they cohere. Justification is neither top-down nor bottom-up but mutual; the original position itself is defended this way, as modeling premises about fairness we already hold. Daniels's widening of the method to include background theories is what Rawls actually practices.

The Dewey Lectures of 1980 gave the method a name and a lineage. Kantian constructivism holds that principles of justice are not discovered as facts about an independent moral order, as rational intuitionism held, but constructed through a procedure that expresses a conception of the person: citizens as free and equal, possessing two moral powers — a capacity for a sense of justice and a capacity to form, revise, and pursue a conception of the good. The procedure's authority is that conception's authority for us. This was Rawls's reading of Kant's autonomy: not a noumenal self legislating from outside nature, but persons bound only by principles they would give themselves under conditions expressing their freedom and equality. The reading is deliberately detranscendentalized — Kant without the two-standpoints metaphysics — and it seeded a metaethical position Korsgaard developed far beyond Rawls's own, more modest, ambitions.

Political Liberalism: reasonable pluralism, overlapping consensus, public reason

A Theory of Justice had argued that a well-ordered society would be stable because its members, raised under just institutions, would find justice congruent with their good — quietly assuming that everyone shared Kantian liberalism as a comprehensive philosophy of life. By the 1980s Rawls judged the assumption false and the falsity important. Free institutions produce, and will always produce, a diversity of reasonable comprehensive doctrines — religious, philosophical, moral — that cannot be reconciled, not because people are foolish but because of the burdens of judgment: evidence conflicts, concepts are vague, lives differ. This is the fact of reasonable pluralism, and a liberalism resting on one comprehensive doctrine — Kant's autonomy, or Mill's individuality — would be simply another sectarian faith, maintained, if at all, by the oppressive use of state power.

Political Liberalism reconceives justice as fairness as a political conception: confined to the basic structure, freestanding from any comprehensive view, drawn from ideas implicit in democratic public culture. Stability is then secured by an overlapping consensus, in which citizens of different comprehensive doctrines each affirm the political conception from within their own — the Kantian because it honors autonomy, the Catholic because it protects conscience, the utilitarian because it promotes welfare — rather than by a modus vivendi that shifts with the balance of forces. The liberal principle of legitimacy follows: political power is legitimate only when exercised under a constitution whose essentials all citizens, as reasonable, may be expected to endorse. From legitimacy comes public reason: on constitutional essentials and basic justice, citizens and officials should argue in terms all can accept as free and equal, not from doctrines others reasonably reject — loosened later by a proviso allowing comprehensive reasons so long as public reasons are in due course supplied. The contrast with Mill illuminates both: Mill's liberalism is comprehensive, defending liberty because it produces a certain kind of human being; Rawls's is political, refusing to say which kind is best, since a free people will never agree.

The Law of Peoples

Rawls's last book extends the contract to relations among societies and refuses the extension nearly everyone expected. The parties to a second original position are not individuals but peoples — liberal societies, and decent non-liberal ones that respect basic human rights and consult their members — and what they choose are familiar principles of international law: independence, non-intervention, fidelity to treaties, just war, and a duty of assistance to burdened societies unable to sustain decent institutions. What they do not choose is a global difference principle. Rawls's reasons: a people's wealth depends chiefly on its political culture, not its resources, so cross-border redistribution would subsidize bad governance; assistance has a target and a cutoff — decent institutions — beyond which inequalities among peoples are not unjust; and liberal peoples must tolerate decent hierarchical ones rather than treat their illiberalism as a fault to correct. The cosmopolitan critics — Beitz and Pogge, who had globalized the original position already in the 1970s — objected that the theory's own logic overruns its border: birthplace is as arbitrary as talent, a global basic structure already shapes life prospects, and making peoples rather than persons the parties abandons the theory's own individualism. Statist defenders (Nagel, Blake) reply that egalitarian justice is triggered by shared coercive authority exercised in one's name, which no global institution has.

Influence and reception

The field can be drawn as a map of Rawls's critics, each marking a boundary of the program. Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) is the libertarian reply: justice is historical, not patterned — holdings are just if justly acquired and transferred — and treating talents as a common asset asserts a claim on persons that self-ownership forbids. Sandel's Liberalism and the Limits of Justice (1982) opened the communitarian front: the original position presupposes an "unencumbered self" prior to all its ends, whereas real persons are partly constituted by community and history, and a politics built on the abstraction cannot sustain the solidarity the difference principle needs — an objection Political Liberalism largely exists to answer. Okin's Justice, Gender, and the Family (1989) accepted the theory and pressed its blind spot: Rawls names the family as part of the basic structure and then exempts it from the principles, though it is where the sense of justice is formed and gendered inequality reproduced; taken seriously, the veil would hide one's sex. G. A. Cohen, from the egalitarian left, argued in Rescuing Justice and Equality (2008) that the difference principle's tolerance of incentive-based inequality concedes to the talented's refusal to work for less, which a just ethos — not merely a just basic structure — would forbid. Mills's The Racial Contract (1997) and the essays on ideal theory charged that a theory beginning from a well-ordered society under full compliance idealizes away the racial domination that actually structured the societies Rawls wrote for, and cannot diagnose it.

Other engagements shaped the view as much as they opposed it. Hart's 1973 critique of the priority of liberty led Rawls to rebuild the first principle around a list of basic liberties tied to the two moral powers; Habermas's 1995 exchange pitted discourse theory against the contract. Sen and Nussbaum's capabilities approach began as an objection to primary goods; Dworkin's luck egalitarianism as a reading of what the difference principle should have said; Scanlon's contractualism carried the method from institutions to interpersonal morality. Recent scholarship has turned skeptical: Forrester's In the Shadow of Justice (2019) reads the Rawlsian consensus as a contingent product of postwar American liberalism, and political realists (Williams, Geuss) charge the program with moralism — politics as applied ethics, power neglected.

Reading path

Begin not with the Theory but with Justice as Fairness: A Restatement (2001): short, clear, the view as Rawls finally wished it stated. Then A Theory of Justice in the revised edition — Part One (chapters 1–3) holds the original position and the argument for the two principles; Part Three, on stability, is what Rawls later rethought. Then Political Liberalism in the expanded edition, Lectures I, IV, and VI first. The Law of Peoples is brief and stands alone. Among the critics, Nozick's chapter 7, Sandel's chapter 1, and Okin's chapter 5 are each one sitting. Secondary: Freeman's Rawls is the standard guide, Pogge's John Rawls the best short life, Forrester's In the Shadow of Justice the historical corrective.

Assessment

Editorially: Rawls's achievement is less any principle than the demonstration that a systematic, contestable theory of justice could be built at all, and his later work is the rare case of a philosopher rethinking a masterpiece's foundations under criticism rather than defending them. Whether the retreat from comprehensive to political liberalism strengthened the view or hollowed it — a theory everyone reasonable can affirm precisely because it no longer says why — is the question the next generation inherited and has not settled. The difference principle is a fair index of the man: the most humane distributive idea of its century, from an author who regarded his own good fortune, and others' misfortune, as nothing anyone earned.
