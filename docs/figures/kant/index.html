Immanuel Kant

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-21.2

1724–1804 · German philosopher of the Enlightenment; author of the critical philosophy and hinge of the modern tradition

Why they matter

Kant is the hinge on which modern philosophy turns: the contest between rationalism and empiricism reads in retrospect as leading up to him, the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as a series of reactions to him. He did not win that contest for either side; he changed the question. Instead of asking what the world is like and whether our faculties can reach it, the critical philosophy asks what conditions make experience and knowledge possible at all — and answers that objects must conform to our cognition, not cognition to objects. That reversal generated a new kind of argument (the transcendental kind), a new diagnosis of metaphysics' perennial failures, an ethics founded on autonomy rather than God or sentiment, an aesthetics built around a genuinely strange datum, and a political cosmopolitanism whose institutions were eventually attempted. Nearly every division of this tree must cite him; only Aristotle is comparably unavoidable.

Life

Kant was born in 1724 in Königsberg, East Prussia, a harness-maker's son, raised in a devout Pietist household whose moral seriousness he kept after shedding its devotions. He entered the university at sixteen, worked as a house tutor, and lectured as an unsalaried Privatdozent for fifteen years before obtaining the chair of logic and metaphysics in 1770. His inaugural dissertation opened the "silent decade": the Critique of Pure Reason appeared only in 1781, when he was fifty-seven, and the rest of the system tumbled out in the twenty years remaining. The outward life was proverbially regular — the daily walk by which neighbors allegedly set their clocks — and narrow: he never married and never traveled more than a day from Königsberg, while lecturing vividly on the geography of the whole world. In 1794 the Prussian king censured his writings on religion; Kant promised silence and considered the promise lapsed when the king died. He died in 1804; his last recorded words were "It is good."

Works

  • Critique of Pure Reason (1781; revised 1787) — metaphysics tried before the tribunal of reason itself: what cognition can and cannot reach, and why.
  • Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics (1783) — the first Critique's argument reorganized, shorter and sharper, for the readers the original had defeated.
  • Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785) — the supreme principle of morality sought, formulated, and grounded in autonomy.
  • Critique of Practical Reason (1788) — morality's rational credentials secured on their own footing; freedom, God, and immortality as practical postulates.
  • Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790) — taste and natural teleology, offered as the bridge between nature and freedom.
  • Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason (1793), Toward Perpetual Peace (1795), Metaphysics of Morals (1797) — the system applied: rational religion, international right, the doctrines of right and virtue.
  • The lectures on anthropology and physical geography (in part Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View, 1798, with the race essays of 1775–88) — the empirical study of humanity, containing the racial theorizing weighed below.

Thought

The Copernican turn and the synthetic a priori

Hume, Kant said, woke him from dogmatic slumber by showing that the concept of cause — necessary connection — can never be extracted from experience, which delivers only succession. Kant generalized the crisis. Judgments divide along two axes: analytic or synthetic (does the predicate merely unpack the subject, or add to it?), a priori or a posteriori. Analytic a priori judgments are trivial; synthetic a posteriori ones, ordinary empirical knowledge. But mathematics, the fundamental principles of natural science, and metaphysics all claim to be synthetic a priori — informative about the world, yet known with a necessity no accumulation of experience could supply. How is that possible? The first Critique exists to answer this question. Kant's hypothesis, on the analogy of Copernicus, reverses the assumed direction of fit: suppose objects must conform to the constitution of our cognitive faculties, rather than cognition to objects. Then synthetic a priori knowledge is possible exactly where it concerns the conditions any object must meet to be experienceable by us — and impossible everywhere else. The price of explaining a priori knowledge is confining it to appearances.

Transcendental idealism

The doctrine that pays that price. Space and time, Kant argues, are not things, nor relations among things in themselves, but forms of human sensibility — the standing structure into which all sensory material must be received. Hence geometry's a priori grip on everything perceivable, and hence the doctrine's two faces: empirical realism (spatiotemporal objects are fully real and knowable) with transcendental ideality (things as they are in themselves are unknowable). What this amounts to is the central interpretive dispute of Kant scholarship. The two-world reading takes appearances and things in themselves as distinct realms, appearances being in some sense mind-dependent constructions — on which Kant looks uncomfortably close to the Berkeleyan idealism he indignantly disavowed. The two-aspect reading, pressed most influentially by Allison, takes them as one set of things considered two ways: as subject to our epistemic conditions, and in abstraction from them. The dispute decides whether Kant's position is a substantive (and perhaps incoherent) metaphysics or a deflationary methodological discipline. Both must answer Jacobi's objection — things in themselves are said to affect our sensibility, though causal concepts apply only within appearances: without the thing in itself one cannot enter the system, with it one cannot remain.

The transcendental deduction

The categories — pure concepts of the understanding such as substance and cause — need a warrant of their own, since Hume had shown they cannot be read off experience. The transcendental deduction, the book's hardest stretch, has an aim stateable plainly: to show that the categories legitimately apply to everything we can experience, because they are the conditions under which a mere stream of representations can amount to experience of an objective world at all — appearances unified enough to belong to one self-conscious subject and to present objects distinct from that subject's states. The keystone is the unity of apperception: the "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations, and that unity is possible only through synthesis governed by the categories. The Second Analogy then cashes the strategy against Hume directly: distinguishing an objective sequence of events from an arbitrary sequence of perceptions itself presupposes causal order. Causation is not gleaned from experience; it is what makes experience of events possible. The argument does not pretend to reach beyond experience — that is its point — but within experience it claims a non-negotiable structure, and it has served ever since as the model for answering skepticism by interrogating what the skeptic's own experience presupposes.

The antinomies and the fate of metaphysics

The Dialectic diagnoses why metaphysics, unlike the sciences, never progresses. Reason inherently seeks the unconditioned — the complete series of conditions for anything conditioned — generating ideas (the soul, the world-whole, God) answering to no possible experience. The antinomies are the diagnosis dramatized: four pairs of contradictory theses, each provable by apparently sound argument — the world has a beginning in time and has none; matter is ultimately simple and infinitely divisible; there is causality through freedom and only natural necessity; a necessary being exists and does not. Kant's resolution: both members of each pair presuppose that appearances are things in themselves; drop that assumption and the mathematical antinomies dissolve (both sides false), while the dynamical ones admit of both sides being true in different registers — nature thoroughly causal among appearances, freedom possible among things in themselves. The same critique demolishes the theoretical proofs of God's existence, with the famous verdict that existence is not a real predicate. Metaphysics as a natural disposition is ineradicable; as a science of the supersensible it is impossible, its ideas surviving only as regulative principles guiding inquiry. Kant described the upshot without embarrassment: knowledge denied to make room for faith — a rational commitment whose warrant is practical.

Autonomy and the moral law

The Groundwork opens by locating the only thing good without qualification — a good will — and asks what principle such a will acts on. Moral requirements are categorical imperatives: binding regardless of what one happens to want, unlike the hypothetical imperatives of skill and prudence. There is at bottom one, in formulas Kant took to be equivalent: act only on maxims you could will as universal laws; treat humanity, in yourself or another, never merely as a means but always at the same time as an end; act as a legislating member of a kingdom of ends. The second formula grounds respect for persons: rational agents have dignity, not price — a worth admitting no exchange — which is why deception and coercion wrong people even when the ledger of consequences favors them. The deepest doctrine is autonomy: the moral law is not imposed by God, nature, or sentiment — groundings Kant condemns as heteronomy — but is the rational will's own legislation, which is why its authority is inescapable. Freedom and the moral law reciprocally imply one another; where the first Critique could only show freedom possible, the second claims our consciousness of the moral law as a "fact of reason" revealing freedom actual. Hence the primacy of practical reason: on questions speculation must leave open — freedom, God, immortality — practical reason licenses rational commitment, as postulates of moral life rather than items of knowledge.

The third Critique: taste and teleology

The Critique of the Power of Judgment begins from a datum most theories flatten: the judgment of taste. Calling something beautiful is not reporting a private agreeableness — we demand agreement and treat dissenters as missing something — yet neither is it applying a concept, for no rule or proof can establish beauty. Kant preserves both faces: the judgment is grounded in disinterested pleasure arising from the free, harmonious play of imagination and understanding, and because these faculties are common to all judgers, the pleasure can be imputed universally. Beauty thus claims universality without concepts, speaking with a "universal voice" that presupposes a common sense while never becoming demonstrable. The book's second half turns to organisms, which confound the mechanical explanation the first Critique licensed: their parts seem to exist for the sake of the whole and through it, and Kant holds that we cannot make biological nature intelligible except through the concept of a natural purpose. Yet purposiveness is a regulative principle only — a necessity of our discursive understanding, not a discovered property — so we must judge organisms as if designed while forever barred from converting that maxim into knowledge of design. Judgment, spanning beauty and life, thereby gestures at the unity of the nature the first Critique described and the freedom the second demanded.

Right, republics, and perpetual peace

Kant derives the state from freedom rather than welfare. Each person has one innate right — independence from another's choice, insofar as it can coexist with everyone's like independence under universal law — and the state exists to make rightful coexistence possible, its social contract an idea of reason rather than a historical event. A legitimate constitution is republican: separation of powers, rule of law, citizens as co-legislators. Toward Perpetual Peace extends right beyond the state in three articles: republican constitutions (peoples who bear war's costs will not vote for it lightly — ancestor of the democratic-peace hypothesis); a federation of free states in place of a world government Kant feared as despotism; and a minimal cosmopolitan right of hospitality, wielded explicitly against European colonial conquest. Publicity supplies a test of political maxims, and "What Is Enlightenment?" makes the public use of reason the engine of a maturity mankind must dare. The League of Nations and the United Nations were built, at second hand, from this pamphlet.

Influence and reception

The immediate aftermath was an explosion launched from Kant's gaps. Jacobi's objection to the thing in itself set the problem, and Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel successively radicalized the critical philosophy into German idealism, each holding that Kant had stopped arbitrarily — Hegel's version being that examining the instrument of knowledge before using it is itself a use of it. Schopenhauer took the opposite exit, keeping the thing in itself and naming it will. When speculative idealism collapsed, "back to Kant" became the slogan of neo-Kantianism, which ruled German universities for two generations: the Marburg school (Cohen, Natorp, Cassirer) reading Kant as the philosopher of mathematical science, the Southwest school (Windelband, Rickert) as the philosopher of validity and value. Analytic philosophy defined itself partly against him: relativity seemed to refute the a priori status of geometry, and the logical empiricists made eliminating the synthetic a priori a founding plank, before Quine turned the same solvent on analyticity itself. Strawson's The Bounds of Sense (1966) then re-founded analytic Kantianism by partition: the deduction and analogies contain profound, detachable insights about the conditions of self-conscious experience, while transcendental idealism is an incoherent metaphor — a severing Allison's two-aspect reading was built to resist. In ethics, Hegel's charge of empty formalism echoes through Schiller, Williams, and Anscombe; yet Kantian ethics is today as alive as any program, in Korsgaard's and O'Neill's constructivism and above all in Rawls, whose justice as fairness is offered as a procedural interpretation of the categorical imperative — autonomy institutionalized as the original position.

Reception now also reckons with the anthropology. In his geography and anthropology lectures and the essays of 1775–88, Kant produced one of the first systematic theories of race — a hierarchy with whites at its summit, attributing to Africans and Native Americans fixed deficits of talent, alongside remarks of plain contempt. The debate, opened forcefully by Eze, Bernasconi, and Mills, concerns this material's relation to the universalism of the ethics. One line holds the racism is no excrescence: if some humans are tacitly discounted as full persons, the "humanity" formula is corrupted at the source — Mills read Kant as legislating for persons while classifying some peoples below that threshold. Kleingeld has argued from the texts that Kant's views changed in the 1790s: the late condemnations of colonialism and slavery in Perpetual Peace and the Metaphysics of Morals sit badly with the race essays and suggest a genuine, if unannounced, revision. A third position accepts the fact without extenuation and notes its structure: the ethics supplies precisely the standard by which the anthropology stands condemned, so the doctrine indicts its author. No serious party now omits the material; it was taught, in his voice, for forty years.

Reading path

Begin with the Groundwork — short, self-contained, the strongest dose of Kant per page — then the Prolegomena as the intended on-ramp to the first Critique. In the Critique of Pure Reason (Guyer–Wood translation), read the B preface, the Aesthetic, the Second Analogy, and the antinomies before attempting the deduction. Then the Analytic of the Beautiful from the third Critique, and Toward Perpetual Peace with "What Is Enlightenment?" — readable in an evening. Secondary: Gardner's guidebook to the first Critique for orientation; Allison's Kant's Transcendental Idealism and Strawson's The Bounds of Sense as the poles of the interpretive dispute; Korsgaard's Creating the Kingdom of Ends for the ethics; Kleingeld, Bernasconi, and Mills for the race debate.

Assessment

Editorially: Kant's reversal is the most consequential change of question in the discipline's history — even philosophers who reject every Kantian doctrine argue in the space it opened. The persistence of the two-world/two-aspect dispute suggests the system's center never quite held; the persistence of the system despite that suggests how little it mattered. And the hardest recent debate has a clean edge: the ethics is the standard by which the anthropology lectures are condemned — a vindication of the philosophy and none of the man.
