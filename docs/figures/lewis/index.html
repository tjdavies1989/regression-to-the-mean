David Lewis

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.3

1941–2001 · American analytic philosopher; the reinventor of systematic metaphysics

Why they matter

Mid-century analytic philosophy had largely concluded, under Quine's and the positivists' tutelage, that grand metaphysical system-building was over. Lewis rebuilt it with the analytic tradition's own tools. From a handful of primitives — concrete possible worlds, set theory, mereology, and a distinction between natural and gerrymandered properties — he constructed unified accounts of modality, counterfactuals, causation, laws, chance, mind, meaning, and knowledge, each analysis feeding the others, the whole assessed as a scientist assesses a theory: costs weighed against benefits across the total system. Nearly every corner of contemporary metaphysics either uses his machinery or defines itself against it, and his conception of philosophy — systematizing our existing opinions at the lowest total cost — has become, often unacknowledged, the discipline's working method. He is the strongest modern case study in a permanent question: what do we owe a theory whose explanatory power is immense and whose central claim almost no one can believe?

Life

Lewis was born in 1941 in Oberlin, Ohio, to two academics. He entered Swarthmore intending chemistry; a year at Oxford (1959–60), where he heard Ryle and was tutored by Iris Murdoch, converted him to philosophy. He took his doctorate at Harvard under Quine in 1967, with a dissertation, written against Quine's skepticism about meaning, that became Convention; at Harvard he met Stephanie Robinson, his wife and occasional co-author. After four years at UCLA he moved to Princeton in 1970 and never left. From 1971 he visited Australia almost yearly; the running argument with Armstrong, Smart, Jackson, and the Canberra and Sydney departments shaped his mature system more than any American interlocutor, and Australia adopted him as an honorary native. The life was outwardly quiet and inwardly exuberant: model railways, Australian-rules football, folk music, cats. He managed diabetes for decades and died of its complications in October 2001, at sixty — the system finished in outline, its postscripts still accumulating.

Works

  • Convention (1969) — the dissertation book: coordination, convention, and the first analysis of what makes a regularity a rule.
  • "Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic" (1968) and "How to Define Theoretical Terms" (1970) — the method-setting papers: modality traded for worlds and counterparts; theoretical vocabulary defined by Ramsey sentences.
  • Counterfactuals (1973) — the similarity semantics for "if things had been otherwise," with "Causation" (1973) as its most consequential application.
  • Philosophical Papers I–II (1983, 1986) — the collected engine-room, including "New Work for a Theory of Universals" and "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance"; the volume II introduction names and defends Humean supervenience.
  • On the Plurality of Worlds (1986) — the magnum opus: modal realism stated, priced, and defended against every rival then on the market.
  • Parts of Classes (1991) — set theory reconstructed mereologically.
  • The Cambridge papers (three volumes, 1998–2000) — the late harvest, including "Elusive Knowledge" and "Causation as Influence."

Thought

Modal realism

Lewis's most notorious doctrine is his most straightforward: possible worlds exist, and they are worlds — concrete, spatiotemporally unified universes of the same kind as ours, differing only in what happens in them. No world is privileged; "actual" is an indexical like "here," true at each world of that world alone. Worlds neither overlap nor stand in any spatiotemporal or causal relation, and a principle of recombination — anything can coexist with anything, size and shape permitting — guarantees plenitude. What this buys is the only genuinely reductive analysis of modality anyone has produced: "possibly P" means "at some world, P," with no modal residue in the analysans. And modality is just the first customer: propositions become sets of worlds; properties, classes of possibilia; counterfactuals, claims about similar worlds; content, a region in logical space. Lewis knew how the theory strikes people — he named the incredulous stare himself, conceded it was the theory's greatest cost, and insisted it be entered in the ledger as a cost, not treated as a refutation. His positive argument is serviceability: the hypothesis is priced like the mathematician's paradise of sets, an ontological outlay repaid in analytic power everywhere else in the system. The ersatz alternatives — worlds as maximal consistent sets of sentences, or as abstract states of affairs — either smuggle modality back in ("consistent"), cannot represent alien possibilities, or posit a primitive representation relation more magical than his plurality. Almost nobody followed him; almost everybody uses the machinery; whether the machinery can be had without the metaphysics is the standing question of the field.

Counterpart theory

If worlds do not overlap, nothing exists at more than one world, and de re modality — Humphrey might have won the election — needs a surrogate for transworld identity. Lewis's answer: you have counterparts, inhabitants of other worlds who resemble you more closely than anything else there does. "Humphrey could have won" is true because some counterpart of Humphrey wins. Kripke's famous objection — Humphrey couldn't care less whether someone else, however similar, wins in another world — Lewis took to rest on a misreading: the counterpart's victory is not a consolation prize but precisely what Humphrey's possible victory consists in. The theory's real dividend is the inconstancy of de re modality. Counterpart relations are similarity relations — respect-relative and context-sensitive — so the statue and the lump of clay, one thing on Lewis's view, can differ modally because "statue" and "lump" invoke different counterpart relations: modal difference without coincident objects. The same flexibility disarms puzzles of essence: how much of your origin is essential to you depends on which counterpart relation the conversation has made salient, which is why essentialist intuitions waver as questions are rephrased.

Counterfactuals and causation

Counterfactuals gave the conditional "if it had been that A, it would have been that C" its modern semantics: true just in case some world where A and C both hold is closer to actuality than any world where A holds without C. Closeness is similarity, weighted to avoid big miracles, maximize perfect match of particular fact, and tolerate small miracles — an ordering designed to fix the past and open the future counterfactually without building in time's arrow by hand. The semantics explains why strengthening the antecedent fails for counterfactuals, and it powered his account of causation, which took over Hume's neglected second definition: c causes e if e counterfactually depends on c, or is linked to c by a chain of such dependences. The analysis relocated causation from general regularities to the particular case — this striking caused this lighting, whatever happens elsewhere — and set the agenda for thirty years. Its nemesis was preemption: where a backup assassin guarantees the death, the effect does not depend on the actual cause. Lewis answered with chains, then quasi-dependence, finally "Causation as Influence" — causation as patterned dependence of the effect's manner and time on alterations of the cause — each repair conceding ground the structural-equations tradition would later occupy: an analysis simple enough to state in a sentence, defended through decades of epicycles because nothing else localized causation so well.

Humean supervenience

The system's unifying program is named for the great denier of necessary connections. Humean supervenience is the thesis that everything true of a world like ours supervenes on the mosaic: the spatiotemporal arrangement of perfectly natural, intrinsic properties instantiated at points — one little matter of local fact after another, nothing else. Laws, causation, chance, dispositions, minds, meanings: all patterns in the arrangement, none extra ingredients. The load-bearing analysis is the best-system account of laws, refined from Mill and Ramsey: the laws of nature are the generalizations belonging to the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength in describing the total history of the world. Laws thus govern nothing — they are the mosaic's most compressible summary — yet remain objective, since which system is best is a fact, not a choice, provided vocabularies are held to natural properties (else trivial systems win by gerrymandered predicates). Chance enters through the same door: objective probabilities are whatever the best system says, linked to belief by the Principal Principle — conform credence to known chance — the platitude any theory of chance must vindicate, and one his own satisfied only after amendment (the "big bad bug" of undermining futures). Anti-Humeans — Armstrong with laws as relations among universals, Maudlin with laws as primitive — objected that summaries cannot explain, support counterfactuals, or ground time's direction. Lewis's reply throughout: the extra ingredients are idle wheels, and everything worth wanting from necessity in nature can be recovered from the pattern.

Natural properties

"New Work for a Theory of Universals" (1983) supplied the primitive the whole edifice leans on. Properties, for Lewis, are abundant — any class of possibilia whatever — but some are elite: perfectly natural, carving reality at its joints, the ones physics is trying to inventory. Naturalness makes some resemblances genuine and others gerrymandered; it defines duplication, and thereby intrinsicness; it fixes the best system's vocabulary, and thereby laws; and as reference magnetism it constrains interpretation — among meaning-assignments that fit our usage equally well, the more natural candidate wins, Lewis's answer to Putnam's argument that reference is radically indeterminate. He stayed neutral on whether naturalness is primitive or earned by universals or tropes; what he insisted on, against his own earlier Quinean scruples, is that no systematic philosophy can do without the distinction. The concession reshaped the field: the properties debate moved from whether universals exist to what naturalness is and how much weight one primitive may bear — and Lewis's answer, in practice, was: all of it.

Convention, language, and the score

Convention answered a challenge of Quine's: how can language be governed by convention if conventions are agreements and agreement presupposes language? Drawing on Schelling, Lewis analyzed convention without agreement: a convention is a regularity in a recurrent coordination problem — driving on the right — that sustains itself because everyone conforms, expects conformity, and prefers to conform given that others do, when an alternative regularity could have served instead. Language fits the mold: a population uses a language L by sustaining a convention of truthfulness and trust in L — asserting only what is true in L, believing accordingly — which is what ties abstract semantic systems to actual populations. The later "Scorekeeping in a Language Game" added dynamics: conversation keeps score, and presuppositions, standards of precision, and salience shift to accommodate what speakers say — an idea that seeded formal pragmatics and Lewis's contextualism about knowledge, on which "S knows that P" holds just when S's evidence eliminates every possibility of error the context does not properly ignore.

Bridges: language.meaning, language.pragmatics, language.context

The Canberra plan and the mind

From "How to Define Theoretical Terms" comes the method his Australian allies made a program. Take a theory — folk psychology, say — as a long conjunction; replace its theoretical terms with variables to form its Ramsey sentence; the terms are then implicitly defined as occupants of the roles the theory specifies. Pain is whatever state is typically caused by damage and causes distress and avoidance. Philosophy's task, on this picture, is location: collect the platitudes fixing a folk concept's role, then find what in the scientific image occupies it. For the mind the result is an analytic functionalism issuing in type identity: the pain role is defined a priori, and science discovers that neural states occupy it — in humans; in Martians, something else, since occupancy is relative to kind ("Mad Pain and Martian Pain" handles the deviant cases). The plan generalizes — Lewis and Jackson ran it on color, value, and free will (his "Are We Free to Break the Laws?" is compatibilism's sharpest modern statement) — and if nothing perfectly occupies a role, the near-enough occupant may claim the name, or the concept stands exposed as empty.

Method: costs, benefits, and equilibrium

Lewis's metaphilosophy is the system's quiet keystone. Philosophical theories, he held, are almost never refuted; they are priced. One comes to philosophy with a stock of existing opinions, and the task is neither to justify nor to overthrow them but to expand them into the most orderly total system available — each theory's benefits in unification and analysis measured against its costs in ontology and violence to common sense. Intuitions are simply opinions, defeasible evidence; a theory that wins on the central cases earns the right to legislate the unclear ones — "spoils to the victor" — so recalcitrant verdicts on exotic preemption cases need not sink an analysis that earns its keep elsewhere. The upshot is an equilibrium conception of progress: philosophy improves not by proofs but by ever-better-priced systems, and rational, permanent disagreement is an expected outcome, since starting opinions differ. Critics ask what disciplines the pricing — whose common sense, weighed how? — and whether the method can distinguish discovering truth from elegantly organizing our prejudices. It remains the closest thing analytic philosophy has to an official self-understanding.

Influence and reception

By his death Lewis was, by common consent, the most influential systematic metaphysician of the late twentieth century, rivaled in analytic philosophy's esteem only by Kripke — and unlike Kripke he left a complete system, which is why so much subsequent metaphysics reads as annotation to him. The counterfactual analysis of causation generated a literature that ended in the structural-equations frameworks of Pearl, Halpern, and Hitchcock; the best-system account remains the Humean pole of the laws debate against Armstrong and Maudlin; naturalness became, through Sider's extension to structure generally, the organizing primitive of much metametaphysics; the semantics of Counterfactuals (paralleled by Stalnaker) is standard equipment in linguistics; "Elusive Knowledge" founded epistemic contextualism; Convention seeded game-theoretic accounts of norms. The pushback is equally structural. Modal realism found almost no believers — Stalnaker, Plantinga, and the ersatzists offered actualist substitutes; van Inwagen and Forrest pressed cardinality and island-universe objections — leaving the awkward settlement his own methodology predicts: a system used daily by people who reject its foundation. Anti-Humeans object that a mosaic cannot explain its own patterns; hyperintensionalists (Fine on essence, Schaffer on grounding) argue that worlds cut too coarsely, and the grounding turn is in large part a revolt against Lewisian analysis. The deepest reservation is methodological: that cost-benefit systematizing, in hands as brilliant as his, optimizes for elegance rather than truth — a cathedral built on a premise its own architect could defend but not make believed.

Reading path

Start with "Causation" and "New Work for a Theory of Universals" (both in Philosophical Papers, with their postscripts): short, exemplary, the fastest route into the method. Then Counterfactuals chapter 1, and On the Plurality of Worlds — chapter 1 makes the case for the plurality, section 2.8 confronts the stare, chapter 3 demolishes the ersatz rivals; it is among the best-argued books in modern philosophy. The introduction to Philosophical Papers II states Humean supervenience and the equilibrium metaphilosophy in a few pages. Then by interest: Convention and "Languages and Language" for language; "Mad Pain and Martian Pain" for mind; "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance" for chance; "Elusive Knowledge" for epistemology. Secondary: Nolan's David Lewis as the accessible overview; Loewer and Schaffer's A Companion to David Lewis for the state of the arguments.

Assessment

Editorially: Lewis is the purest available experiment on whether philosophy can be run as theory choice, and the result is double-edged. No one has extracted more explanation from fewer primitives, and the system's honesty — every cost priced, including the fatal one — rebukes programs that hide theirs. But modal realism's fate suggests a limit of the method itself: when theories are priced rather than refuted, a conclusion supported by impeccable accounting can still find zero buyers, and Lewis's own equilibrium conception explains why without telling us who is right. The system survives as the field's common instrument and its cautionary tale at once.
