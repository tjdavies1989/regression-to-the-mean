Plato

◑ survey  ·  anchor  ·  layer 1  ·  pass 2026-08-26.3

c. 427–347 BCE · Athenian philosopher; student of Socrates, founder of the Academy, and builder of philosophy's first system

Why they matter

Plato is the first philosopher from whom a complete body of work survives, and the first to treat the discipline's problems as one problem: what is real, what can be known, and how to live turn, in his hands, on a single hinge — whether there are stable, intelligible objects behind the flux of appearance. His answer, the theory of Forms, is Western philosophy's founding metaphysical posit, and nearly every later position defines itself for or against it: realism about universals, mathematical platonism, rationalism about knowledge, the distrust of art, the dream of rule by expertise. He also built the machinery: the Academy is the ancestor of every university, and the dialogue form — philosophy as staged argument rather than delivered doctrine — wrote self-criticism into the subject at its origin.

Life

Plato was born around 427 BCE into an aristocratic Athenian family; relatives of his mother, Critias and Charmides, became leading members of the Thirty Tyrants, the junta that ruled Athens after its defeat by Sparta. The decisive events of his youth were negative: Athens's political collapse in the Peloponnesian War, the oligarchs' brutality, and above all the restored democracy's execution of Socrates in 399, which convinced him existing cities were beyond piecemeal cure. He traveled afterward to Megara and to southern Italy and Sicily, where contact with Pythagoreans, especially Archytas of Tarentum, left permanent marks: the mathematics, the immortal soul, philosophy as a shared, disciplined life. Around 387 he founded the Academy outside Athens, a research community whose members included Eudoxus, Theaetetus, and Aristotle, who studied there for twenty years. His one sustained attempt at practical politics — two returns to Syracuse, in 367 and 361, to make a philosopher of the tyrant Dionysius II — failed humiliatingly. He died in 347, writing to the end; the Laws was left unrevised.

Works

Everything Plato wrote for publication survives — a fortune granted to no other ancient philosopher — in roughly twenty-six dialogues plus letters of disputed authenticity. Plato appears in none as a speaker. Scholarship groups them, on stylistic and doctrinal evidence, into three loose phases:

  • Early ("Socratic") dialogues — Apology, Crito, Euthyphro, Protagoras, Gorgias: short, combative, dominated by Socrates' cross-examination (elenchus), typically ending in avowed perplexity about a virtue's definition.
  • Middle dialogues — Meno, Phaedo, Symposium, Republic, Phaedrus: the constructive system — Forms, recollection, the immortal tripartite soul, the ideal city — with Socrates now the mouthpiece of positive doctrine.
  • Late dialogues — Parmenides, Theaetetus, Sophist, Statesman, Philebus, Timaeus, Laws: self-criticism, new method, technical metaphysics; Socrates recedes, replaced by an Eleatic Stranger, the aged Parmenides, or the Athenian of the Laws.

Thought

The Forms: one over many

The theory of Forms answers a clutch of problems with a single posit. Many distinct things are beautiful, or equal, or just; what is the beautiful, in which they all share? Sensible instances are compresent with their opposites — the same act just in one context, unjust in another — so no sensible thing can be what justice is. And knowledge, as opposed to shifting opinion, needs objects that hold still. Plato's answer: over every such many stands one Form — Beauty itself, Equality itself — an eternal, changeless, non-sensible object that is what its instances merely have. Particulars are what they are by participation in Forms; Forms are at once the causes of things' characters, the objects of genuine knowledge, and the standards against which instances fall short. The Republic's divided line arranges reality and cognition in matching tiers: images and the guesswork aimed at them; sensible things and mere belief (pistis); mathematical objects and the hypothesis-driven thinking (dianoia) of the geometers; at the top the Forms, grasped by intellection (noēsis) through dialectic, the whole ordered under the Form of the Good, which does for intelligibility what the sun does for sight. The scheme is the ancestor of every two-worlds metaphysics and every realism about universals — with mathematics fixed, permanently, as the bridge from sense to intellect.

The Parmenides: self-criticism as method

The Parmenides stages the most powerful objections ever raised against the Forms — raised by Plato, against himself, a generation before Aristotle repeated them. The young Socrates presents the theory; the aged Parmenides dismantles it. Which things have Forms — mud and hair too? Does a participant get the whole Form (dividing it from itself) or a part (so a small thing partakes of a fragment of Largeness)? Sharpest is the argument later called the Third Man: if the many large things are large in virtue of one Form, Largeness, and Largeness is itself large — as Plato's talk of Forms as perfect exemplars suggests — then the large things plus Largeness form a new many, requiring a further Form over them, and so on without end. The regress runs on two tacit premises — self-predication (the Form of F is itself F) and non-identity (nothing is F in virtue of itself) — and the modern literature is largely a taxonomy of which to abandon. What matters interpretively is that Plato prints the objections without printed answers, has Parmenides insist that philosophy is impossible without Forms all the same, and prescribes a grueling dialectical training. This is not embarrassment; it is the founding demonstration that a theory is held responsibly only together with the strongest case against it, and the late dialogues' revised machinery is best read as his answer.

Knowledge as recollection

The Meno poses a paradox that still frames the theory of inquiry: you cannot seek what you know (you have it already) nor what you do not know (you would not recognize it if found) — so inquiry is either idle or impossible. Plato's answer is recollection (anamnēsis): learning is the recovery of knowledge the soul possessed before birth. Socrates elicits from an uneducated enslaved boy, by questions alone, the truth that the square on a diagonal doubles the original — drawn out of him, Plato argues, not put in. The Phaedo tightens it: we judge sensible equals deficient against Equality itself — a standard never met in experience — so acquaintance with the Forms must predate experience. Stripped of the reincarnation mythology, this is the first theory of a priori knowledge — some knowledge, paradigmatically mathematical, is native to the mind, triggered rather than taught — and its descendants run through Augustine's illumination, Descartes' and Leibniz's innate ideas, and contemporary rationalism. The paradox survives its Platonic solution as a standing constraint on any account of inquiry: partial knowledge must be possible.

The Republic: soul, city, and the cave

The Republic asks why one should be just when injustice pays, and answers with a psychology and a politics built in parallel. The soul has three parts — reason, which calculates and seeks truth; spirit (thumos), the seat of anger, honor, and shame; and appetite — a division argued from inner conflict (the thirsty man who refuses to drink contains a part that pulls and a part that restrains). Justice in the soul is each part doing its own work under reason's rule, a kind of psychic health; and since a healthy soul is worth having whatever its reputation, justice pays intrinsically — answering Glaucon's challenge, put with the ring of Gyges, that anyone who could sin invisibly would. The city-soul analogy scales the structure up: the just city's three classes — rulers, auxiliaries, producers — each do their own work, and the notorious conclusion is that cities will not flourish until philosophers rule, since only knowers of the Form of the Good know what institutions are for. The cave condenses the whole: prisoners bound before shadows mistake them for reality; release, painful ascent to daylight, and reluctant return to govern those still bound allegorize education, the philosopher's condition, and the cost of enlightenment among the unenlightened. At a stroke the Republic founded moral psychology, ideal political theory, the critique of democracy as rule by appetite, and — in expelling the imitative poets — the philosophy of art as a problem.

Eros and ascent: the Symposium

The Symposium takes a phenomenon the Republic mostly polices — desire — and makes it the engine of philosophy. In Diotima's speech, eros is neither god nor mortal but an intermediate spirit, child of Resource and Poverty: love is precisely the condition of lacking and reaching, and what lovers ultimately want is to "give birth in beauty" — to generate children, deeds, or ideas, and thereby touch immortality. Hence the ladder of ascent: from love of one beautiful body, to bodily beauty as such, to the beauty of souls, of practices and laws, of knowledge, until the lover suddenly beholds Beauty itself — the Form of which every lower beauty was a partial reflection. This is not the mysticism it resembles. The ascent does not renounce the lower rungs but reinterprets them: particular beauties are genuine, if partial, disclosures of the Form, and desire for them is the same desire dialectic completes. And the ladder makes the philosopher's motivation continuous with everyone's: not a specialist taste, but ordinary longing correctly aimed. Alcibiades' drunken irruption — his portrait of the Socrates he loved and could not have — is Plato's dramatized concession of what the theory costs in human particularity.

The late turn: division, and the Sophist on non-being

The late dialogues replace the elenchus with a new instrument: collection and division — gathering a scattered kind into view, then dividing it along its natural joints (the Phaedrus's image) until the target is defined by its place in a network of kinds. The method's masterpiece is the Sophist, which, hunting the sophist as a dealer in falsehoods, collides with Parmenides' interdiction: to speak falsely is to say what is not, and what is not, Parmenides held, cannot be said or thought at all. Plato's Eleatic Stranger commits what he calls parricide against "father Parmenides" by distinguishing senses of "is not": non-being is not the impossible contrary of being but difference — "Theaetetus flies" is false not because it invokes sheer nothing but because flying is different from everything Theaetetus is doing. The solution runs through the "greatest kinds" — being, sameness, difference, motion, rest — which blend with one another in determinate ways: Forms are no longer isolated units for contemplation but nodes in a structure, and dialectic becomes the science of their combinations. This is the first workable account of falsehood and negation, the beginning of the analysis of predication, and the moment Platonism turned from vision to logic.

The Timaeus: a likely story of the cosmos

The Timaeus — for a millennium the only Plato the Latin West could read — offers a cosmology with a built-in disclaimer: since the sensible world is a changing image of an eternal model, an account of it can be at best a "likely story" (eikōs mythos), not fixed knowledge. A divine craftsman, the Demiurge, being good and ungrudging, orders a pre-existing chaos into the best possible world by looking to the eternal Forms as blueprint — creation as rational artistry, not from nothing. Between model and copy Plato inserts a third thing, the receptacle (chōra): the characterless medium in which qualities come to be, ancestor of every later notion of space and matter. The world receives a soul; time enters as "a moving image of eternity," generated with the heavens; and the four elements are reduced to geometry — corpuscles shaped as regular solids, built from elementary triangles, so that physics bottoms out in mathematics. Read literally or — as the early Academy already debated — as timeless dependence dressed in narrative, the Timaeus fixed the agenda for natural theology and design arguments; its conviction that nature is written in mathematics resurfaces, acknowledged, in Kepler and Galileo.

The dialogue form and the Socratic problem

Plato never speaks in the dialogues. Every doctrine above is voiced by a character — usually "Socrates," a real man who wrote nothing and whom Xenophon and Aristophanes drew differently. The Socratic problem — where the historical Socrates ends and Plato begins — is strictly insoluble; the standard hypothesis assigns the early method-without-doctrine to something like the real Socrates, the constructive metaphysics to Plato. The deeper question is what the form means. On the traditional reading, the dialogues are doctrine in costume, and the mouthpiece of the moment speaks for Plato. On anti-dogmatist readings, ancient and modern, the form is the message: philosophy is an activity between interlocutors, arguments are to be re-enacted rather than believed, and the Phaedrus's critique of writing — a text cannot answer questions or choose its readers — explains why Plato refused the treatise. The consequence is permanent: every statement "Plato held that..." is a reconstruction, and the form forces on readers exactly the labor of active judgment his theory of education demands — which may be its point.

Influence and reception

The Academy outlasted him by three centuries and disagreed with him almost immediately: Speusippus abandoned the Forms for numbers; Aristotle made the criticism of participation and the Third Man the launching point of his own metaphysics; and from Arcesilaus onward the Academy read Plato as a skeptic. The dogmatic Plato returned in Middle Platonism (Forms as thoughts in God's mind) and above all in Plotinus, whose Neoplatonism — the One beyond being, Intellect, Soul, the Symposium's ascent systematized into mysticism — was the reigning philosophy of late antiquity and, for centuries, simply what "Platonism" meant. Through Augustine, who found in "the books of the Platonists" everything in Christianity except the Incarnation, Christian Platonism became a load-bearing wall of Western theology. Ficino's Renaissance translations, the Cambridge Platonists, and mathematical platonism's perennial rebirths — Frege and Gödel accepted the label — kept the metaphysics alive. The pushback is equally canonical: Aristotle on Forms; Nietzsche, for whom Christianity was "Platonism for the people" and Plato the inventor of the life-slandering "true world"; Popper, who indicted the Republic as totalitarianism's blueprint — a charge scholarship has qualified without quite dissolving the illiberal core of philosopher-rule. Whitehead's remark, in context, makes the sober point: Plato staked out the problems; the tradition is the working-through.

Reading path

Begin with the Apology, Crito, and Euthyphro — short, dramatic, the best portrait of the Socratic method — then the Meno as the hinge into epistemology; then the Symposium and Phaedo, the middle metaphysics in its two moods, erotic and austere; then the Republic, the center of the corpus, whole. Only after that attempt the hard late work: Theaetetus, Parmenides, Sophist, Timaeus. Cooper's Plato: Complete Works (Hackett) is the standard edition. Secondary: Annas's An Introduction to Plato's Republic; Kraut's Cambridge Companion to Plato; Vlastos's Socrates: Ironist and Moral Philosopher for the Socratic problem.

Assessment

Editorially: Plato's greatness lies less in the Forms than in the fact that the Parmenides and Sophist were written by the man who invented them. The system is the first great instance of the metaphysical impulse; the self-criticism, of philosophy's answering discipline; the dialogue form holds the two in permanent, deliberate tension. Read as doctrine, Plato was refuted first by himself. Read as a demonstration of what it is to hold a theory responsibly — build it, arm its enemies, keep thinking — he is not yet superseded.
