# Reference

Point at a chair and say "that chair," and contact between word and world seems unmysterious. But "Aristotle" reaches a man dead for millennia, and "phlogiston" reaches nothing at all. Reference is the word–world relation itself, and the problem is to say what makes it hold: in virtue of what does a term designate one thing rather than another — or nothing?

Frege's answer interposes a *sense* between word and object: a mode of presentation, grasped by anyone who understands the term, which determines what (if anything) is referred to. The puzzle of informative identities motivates the apparatus, but so do empty names — "Odysseus" has a sense though no bearer, which is why Homer is intelligible — and attitude contexts, where how the object is presented matters. Russell went further: definite descriptions do not refer at all. "The present king of France is bald" asserts that exactly one thing reigns over France and it is bald — false, not nonsense — and ordinary names are disguised descriptions. Between them, Frege and Russell fixed the descriptivist orthodoxy: reference is routed through descriptive information the speaker possesses.

Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* struck this picture three blows. The modal argument: Aristotle might have done none of the things we know him for, yet "Aristotle" would still have named him; the name designates the same man in every counterfactual situation — it is a *rigid designator* — while descriptions shift. The epistemic argument: if "Gödel" abbreviated "the prover of incompleteness," then "Gödel proved incompleteness" would be knowable a priori, and it is not. The semantic argument: if an unknown Schmidt had actually proved the theorem, "Gödel" would still refer to Gödel, not to whoever satisfies the description. The positive picture is causal-historical: an initial baptism, then transmission from speaker to speaker, each intending to use the name as the last did. Reference can succeed in near-total ignorance. Rigidity also yields the necessary a posteriori — Hesperus is necessarily Phosphorus, though astronomy had to discover it — semantics reshaping modal metaphysics. Putnam extended the lesson to kind terms: on Twin Earth, where the watery stuff is XYZ rather than H2O, speakers with our exact psychology refer to a different substance. "Meanings just ain't in the head": the extension of "water" is fixed by the actual stuff's nature and by deference to experts — an externalism that spread into the theory of mind.

The recurring embarrassment for the anti-descriptivist victory is the one Frege and Russell handled best: empty names and negative existentials. "Vulcan does not exist" is true and seems to be about Vulcan; if a name's meaning is its bearer, the sentence should misfire. Causal theorists owe a story — gappy propositions, pretense, even Meinongian nonexistent objects — and none is comfortable. Descriptivism, meanwhile, mounted a counterattack: two-dimensionalism (Chalmers, Jackson) assigns every term a primary intension, tracking how reference is fixed and preserving an a priori, description-like dimension, alongside a rigid secondary intension — conceding Kripke's data while restoring descriptive knowledge to semantic work. Beneath the whole dispute lies the metasemantic wildcard. Putnam's model-theoretic argument suggests that use alone radically underdetermines reference: too many assignments fit our dispositions. Lewis's reply is *reference magnetism* — some candidates are intrinsically more eligible because more natural, so the world's own joints help fix what our words mean. Whether that is insight or desperation is a question reference shares with the metaphysics of properties.
