# Philosophy of Language

A sentence is a burst of noise or a smear of ink, and yet it can be *about* Vienna, *true* of the tides, *understood* by a stranger. Philosophy of language asks how physical marks come to carry meaning at all — and the candidate answers pull in different directions. For the truth-conditional tradition descending from Frege, Tarski, and Davidson, to know what a sentence means is to know what the world would have to be like for it to be true; meaning is a relation between language and reality. For use theorists inspired by the later Wittgenstein, and for inferentialists like Brandom, meaning is instead a role in practice — what a word does, what moves it licenses — and the world-directedness is downstream. Griceans locate meaning in psychology: a speaker means something by an utterance in virtue of intending to produce a belief in an audience through recognition of that very intention. Each program can claim striking successes; none has annexed the whole territory.

The questions sharpen around reference. How does the name "Aristotle" hook onto a man dead for millennia? Frege posited a *sense* — a mode of presentation — mediating between word and object, which elegantly explains why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative when "Hesperus is Hesperus" is not. Russell dissolved names into disguised descriptions. Then Kripke argued that names are rigid designators attached to their bearers by causal-historical chains, not by descriptions a speaker could supply — a result that reshaped metaphysics as much as semantics. Truth generates its own storm: is it a substantive property (correspondence, coherence) or, as deflationists urge, exhausted by the triviality that "snow is white" is true just in case snow is white? And beneath everything lies Wittgenstein's rule-following problem, radicalized by Kripke: what fact about a finite creature determines that by "plus" it means addition rather than some deviant function agreeing with addition on every case so far computed? If no such fact exists, meaning itself threatens to dissolve into communal practice — or into nothing.

The field articulates along a now-standard division of labor. Semantics studies literal, conventional meaning — theories of meaning, reference, truth, and the machinery of context-sensitivity that lets "I am here now" say something different in every mouth. Pragmatics studies what speakers do with sentences: assertion, promising, implicature, the gap between what is said and what is meant. A further cluster handles the hard cases — attitude reports, metaphor, fiction — where the standard machinery creaks. The boundary with logic runs through logical form and formal semantics; with mind, through intentionality and the priority dispute between thought and language; with metaphysics, through truth and modality; with epistemology, through testimony and the a priori.

## Children

- `meaning` — **Theories of Meaning** — What meaning is and what a theory of it must deliver: truth-conditional, use-based, inferentialist, and intention-based programs. *(anchor)*
- `reference` — **Reference** — How words attach to things: sense and reference, descriptivism versus the causal-historical picture, rigid designation, and empty names. *(anchor)*
- `truth` — **Truth** — Whether truth is a substantive property or a deflationary device: correspondence, coherence, pragmatist, and minimalist theories. *(anchor)*
- `pragmatics` — **Pragmatics and Speech Acts** — What speakers do with words: Austin and Searle on speech acts, Grice on implicature, and the semantics–pragmatics boundary. *(standard)*
- `attitudes` — **Propositional Attitude Reports** — The semantics of "believes that" and its kin, where substitution of co-referring terms breaks down. *(standard)*
- `context` — **Context-Sensitivity and Indexicality** — Indexicals, demonstratives, and Kaplan's character–content framework, and how far context-dependence spreads through the language. *(standard)*
- `figurative` — **Figurative Language** — Metaphor and fictional discourse: how language means beyond the literal, and how we talk truly about what does not exist. *(satellite)*
- `rule-following` — **Rule-Following and Normativity** — Wittgenstein's paradox and Kripke's skeptic: what makes meaning determinate, and whether meaning is essentially normative. *(standard)*

## Bridges

- **Logic**: formal semantics is joint property, and truth is homed here while bridging to the paradoxes; Frege founded both fields in a single stroke.
- **Mind**: intentionality, the language of thought, and attitude reports make the border with philosophy of mind the division's busiest; which is prior — thought or language — is itself a live dispute.
- **Metaphysics**: rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori show semantic argument settling modal questions; truth and ontology keep the border open.
- **Figures and traditions**: Frege, Wittgenstein, Russell, Quine, Kripke, and Grice anchor the figures register; ordinary language philosophy and the analytic tradition itself enter through traditions.
