# Context-Sensitivity and Indexicality

The word "I" has one meaning in English, yet no two speakers use it to talk about the same thing. Any account on which meaning determines reference outright is broken by the smallest words in the language. Kaplan's framework, the field's fixed point, splits meaning in two: *character*, a rule carried by the expression type — "I" refers to the speaker, "now" to the time of utterance — and *content*, what a particular use contributes to what is said. Character takes a context and yields a content; content takes a circumstance of evaluation and yields a truth value. The pure indexicals ("I", "now", "here") consume context automatically; demonstratives ("that", "she") need something more — a demonstration, or on later views a directing intention — and what exactly completes them remains contested. The contents delivered are Russellian: "I" contributes the speaker herself, not a mode of presenting her, making indexicals the cleanest exhibit for direct reference.

The two-tier structure generates a distinctive logic. In Kaplan's logic of demonstratives, "I am here now" is valid — true whenever uttered, since a context locates its agent at its place and time — yet what it expresses is contingent: I could have been elsewhere. Validity without necessity yields a cousin of the contingent a priori, secured by semantic machinery rather than by descriptive reference-fixing, and character explains the cognitive difference between "I am in danger" and "Kaplan is in danger" without reinstating Fregean senses. But the machinery strains against ordinary technology. The answering machine that truly says "I am not here now" seems to falsify the validity; postcards and recordings pry apart the time of production and the time your words take effect. Responses — intended contexts, conventions of playback, tokens interpreted at decoding — divide over whether context is a brute setting or something speakers partly make.

How far does context-sensitivity spread? "Every bottle is empty" quantifies over the bottles that matter, not the universe; "tall" needs a comparison class and a threshold; and epistemic contextualists argue that "knows" itself shifts standards — which would let the skeptic be right in the seminar and irrelevant at the airport. Semanticists divide between positing covert variables in logical form (Stanley and Szabó) and letting free pragmatic enrichment do the work; minimalists resist the spread altogether, holding the genuinely context-sensitive expressions to Kaplan's short list. MacFarlane radicalizes the debate: for "tasty", epistemic "might", and perhaps "knows", the context that matters is not where the sentence is *used* but where it is *assessed* — the same assertion true as I make it, false as you evaluate it. Its strength is the retraction data contextualism fumbles: we take back past claims rather than saying "I spoke truly then." Finally, Kaplan banished *monsters* — operators that shift character or context, as attitude verbs shift circumstance — declaring that English has none and could have none. Amharic, where the "I" of a speech report can pick out the reported speaker, and shifty indexicals elsewhere suggest the ban is a parochial fact about English, if it is a fact at all; what monsters would do to the framework is an open wound.
