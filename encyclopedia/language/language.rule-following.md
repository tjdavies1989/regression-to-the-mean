# Rule-Following and Normativity

A pupil has continued the series "add 2" correctly up to 1000, then writes 1004, 1008. Told he has gone wrong, he replies that he is doing exactly what he was told — this *is* how he understood the rule. Everything in his training is consistent with his deviant reading. Wittgenstein's investigation of such cases (*Philosophical Investigations* 138–242) issues in a paradox stated at PI 201: no course of action can be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule. If accord requires an interpretation, another interpretation can always be interposed; interpretations "hang in the air along with what they interpret." Yet if nothing determines what counts as following the rule, the distinction between correct and incorrect application — the distinction in which meaning seems to consist — collapses.

Kripke's reading radicalized the problem into a scepticism about meaning itself. His sceptic grants total access to my past behavior and mental history and asks: what fact about me constitutes my having meant *addition* by "plus," rather than *quaddition* — a function agreeing with addition on every sum I have actually computed and diverging beyond? Past use is finite and fits both hypotheses. Introspectible states fail: no image or feeling carries instructions for infinitely many new cases. Dispositions fail twice over: they are finite where the function is infinite, and they are descriptive where meaning is normative — my disposition fixes what I *would* say, but meaning addition fixes what I *ought* to say, and I may be disposed to err. Kripke concludes there is no such fact, and offers a sceptical solution: attributions of meaning are not descriptions of facts but moves licensed by assertability conditions grounded in communal agreement. I count as meaning addition insofar as my responses agree with my community's; a speaker considered in isolation cannot be said to follow a rule at all — which is Kripke's route into the private language argument (PI 243 ff.): without outward criteria there is no difference between following a rule and merely believing one is.

The responses divide. Sophisticated dispositionalists idealize — my meaning is what I would say under optimal conditions — but specifying "optimal" without invoking the very content at issue has proven stubbornly hard, and the normativity objection stands or falls with a separate dispute: Boghossian and others have argued that meaning is essentially normative, so that no descriptive fact could constitute it, while Hattiangadi replies that the relevant "oughts" are merely hypothetical — you ought to answer "125" only if you aim to speak truly — leaving naturalistic accounts unthreatened. McDowell's straight solution is quietist: the paradox arises only under the assumption that grasping a rule must be an interpretation; Wittgenstein's own view is that there is "a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation" — mastery of a practice, exhibited in obeying and going against — so the sceptical problem is dissolved, not answered. Its strength is fidelity to Wittgenstein's text; its cost is that many find dissolution indistinguishable from refusal. The problem's reach explains its standing: any theory of meaning, and any naturalized theory of mental content, must locate the fact the sceptic says cannot be found.
