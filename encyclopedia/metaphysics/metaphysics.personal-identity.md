# Personal Identity

You expect to wake up tomorrow. Not merely that someone will wake in your bed with your memories — that *you* will. Prudence, promises, and punishment all presuppose it: the person jailed for last year's crime is supposed to be the very person who did the deed. The problem of personal identity asks what makes this so. What relation between a person at one time and a person at another makes them one person rather than two? And — the question the field eventually turned on itself — is that even the right thing to ask?

Locke gave the problem its modern form by cutting identity loose from substance: a person extends exactly as far as consciousness extends, so the prince's memories in the cobbler's body carry the prince. Butler's objection stands at the entrance: memory presupposes identity and so cannot constitute it — I can genuinely remember only what *I* did. Reid added that memory is not transitive where identity must be: the old general remembers the young officer, the officer remembered the boy, the general has lost the boy entirely. The modern psychological-continuity view is Locke repaired against both: quasi-memory (memory-like states caused in the right way, with no identity built in) answers Butler, and overlapping chains of connectedness — memories, intentions, character — answer Reid.

Parfit pushed the repaired view to its crisis. Split a brain and house each hemisphere in a new body: both resulting people are psychologically continuous with you, and identity, being one–one, cannot hold of both. Yet fission seems about as good as ordinary survival — hardly death twice over. Parfit's conclusion: identity is not what matters. What matters is Relation R, psychological connectedness and continuity by any cause, which can branch and hold by degrees though identity can do neither. Persons are not further facts over and above brains, bodies, and interrelated experiences; some questions of identity are empty, and rational concern should follow R, not identity.

Animalism replies that the whole tradition has changed the subject. You are a human organism, and organisms persist by biological continuity: you were once a fetus with no psychology at all, and you could outlast your psychology in a vegetative state. Olson's thinking-animal argument gives the view its edge — the animal sitting in your chair is thinking your thoughts; if you are not that animal, there are two thinkers in your chair, with no way to say which is you. The transplant intuition (that you would go where your cerebrum goes) is the price animalists knowingly pay.

Two further options reject the terms. Narrative views (MacIntyre, Schechtman) distinguish reidentification from *characterization*: the identity that matters in life is constituted by the self-story that makes actions and commitments intelligible as one life — which is why "identity crisis" names something real that no continuity criterion touches. And the no-self option denies the persisting subject outright. Hume looked within and found only a bundle of perceptions, never the perceiver. The Buddhist analysis is the worked-out rival system, not a footnote: the person as a conventional designation upon causally connected aggregates — Milinda's chariot — refined over two millennia with its own accounts of memory and responsibility. Parfit himself noted the convergence.

## Bridges

- To `metaphysics.identity`: persistence theory sets the menu here — fission is tractable for perdurantists and stage theorists in ways it is not for endurantists.
- To `mind.self`: the metaphysics of persons meets the first-person perspective — what the subject of experience is, and whether introspection could even find a self.
- To `ethics`: if Parfit is right that identity is not what matters, prudence loses its special authority, and the boundaries between egoism, ethics, and concern for future people are redrawn.
- To `traditions.buddhist-philosophy`: no-self is a complete rival tradition, with its own debates over karma and responsibility without a persisting subject.
