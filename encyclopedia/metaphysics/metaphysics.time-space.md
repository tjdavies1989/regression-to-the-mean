# Time and Space

Nothing is more familiar than that time passes: the future bears down, becomes present for an instant, and hardens into the past. Yet physics describes a four-dimensional geometry in which nothing flows, and the modern field begins with an argument that the flow is contradictory in itself. McTaggart (1908) distinguished two orderings of events: the A-series, which sorts them as past, present, or future, and the B-series, which merely relates them as earlier and later. He argued that genuine change requires the A-series, and that the A-series is contradictory: every event must be past, present, and future, and each attempt to relieve the incompatibility ("it *was* future, *is* present") re-invokes the series being explained. Conclusion: time is unreal. Few accept the conclusion; the field's map is the argument's wreckage. A-theorists keep passage and deny the contradiction; B-theorists keep time and deny passage, calling the feel of flow a projection of how temporal creatures experience a static manifold — against which stands Prior's "Thank goodness that's over," thanks for an A-fact no B-fact can supply.

The ontological question cross-cuts the argument. Presentism says only the present exists — the view closest to common sense, strained by past truths that seem to need past truthmakers, and by relativity. Eternalism says past, present, and future events are equally real: the block universe. The growing block (Broad) splits the difference — past and present real, future not. Here the empirical pressure is sharpest: special relativity makes simultaneity frame-relative, and Putnam argued that since what is "present," and so real, differs between observers in relative motion, any metaphysics requiring a privileged world-wide now contradicts our best physics. Presentists answer by relativizing existence or positing an undetectable preferred frame. The block, meanwhile, has its own embarrassment: it is time-symmetric, and our world manifestly is not — entropy increases, memory reaches only backward, causes precede effects. The dominant program grounds time's arrow in thermodynamics: entropy increase plus a low-entropy past boundary condition, details owned by philosophy of physics.

Space raises the mirror-image dispute. Substantivalists, following Newton, hold that space (now spacetime) is an entity in its own right; relationists, following Leibniz, that it is nothing over and above relations among bodies. Newton's rotating bucket — the water's concave surface betrays rotation relative to *something* beyond the bucket — was the substantivalist's exhibit for two centuries, until general relativity complicated the ledger: the hole argument (Earman and Norton) contends that treating spacetime points as genuine individuals forces an odd indeterminism and pushes many toward "sophisticated" substantivalism or structuralist middle ways. Time travel: Lewis argued that the grandfather paradox shows only that a time traveler *will not*, not that she *cannot*, kill her grandfather — an ability claim true relative to one set of facts, false relative to another — so backward time travel is coherent though constrained. That the question is not idle fiction is Gödel's doing: he found solutions of Einstein's field equations containing closed timelike curves, and inferred that such a universe contains no objective lapse of time at all.
