# Grounding and Fundamentality

Socrates exists if and only if his singleton set {Socrates} does; each necessitates the other; they inhabit exactly the same possible worlds. Yet the set exists *because* the man does, and not the other way around. Whatever draws that asymmetry, modal notions provably cannot: necessitation, supervenience, and entailment are all symmetric across the case. Kit Fine's argument to this effect, together with programmatic statements by Jonathan Schaffer and Gideon Rosen, launched the past two decades' most consequential addition to metaphysical ideology — **grounding**, the relation of holding *in virtue of*. The mental is grounded in the physical; a law's validity in facts about legislatures; a disjunction in its true disjunct; the whole (or the parts — see below) in the other. Grounding is explanatory but not causal, tighter than supervenience, and, its friends argue, primitive: the notion metaphysicians were groping for all along when they spoke of dependence, priority, and reduction.

Take grounding seriously and metaphysics acquires a shape: a partial ordering of reality into the more and less fundamental, terminating (if it terminates) in the ungrounded. Its formal behavior — standardly irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, well-founded, each axiom now under attack from purported counterexamples and from infinitist and coherentist rivals — is a research program in its own right. So is fundamentality itself. Schaffer's **priority monism** holds that the one whole, the cosmos, is fundamental and its parts derivative, arguing from the possibility of gunk and from quantum entanglement; priority pluralism runs the ordering the other way, from smallest to largest. Both agree on what exists and disagree only about what grounds what — which, Schaffer urges, was the interesting question all along.

The skeptics deny that the new primitive earns its keep. Jessica Wilson argues that the real work is done by the specific "small-g" relations — set formation, realization, composition, determination — and that big-G Grounding, stripped of them, is too coarse even to fix a direction of priority. Thomas Hofweber's charge is harsher: "esoteric metaphysics," a discipline built on notions intelligible only to those already inside it. Others press the unity question — why think these heterogeneous dependencies are one relation at all?

The node also houses **metametaphysics**, because the deepest doubt about ontology is a doubt about grounding-style discourse generally. The Carnap revival re-arms the internal/external distinction against Quine's collapse of it. Eli Hirsch's quantifier variance holds that "exists" has many candidate meanings of equal metaphysical merit, so that debates like composition are *verbal disputes*, settled by charity to ordinary English. Amie Thomasson's easy ontology derives entities from trivial inferences. Against all of this stands Theodore Sider's *Writing the Book of the World*: reality has objective structure, some vocabulary carves at the joints, and a dispute is substantive when conducted in joint-carving terms — the quantifier included. Truthmaking, finally, is grounding's ancestor: Armstrong's demand that every truth be made true by something was already an in-virtue-of claim, and its classic puzzles (negative existentials, maximalism) now read as chapters of the grounding literature.

## Bridges

- To `meta`: whether ontological disputes are settleable, and by what method, is the metaphilosophical question of philosophical progress in local costume; metametaphysics is its best-developed case study.
- To `logic.mathematics.ontology`: numbers are metaontology's favorite test case — neo-Fregean abstraction and easy ontology promise them cheaply; joint-carving realism asks what the discount costs.
- To `language`: quantifier variance and verbal disputes turn ontology into semantics; truthmaking patrols the border between truth's theory and its grounds.
- Within the division: the Carnap–Quine quarrel is homed at `metaphysics.ontology`; this node owns its modern rerun.
