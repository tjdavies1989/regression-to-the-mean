# Causation

Strike a match and it lights. Nothing seems better known than that the striking *made* the lighting happen — causation is how the world hangs together, the difference between one thing following another and one thing happening *because* of another. Yet look as closely as you like at the striking and you will see the match move, the flame appear, and never the making itself. That is Hume's challenge, and the field's founding text contains the field's founding equivocation: Hume offers two "definitions" of cause as if they were one. A cause is an object followed by another, "where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second" — regularity; "or in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed" — counterfactual dependence. These are not the same relation, and the subsequent literature is in large part the working-out of each disjunct.

The regularity program refined Hume's first definition — most carefully in Mackie's INUS account, on which a cause is an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition — and fell to familiar counterexamples: regularities without causation (day follows night), and effects of a common cause that co-vary lawfully without causing one another. Lewis took up the second definition: an event causes another when, had it not occurred, the other would not have — dependence traded through the machinery of possible worlds. Its strength is that it locates causation in the particular case rather than the general run. Its nemesis is preemption: a backup cause waits in the wings, so the effect does not counterfactually depend on the actual cause, which caused it all the same. Early, late, and trumping preemption consumed the program in epicycles, driving Lewis to the baroque "influence" account. Probabilistic theories — causes raise the probability of their effects — inherit parallel troubles, since a genuine cause can lower its effect's chance and yet bring it about.

Two responses now dominate. Process theories (Salmon, Dowe) say causation is a physical connection: the transmission of a mark, or of a conserved quantity such as energy or momentum. This handles preemption elegantly — the actual cause is the one connected to the effect — but strains on absences: the gardener's failure to water kills the plant with no quantity transmitted, and if omissions can cause, causation cannot simply be a physical thread. Absences also sharpen the problem of causal selection: the striking, the oxygen, and the dryness were all needed for the fire, and what promotes one to "the cause" and demotes the rest to background conditions may be interest-relative rather than metaphysical. Interventionism (Woodward) has become the lingua franca of philosophy of science and statistics alike: X causes Y when suitably surgical interventions on X would change Y. Whether this is an analysis of causation or an admirably precise methodology resting on causal notions it cannot discharge is the open question. Against the whole Humean inheritance stands the powers revival: dispositional essentialists (Harré and Madden, Ellis, Mumford, Bird) hold that properties are essentially powers, and that the match was disposed to light all along — necessity in nature, not projected onto it.

- `absences` — **Absences and Omissions** — whether what does not happen can cause, and causation by prevention. *(satellite)*
- `selection` — **Causal Selection** — what distinguishes the cause from mere background conditions, and whether the distinction is principled. *(satellite)*
