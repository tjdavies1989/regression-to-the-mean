# Modality

Some truths could have been otherwise; others could not. There are eight planets, but there might have been nine; two and two make four, and nothing could have made them five. The distinction is as ordinary as regret and as entrenched as science, whose laws are supposed to support counterfactuals that mere accidents do not. Yet everything anyone has ever observed is actual. What, then, is the difference between what is and what must be, and where in the world does it lie? That question is the metaphysics of modality. Its formal grammar — boxes and diamonds, accessibility relations, the systems T, S4, and S5 — is homed at `logic.modal`; the semantics runs identically on every answer to the ontological question, which is why that question falls here, and why the bill for the machinery is paid on this side of the border. The entry proceeds from the shape of the problem through its history to the source of necessity, and then to the four regions into which the contemporary field articulates: what possible worlds are (`worlds`), what essence is and whether necessity flows from it (`essence`), how modal truth could be known (`modal-epistemology`), and how one thing shows up in many possibilities (`counterparts`). It closes at the hyperintensional frontier where all four now meet.

## The shape of the problem

Three distinctions organize everything that follows. The first is between kinds of modality. "Must" and "can" are promiscuous: a bridge must bear its load (physical necessity), a promise must be kept (deontic), the butler must be the murderer (epistemic), a bachelor must be unmarried (conceptual), and two and two must make four (logical or mathematical). The metaphysician's target is the widest of the objective modalities — what could have been the case, full stop, given only what things are — which Kripke called metaphysical necessity and distinguished sharply from what can be known a priori. The dominant formal picture, owed to Lewis and to Kratzer's semantics for natural-language modals, treats every "must" as a universal quantifier over a domain of possibilities restricted by context: physical necessity ranges over worlds that share our laws, deontic necessity over worlds where obligations are met, metaphysical necessity over all worlds whatever. Whether metaphysical necessity really is the outermost ring, and whether the rings are ranked at all, is contested below.

The second distinction is between modality de dicto and modality de re — between the necessity of a proposition and the necessary possession of a property by a thing. That nine is necessarily greater than seven is unproblematic de dicto. That the number of the planets is necessarily greater than seven is false de dicto and, on the de re reading, asks whether that very number has the property essentially however it is picked out. Quine's mid-century indictment of quantified modal logic was that the de re reading commits one to an Aristotelian essentialism about objects simpliciter; Kripke's rehabilitation was the reply that the commitment is true. The essentialism debate and the transworld-identity debate both turn on this hinge.

The third distinction is between the semantics of modal discourse and its metaphysics. Possible-worlds semantics assigns truth conditions: necessarily p just in case p holds at every accessible world. That such a semantics is adequate tells one nothing about what worlds are, whether "world" is a modality-free notion, or whether the semantics reduces modality or merely models it. The recurring temptation, warned against from Quine's criterion of commitment to Divers's agnosticism, is to read an ontology off a model; the standing joke — almost nobody believes in Lewis's worlds and almost everybody quantifies over them — is the practice that warning targets. Keeping semantics and metaphysics apart is the entry's recurring task.

## History

**Antiquity and the Middle Ages.** Aristotle bequeathed the vocabulary and the first systematic puzzles: a modal syllogistic; the contrast between what belongs to a thing per se and per accidens; the essence as "what it was to be" a thing, captured in real definition; and, in *De Interpretatione* 9, the sea-battle argument that future contingents threaten either bivalence or openness. On the reading Hintikka made standard, Aristotle and much of antiquity operated with a temporal or "statistical" conception of modality: the necessary is what always is, the possible what is at some time, and no genuine possibility remains forever unrealized — the principle of plenitude Lovejoy traced from Plato through the Neoplatonists. Diodorus Cronus made the reduction explicit, defining the possible as what is or will be true; the Hellenistic quarrel over his Master Argument is the first recorded dispute about whether modality is anything over and above the course of history. The medievals loosened the tie between modality and time. Avicenna's distinction between what is necessary in itself and what is possible in itself but necessary through another, together with his separation of essence from existence, gave the tradition its first developed ontology of contingency (see `figures/ibn-sina`). Duns Scotus, on Knuuttila's influential reconstruction, introduced synchronic contingency: at the very instant a thing is thus, it could have been otherwise, so that possibility is a domain of alternatives simultaneous with actuality rather than a schedule of what will eventually occur. That domain — logical possibility as a space of compossible alternatives, prior even to the divine will — is the ancestor of the possible world (see `traditions/medieval-latin`).

**Leibniz.** The possible-worlds idiom is Leibnizian. God contemplates infinitely many complete worlds, maximal collections of compossible individuals, and actualizes the best; necessary truths are those whose denial yields a contradiction in finitely many steps, contingent truths those whose analysis would run to infinity. Every individual has a complete concept containing everything it will ever do, which is why Arnauld objected that Leibniz had made everything necessary. Leibniz's reply — that the concept is contingent because its instantiation is, and that a differently acting Adam would not be this Adam but a similar possible one — has been read by some commentators as an anticipation of counterpart theory, and the tension between complete concepts and de re contingency is the seed of the haecceitism debate treated at `counterparts`.

**Hume, Kant, Mill.** Hume set the empiricist terms: relations of ideas are necessary and knowable by thought alone, matters of fact contingent and knowable only by experience; whatever the mind clearly conceives is possible; and no necessary connection is to be found among distinct existences. The conceivability maxim is the founding principle of modal epistemology, and the dictum against necessary connections is the founding scruple of the program Lewis would later name Humean supervenience (see `figures/hume`). Kant made necessity and strict universality the marks of the a priori, so that the necessary, the a priori, and — for the analytic — the conceptual were aligned; the alignment held for a century and a half, and breaking it was Kripke's signal achievement (see `figures/kant`). Mill denied that inconceivability of the contrary proves anything but the strength of an association, and treated essences as belonging to names rather than to things — the deflationary line that runs unbroken to modern conventionalism.

**The twentieth century.** Two revolutions, one formal and one philosophical, produced the contemporary field. The formal revolution began when C. I. Lewis built the systems of strict implication later labeled S1 through S5 (1918; with Langford, 1932). Ruth Barcan Marcus added quantifiers (1946), stated the formula that bears her name, and proved the necessity of identity (1947). Carnap's *Meaning and Necessity* (1947) interpreted necessity as truth in all state descriptions, a modal logic fit for the positivist conviction that necessity was linguistic. What made the axiom systems intelligible rather than merely competing was the relational semantics developed in the late 1950s by Kanger, Hintikka, Prior, and above all Kripke (1959, 1963): necessity as truth at all accessible worlds, with each system answering to a condition on accessibility. The philosophical revolution ran in the opposite direction. Quine's papers of the 1940s and 1950s argued that quantifying into modal contexts was either unintelligible or committed to essentialism; the positivist consensus he inherited held that necessity was analyticity and analyticity a matter of convention, until "Two Dogmas" removed the convention and left necessity with no home at all. The reversal came in Kripke's Princeton lectures of 1970, published as *Naming and Necessity*: names are rigid designators; identity statements between them are necessary if true; some necessities are known only a posteriori and some contingencies a priori; and objects have essential properties of origin and kind. Almost simultaneously Lewis, in *Counterfactuals* (1973) and definitively in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), made the semantics into an ontology; Plantinga (1974), Adams (1974), and Stalnaker (1976) supplied the actualist alternatives; and by the mid-1980s the metaphysics of modality was the flagship of a revived discipline (see `figures/lewis`). The two subsequent turning points are Fine's "Essence and Modality" (1994), which reversed the order of explanation between essence and necessity, and Williamson's work from *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007) to *Modal Logic as Metaphysics* (2013), which relocated modal epistemology to counterfactual competence and reopened, under the name necessitism, the question whether everything that exists exists necessarily.

## The source of necessity

Behind the regional debates stands one question: what, if anything, makes necessary truths necessary? Blackburn's dilemma (1986) is the cleanest statement of why every answer is under strain. Suppose one explains why p is necessary by citing some fact F. Either F is itself necessary, in which case the necessity has been relocated rather than explained and a regress threatens; or F is contingent, in which case p's necessity rests on something that might have failed, and it becomes unclear in what sense p could not have been otherwise. Blackburn's own conclusion was projectivist: necessity is our refusal to countenance alternatives, expressed in the material mode. Realists reply variously — Hale (2002) that the first horn is harmless when the explaining fact is a nature rather than one more necessity awaiting explanation, Cameron (2010) that the second horn confuses grounding with entailment — but the dilemma organizes the field, because the live positions are best understood as choices about where explanation may stop.

Five stopping points are on the market. **Reductive realism** (Lewis) stops nowhere modal: necessity is quantification over concrete worlds, and the worlds are simply there, as sets are; the price is the ontology treated at `worlds`. **Primitivism**, or modalism — Prior's, Plantinga's, Forbes's, and in a different key Stalnaker's — takes modality as a basic ideological commitment, on the ground that every attempted reduction either fails or reintroduces the notion it analyzes; its cost is silence at exactly the point where explanation is wanted, and Peacocke's principle-based conception is its most developed attempt to say something nonetheless. **Essentialism** (Fine, Lowe, Hale) stops at natures: necessary truths hold in virtue of the essences of the things they concern, and essence is prior to and finer than modality; the bill is presented at `essence` and paid, if at all, at `modal-epistemology`. **Deflationism** stops at us — at rules of individuation (Sidelle), at semantic norms (Thomasson), or, in Sider's Humean reductionism, at a list: the necessary truths are those falling under listed categories (logical, mathematical, analytic, and so on), with no modal fact underlying the list. Its difficulty is the contingency objection, that conventions and lists are themselves contingent and cannot be what makes anything necessary. **Dispositionalism**, or the powers theory (Borghini and Williams, Jacobs, Vetter), stops at actual things' potentialities: something is possible just in case some actual object has, or had, a power whose manifestation would make it so. Its difficulty is reach — global possibilities, possibilities involving alien kinds, and the necessities of mathematics are hard to trace to anyone's powers. Vetter's *Potentiality* (2015) is the developed statement, addressing reach by iterating potentiality; its critics ask whether the iteration is any less promissory than the ersatzer's appeal to consistency.

A second question about the source is whether there is one. Fine's "The Varieties of Necessity" (2002) argues that metaphysical, natural, and normative necessity are three irreducible kinds: neither natural nor normative necessity can be defined as metaphysical necessity relative to some body of truths, since the relativized notion returns the wrong verdict on the modal status of those very truths, and neither is a species of the metaphysical. The opposite tendency is dispositional essentialism (Shoemaker, Ellis, Bird): properties have their causal profiles essentially, so the laws are metaphysically necessary and natural necessity collapses upward into the metaphysical — a dispute this entry shares with `science.laws`. Whether metaphysical modality is genuinely absolute (Hale, Williamson) or one restriction among others is the corresponding question at the outer edge.

A third question has become the busiest of the past decade: whether everything is necessarily something. Necessitism, defended by Williamson (2013) following Linsky and Zalta, holds that ontology is constant across worlds — what is contingent is only whether a thing is concrete, never whether it is. The argument is that the simplest and strongest higher-order modal logic, validating the Barcan formulas and their converses, is the one necessitism vindicates, and that the contingentist's alternatives are formally maimed. Contingentism (Stalnaker 2012; Fritz and Goodman on counting incompossibles) replies that a logic is not entitled to legislate ontology, and that the necessitist's merely possible objects are actualism's old possibilia under a new description. The formal core of this dispute belongs at `logic.modal`; the metaphysical stakes — whether what exists varies across possibilities — belong here and at `metaphysics.ontology`.

## What worlds are

The realist proposal is the field's centerpiece and its scandal, and `worlds` gives its full accounting. Lewis's plurality is exactly what the semantics appears to quantify over: infinitely many concrete universes, each a maximal sum of spatiotemporally related things, none in any spatiotemporal or causal relation to another, none privileged; "actual" is indexical, true at each world of itself alone. Plenitude comes from recombination — duplicates of any parts of any worlds can be patched together, size and shape permitting — and what the ontology buys is the only fully reductive analysis on offer: counterfactuals by comparative similarity among concrete worlds, properties as sets of possibilia, propositions as sets of worlds, and modality itself as quantification with no modal remainder. Lewis's argument is avowedly Quinean and cost-benefit: the hypothesis is priced like set theory, an ontology repaid in explanatory power across the whole system, and the incredulous stare is entered honestly as a cost rather than a refutation. The child records the structural objections: the isolation criterion that rules island universes impossible by fiat; the Forrest–Armstrong cardinality argument that unrestricted recombination generates more worlds than any set can hold, forcing restrictions that Nolan (1996) argued are either arbitrary or avoidable at the price of proper-class plenitude; Adams's ethical objection that preventing an evil here merely leaves it actual elsewhere. Two further objections belong to the history. The epistemological objection, pressed from the 1970s, is Benacerraf's problem transposed: worlds causally isolated from us cannot be known by any causal route. Lewis's reply, that modal knowledge is like mathematical knowledge and needs no causal contact, stands or falls with the epistemology at `modal-epistemology`. Lycan's (1979) and Shalkowski's (1994) objection is the one Lewis never squarely answered: a plurality of concrete universes can ground modality only if the plurality comprises all the possibilities and only the possibilities, and nothing in a mere totality of existing things says that it does — so either the reduction imports primitive modality at the boundary, or "possible" has been replaced by "exists somewhere in a totality whose extent is an accident."

The actualist alternatives are still organized by Lewis's taxonomy of ersatzism, and their finer treatment is promised at `worlds.ersatzism`. Linguistic ersatzism — Carnap's state descriptions, Adams's world-stories — builds worlds as maximal consistent sets of sentences or propositions; its costs are the primitive modality in "consistent" and its blindness to alien possibilities, since a worldmaking language drawn from actuality cannot distinguish possibilities involving properties never instantiated here. Sider's "The Ersatz Pluriverse" (2002) and Melia's defenses answer the second charge by quantifying over properties rather than naming them, and treat the first as a price every actualist pays knowingly. Combinatorialism (Armstrong 1989), on which possibilities are recombinations of actual simples and universals, is a distinctive ersatzism with the same alien-universals difficulty and a principled refusal to pay it. Pictorial ersatzism inherits the realist's costs in proportion to its fidelity. Magical ersatzism — Plantinga's maximal possible states of affairs, Stalnaker's ways things might have been construed as properties of the totality — takes worlds as simple abstracta that represent without structure; Lewis's charge that the representing relation is then a primitive about which nothing can be said is met by Stalnaker's calm concession that the reduction of modality was never a reasonable demand, and by van Inwagen's observation that every theory has primitives and the only question is which. Rosen's modal fictionalism (1990) declines to believe any of it: worlds-talk is true under the prefix "according to the fiction of the plurality of worlds." The Brock–Rosen objection (1993) notes that since the fiction says there are many worlds at every world, "necessarily there are many worlds" comes out true without the prefix; Hale's dilemma (1995) asks whether the fiction is necessarily or contingently false, and finds that the first horn trivializes the prefix while the second lets the fiction contradict itself; the timid and strong variants and their replies are promised at `worlds.fictionalism`. Divers's agnosticism (2004) — use the semantics, suspend judgment on the ontology — describes what much of the field in fact does.

The current state of play has shifted the ground under all of these. First, the worlds-free theories of the source of necessity — modalism, essentialism, and the powers theory — deny that a metaphysics of modality must be a metaphysics of worlds at all, and treat worlds as a representational convenience earned, if at all, by whatever primitive one has chosen. Second, Williamson's necessitism reconceives worlds themselves as higher-order entities, and relocates the ontological dispute from the concreteness of worlds to the constancy of the domain. Third, the hyperintensional frontier treated below suggests that even a well-founded ontology of worlds would be too coarse for the distinctions metaphysics now draws. What has not shifted is the fact the child records: worlds-semantics is the lingua franca of metaphysics, semantics, and decision theory, and its users, asked what worlds are, gesture toward ersatzism and change the subject. Whether that is theft over honest toil or the correct attitude toward a model is a question about method as much as about modality, and it is pursued at `figures/lewis` and `meta.methods`.

## Essence

The essence debate has a longer history than the worlds debate and has undergone a more complete reversal within living memory; `essence` sets it out. Aristotle's real definitions and the Porphyrian tree of genus and differentia organized the metaphysics of kinds for two millennia. Locke split real essence, the unknown constitution on which a thing's discoverable qualities depend, from nominal essence, the abstract idea a word marks, and doubted that the former could be known; Mill denied that anything but names had essences. The twentieth-century nadir was Quine's. His mathematical cyclist — necessarily rational as a mathematician, necessarily two-legged as a cyclist, and neither necessarily anything as the man he is — was meant to show that de re necessity makes sense only relative to a description, so that quantified modal logic, which asks what an object is necessarily however described, presupposes an essentialism no empiricist could accept. The formal replies came first: Marcus (1961) argued that names are tags rather than descriptions, so that identity between them is necessary and the puzzle cases dissolve; Parsons (1969) showed that quantified modal logic entails no nontrivial essentialist claim; Plantinga (1969, 1974) showed that de re modality can be defined from de dicto modality plus identity. The philosophical reply was Kripke's. If "Socrates" is a rigid designator, tracking one man at every world where he exists, then asking what he could have been requires no privileged description. And with de re modality legitimate, Kripke argued for substantive essences: origin (this table could not have been made from an entirely different block of wood; a person could not have come from different gametes), kind (Socrates could not have been a non-human), and composition — each necessary, each known only a posteriori. The child traces the argument literature: Salmon's finding that the origin argument needs a sufficiency premise Kripke's text lacks, Forbes's defense by way of the branching conception of possibility, Robertson's tolerance and recycling puzzles on which modest premises yield too little necessity or too much, and Mackie's minimalist essentialism (2006), which concludes that objects have only trivial essences and that the origin intuitions are artifacts of how cases are described. The finer accounting is promised at `essence.origin`.

The reversal is Fine's. Kripke's rehabilitation still defined essential properties modally, as those a thing has necessarily if it exists. Fine's counterexamples (1994) show the modal definition overgenerating: Socrates necessarily belongs to his singleton, is necessarily distinct from the Eiffel Tower, and necessarily exists only where two and two make four — yet none of this is part of what Socrates is. Essence, on the definitional account, is real definition: the constitutive core of what it is to be a thing, with a consequential penumbra of what follows from that core; metaphysical necessity is then truth in virtue of the natures of all things, and the order of explanation runs from essence to modality rather than the reverse. Ontological dependence follows the same pattern — x depends on y when y figures in x's definition — cutting finer than the modal-existential account on which Socrates and his singleton depend on each other symmetrically; the details are promised at `essence.dependence`, and the same cut helped ignite the grounding literature at `metaphysics.grounding`. The essence-first program that followed — Lowe's serious essentialism, on which essences are not further entities but the very identities of things; Hale's essentialist grounding of necessary beings; Correia's logics and varieties of essence; Koslicki's neo-Aristotelian hylomorphism — is now the dominant realist option, and it has drawn a counter-literature of three kinds. Modalists who resist the inversion (Wildman, Cowling) argue that a modal account restricted to sparse or natural properties survives Fine's cases, since set membership and distinctness from towers are not sparse; Brogaard and Salerno recover essence from counterpossible conditionals. Leech asks whether essence explains necessity or merely relabels it. And Teitel (2019) argues that Fine's reduction, combined with contingent existence, returns the wrong necessities unless the natures themselves are necessary beings — an internal tension the program has yet to close.

Against realism of both Kripkean and Finean kinds stand the deflations, promised in detail at `essence.deflationism`. Sidelle's conventionalism (1989) keeps Kripke's data — water is necessarily H₂O — while factoring each necessary a posteriori truth into an analytic frame ("whatever water's actual deep structure is, it has it necessarily"), which is a matter of our conventions of individuation, and an empirical filling, which is not; essences are shadows cast by how we individuate, and Kripke's arguments establish nothing about mind-independent natures. Thomasson's modal normativism (2020) goes further: metaphysical modal claims are not descriptions of modal facts at all but object-language expressions of semantic rules — permissions and requirements on the use of terms, spoken in the material mode — so that modal knowledge is cheap (conceptual mastery plus empirical fact) and much of modal metaphysics is a dispute about rules mistaken for a dispute about the world. The best argument for deflationism is epistemological: it alone explains how creatures whose faculties are tuned to actuality could be so confident about necessity. The best argument against it is the contingency objection, that conventions are contingent and could not make it necessary that water is H₂O; the reply — that the deflationist explains our necessity-talk, not a necessity-fact, and so owes nothing at the level of facts — is where the debate now stands. Blackburn's projectivism and Brandom's modal expressivism, on which modal vocabulary makes explicit the inferential commitments implicit in ordinary description, are the same instinct at a greater distance from the Kripkean data. The question that survives is the child's: whether essence is discovered or legislated. The deflationist owes an account of why definitions so often look like findings; the essentialist owes an account of how they are found.

## Modal knowledge

Whatever the ontology, only one world is ever observed, so every modal belief that outruns actuality needs a source; `modal-epistemology` inventories the candidates. The history of the problem is the history of the conceivability principle and its discontents. Descartes's real-distinction argument moved from the clear and distinct conception of mind without body to their possible separation (see `figures/descartes`); Arnauld's objection — that one can clearly conceive a right triangle without conceiving what follows from its being one — is the first statement that conceivability tracks possibility only when the conception is complete. Hume made conceivability the criterion of possibility; Mill replied that inconceivability measures habit. Kant's alignment of the necessary with the a priori made modal knowledge a special case of a priori knowledge and so, for the positivists, of linguistic knowledge — a solution that lasted exactly as long as the alignment. Kripke's necessary a posteriori broke it and created the modern problem: if water is necessarily H₂O and we can apparently conceive otherwise, conceivability is either unreliable or in need of disambiguation. Kripke's own diagnosis — that what we conceive is an epistemic counterpart, a situation qualitatively like ours in which the watery stuff is something else — is the seed of every subsequent refinement.

The refinements are Yablo's and Chalmers's, promised at `modal-epistemology.conceivability`. Yablo (1993) distinguished conceiving that p from merely finding no contradiction in p: to conceive that p is to imagine a world one takes to verify p, and the resulting appearance of possibility is defeasible evidence in the way perceptual appearance is. Chalmers (2002) split the notion along two axes, ideal against prima facie and primary against secondary, so that the Kripkean cases are absorbed — the water-thinker conceives a primarily possible scenario that is not secondarily possible — and the surviving thesis is that ideal primary positive conceivability entails primary possibility. That thesis is the engine of the zombie argument at `mind.consciousness.anti-physicalist-arguments`, and its critics — Hill and the phenomenal-concept strategists among them — ask whether "ideal" has been defined so as to guarantee the entailment or discovered to satisfy it. Bealer's modal reliabilism, on which intuitions are evidence in virtue of a modal tie between conceptual competence and truth, and Peacocke's principle-based conception, on which knowledge of possibility is knowledge of what the principles of possibility permit, are the rationalist positions that accept the entailment while trying to explain why it holds.

Two lines of skepticism press against the rationalist mainstream. Van Inwagen's modal skepticism (1998), promised at `modal-epistemology.modal-skepticism`, concedes the everyday — the table could have stood elsewhere — and denies knowledge of remote possibilities, since our imaginative verdicts are trained on cases near actuality and give out precisely where philosophy's arguments live: transparent iron, a maximally great being, a zombie. Yablo's account itself predicts this, since imagining a verifying world requires detail the remote cases never supply. Nozick's evolutionary version (2001) adds a debunking explanation: a capacity selected for tracking nearby possibilities has no reason to be reliable beyond them. The other line is Williamson's relocation (2007). Evaluating counterfactuals is an ordinary skill, and "necessarily p" is equivalent to "if not-p were the case, a contradiction would be"; so modal knowledge is counterfactual knowledge and needs no dedicated faculty, no rationalist intuition, and no positivist convention. The deflation is genuine but, as the child observes, vindicates less than it seems: the imaginative simulations behind counterfactual judgment are reliable where experience constrains them, which is roughly van Inwagen's safe zone, and Roca-Royes (2011) presses that knowledge of necessities, on Williamson's equivalence, rests on counterpossibles the simulation heuristic handles worst.

Two programs bypass conceivability entirely. Lowe's essentialist epistemology, following the Finean inversion, holds that to grasp what a thing is — which any thinker who succeeds in referring to it already does — is to grasp its essence, from which modal truth is derived; this makes the epistemology of essence prior to the epistemology of modality, and exactly as secure as our claim to know essences non-modally. Modal empiricism, promised at `modal-epistemology.modal-empiricism`, grounds modal knowledge in actuality: Roca-Royes's similarity-based route (this glass can break because a relevantly similar one did), Strohminger's perceptual knowledge of nonactual possibilities, Fischer's justification via theories, and the general strategy of extrapolating from experienced variation. Empiricism is safe for nearby possibility, silent about zombies, and content to be so. Two constraints frame the whole region. Peacocke's integration challenge asks any account to reconcile its metaphysics of modality with its epistemology — the Benacerraf problem for Lewis's worlds is its sharpest instance, and the essentialist and deflationist accounts are attractive in large part because they can answer it. And the stakes are out of proportion to the security: the zombie argument, Cartesian disembodiment, and the modal ontological argument at `religion.existence-of-god` — where S5 converts the possibility of a necessarily existing perfect being into its actuality, and the possibility of its absence into its impossibility — each turn on a single remote-possibility premise. Every account surveyed draws a line between trustworthy and untrustworthy modal judgment, and each, as the child notes, draws it where its proponents' other commitments need it.

## Individuals across possibilities

The last region asks what it is for one thing to figure in many possibilities; `counterparts` maps it. The problem was posed by Chisholm's "Identity Through Possible Worlds" (1967) and Kaplan's "Transworld Heir Lines" (a 1967 talk, published 1979), which asked for criteria of transworld identity — qualitative marks by which to locate an individual across worlds — and found none. Kripke's response was deflation: the demand assumes worlds are distant planets seen through a telescope, their inhabitants awaiting identification, whereas worlds are stipulated; when we say Humphrey might have won, we specify the possibility as one about Humphrey, and no qualitative criterion is needed. For anyone whose worlds are abstract representations, transworld identity is trivial, since the representation mentions the man himself, and Plantinga's *The Nature of Necessity* made the point at length. Lewis cannot say this. Concrete worlds do not overlap, and Lewis argued they could not: an individual literally in two worlds would have incompatible accidental intrinsics, five fingers here and six there, with no way to be both. So individuals are world-bound and represented elsewhere by counterparts — inhabitants of other worlds resembling them, in contextually relevant respects, more closely than anything else there does. Counterparthood is similarity and inherits its disorder: intransitive, possibly asymmetric, one-many or many-one, and, crucially, inconstant, since which respects matter shifts with how the object is picked out.

Kripke's footnote objection — Humphrey could not care less whether someone else wins elsewhere — remains the intuitive case against counterparts. Lewis's reply, that the modal property of possibly winning belongs to Humphrey himself in virtue of his counterpart's victory, just as on ersatzism it belongs to him in virtue of a set of sentences, is the standard answer; the deeper reply, pressed by Divers and others, is that every non-primitivist treatment of de re modality makes it consist in something other than the man's own victory, so that the objection is the demand for primitive modality in disguise. The formal objections are sharper. Hazen (1979) showed that Lewis's 1968 translation scheme mishandles certain iterated modalities, and Fara and Williamson (2005) that no counterpart-theoretic translation validates the right logic for an actuality operator; the formal costs and the available revisions are homed at `logic.modal`. Forbes's *Languages of Possibility* (1989) and Stalnaker's "Counterparts and Identity" (1986) showed that counterpart semantics can be detached from concrete worlds and run by actualists who want its flexibility without its ontology.

That flexibility is the theory's genuine dividend. Gibbard's Lumpl and Goliath, a lump and a statue created and destroyed together, seem to be one thing; yet Lumpl could survive squashing and Goliath could not, and Leibniz's Law then forces two coincident objects. Counterpart theory dissolves the puzzle: one object, two names invoking different counterpart relations, so that de re modal predication is covertly relative in the way "big" is. Admirers count this as constitution solved for free, and it competes directly with coincidentalism and constitution views at `metaphysics.material-objects`; critics reply that inconstancy relocates modality from things to descriptions and so half-concedes what Quine alleged and Kripke had discredited. The deepest fault line is haecceitism — whether worlds can differ non-qualitatively, in which individuals occupy which roles — promised at `counterparts.haecceitism`. Chisholm's paradox builds a sorites: Adam and Noah swap properties one world at a time until a world qualitatively just like ours has the two men interchanged, and tolerant essences together with the transitivity of accessibility deliver either that conclusion or arbitrary cutoffs. Salmon's *Reference and Essence* (1981) and "The Logic of What Might Have Been" (1989) drop transitivity, and with it S4; Forbes blurs essences; Lewis exploits intransitive counterparthood to halt the slide without touching the logic and offers cheap haecceitism — one world representing distinct de re possibilities under different counterpart mappings — while denying that worlds themselves differ non-qualitatively. Adams's "Primitive Thisness and Primitive Identity" (1979) argues the other side: thisnesses are primitive, as a world of two indiscernible spheres shows, and identity across worlds needs no qualitative grounding at all — Kripke's stipulation vindicated from beneath. The necessity of identity, proved by Marcus and made famous by Kripke, and stage theory's borrowing of the counterpart relation to run persistence through time, are the shared border with `metaphysics.identity`.

## The hyperintensional frontier

All four regions now run into one wall. Possible-worlds semantics identifies necessarily equivalent contents: every necessary truth is the same proposition, every impossibility the same, being triangular is being trilateral, and any two essences that necessitate the same facts are indistinguishable. But essence, ground, explanation, and intentional content all cut finer than necessary equivalence — Socrates and his singleton necessitate each other, and the grounding literature at `metaphysics.grounding` exists because the priority between them is nonetheless real. Nolan (2014) called the resulting shift the hyperintensional revolution, and three responses compete. Impossible worlds (Nolan 1997; Berto and Jago 2019) add worlds where the impossible happens, so that counterpossible conditionals can be non-vacuous and distinct necessities can be told apart; the cost is a semantics whose worlds are not closed under consequence, and Williamson defends the orthodox vacuous treatment of counterpossibles against it. Structured propositions locate the extra grain in the constituents and mode of combination of contents rather than in worlds. The intensionalist camp has its own best argument, and it is not merely conservative: Stalnaker holds that apparent hyperintensional differences are differences in how a content is represented, not in what is the case, so that the extra grain belongs to the theory of mind and language rather than to metaphysics; Lewis, in "General Semantics," had already built structured meanings out of intensions, so that a worlds theorist recovers grain without leaving worlds; and Williamson's vacuism about counterpossibles is presented as the principled cost of a logical space closed under consequence rather than as an oversight. Fine's truthmaker semantics (2012, 2017) replaces worlds with states — partial, possibly incoherent situations that exactly verify or falsify — and recovers counterfactuals, ground, and essence within one framework and without possible worlds at all. Whether the future belongs to enriched worlds, structured contents, or exact verification is a question this node shares with `logic.modal` and its hyperintensionality child; on the view Nolan and Fine press, the possible world, the field's central instrument for fifty years, has become one tool among several rather than the frame in which every question is posed.

The state of play is therefore a set of live oppositions. On the source of necessity: essentialism against deflationism, with primitivism refusing the question, the powers theory as the naturalist alternative, and reductive realism as the fully priced option almost nobody buys. On ontology: necessitism against contingentism, now sharing the stage with realism against ersatzism. On essence: definitional against modal accounts. On epistemology: rationalist conceivability against counterfactual competence, essentialist understanding, and empiricist extrapolation, with modal skepticism disciplining all four. On individuals: stipulated identity against counterparts, anti-haecceitism against primitive thisness. On semantics: worlds against states.

## Assessment

Editorially: that practitioners quantify over worlds they do not believe in is not theft; it is what using a well-tested model with an open interpretation looks like, and the right pressure on the practice is not "what are worlds?" but "what in the model is doing the work?" On that question the recent evidence favors the Finean camp: the distinctions metaphysics actually needs — priority, definition, ground — are hyperintensional, and no ontology of worlds, however lavish, recovers them. That leaves essentialism holding the two bills this entry keeps presenting: Blackburn's, which it answers only by declaring natures the place where explanation properly stops, and the epistemological bill, which it has not answered at all. The deflationists' advantage is that they can pay the second; their cost is that the necessity they deliver is not the one anyone was asking about.

- **Logic (`logic.modal`)**: the two halves of one debate — formalism there, ontology and epistemology here.
