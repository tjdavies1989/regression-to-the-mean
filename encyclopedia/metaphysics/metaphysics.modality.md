# Modality

Some truths could have been otherwise; others could not. There are eight planets, but there might have been nine; two and two make four, and nothing could have made them five. The distinction is as ordinary as regret — "I could have caught that train" — and as entrenched as science, whose laws are supposed to support counterfactuals mere accidents do not. Yet everything we ever observe is actual. Where, then, is the difference between what is and what must be? What are we talking about when we talk about what never happened but could have?

The formal grammar of the question — boxes and diamonds, worlds and accessibility — is homed at `logic.modal`. The semantics runs identically on every answer to the ontological question, which is why that question falls here: what *are* possible worlds?

Lewis's answer deserves its full strength. Worlds are concrete universes, spatiotemporally isolated from ours, differing from actuality in what happens but not in kind; "actual" is an indexical, like "here." The reward is a genuine reduction: modality, counterfactuals, properties, propositions, and mental content all analyzed without modal residue. The incredulous stare the theory provokes is not an argument, Lewis insisted, and no rival has matched its explanatory yield. Ersatzists — Plantinga's maximal possible states of affairs, Adams's world-stories built from actual propositions, linguistic ersatzism's sets of sentences — keep the machinery while making worlds actual abstracta; the price is that "possible" reappears, unanalyzed, in specifying which ersatz worlds count, so the reduction is surrendered. Fictionalists cut deeper: worlds-talk is a useful fiction, true only prefixed by "according to the modal fiction" — inheriting ersatzism's economy along with the awkward question of what makes that fiction, rather than another, correct.

A second axis: which properties do things have necessarily? Kripke made essentialism respectable again — this table could not have been made of ice; Socrates could not have been a fried egg. Fine then inverted the standard picture: essence cannot be *defined* as necessary property-having, since Socrates necessarily belongs to the set {Socrates}, yet nothing in what Socrates is involves that set. Essence is closer to real definition, and necessity flows from it rather than the reverse — a reversal that helped ignite the grounding literature (`grounding`). Kripke also uncoupled necessity from apriority: that Hesperus is Phosphorus, that water is H₂O, are necessary but discoverable only empirically. Two-dimensionalism (Chalmers, Jackson) is the leading attempt to restore order, distinguishing two intensions of a term and locating the necessary a posteriori where they come apart.

Finally, the epistemology. If possibilities are causally isolated — concrete or abstract — how do we know any modal truth beyond the actual? Conceivability arguments, refined by Chalmers into the claim that ideal positive conceivability entails possibility, carry famous cargo, above all the zombie argument against physicalism. Williamson deflates the mystery: modal knowledge is a byproduct of our ordinary, evolutionarily unremarkable competence with counterfactuals — no special faculty required, but also no guarantee for philosophy's exotic cases, where van Inwagen's modal skepticism bites. And from the logic side comes hyperintensional pressure: essence, ground, and content all cut finer than necessary equivalence, suggesting that worlds — whatever they are — are too coarse a sieve for the distinctions metaphysics now trades in.

## Bridges

- **Logic (`logic.modal`)**: the two halves of one debate — the formalism there, the metaphysics and epistemology here; Kripke semantics prices every ontology of worlds equally, so the bill is paid on this side. Hyperintensionality is the pressure that crossing now transmits.
- **Language**: rigid designation, the necessary a posteriori, and two-dimensional semantics are joint property with the theory of reference.
- **Epistemology**: modal knowledge is a standing test case for rationalism about the a priori and for the limits of armchair method.
- **Mind**: the conceivability arguments against physicalism consume modal epistemology's verdicts directly; zombies live or die by them.
