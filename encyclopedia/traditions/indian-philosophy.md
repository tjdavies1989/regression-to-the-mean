# Indian Philosophy

Indian philosophy organized itself, long before any outsider did, into **darśanas** — literally "viewings," standpoints from which the whole of reality is seen. The classical doxographies count six āstika ("affirmer") schools — Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta, Sāṃkhya, Yoga — set against the nāstika schools: the Buddhists, the Jains, and the materialist Cārvāka. The division is the tradition's own, but its lens distorts if pressed. "Āstika" tracks acceptance of Vedic authority, not theism — Sāṃkhya and Mīmāṃsā are āstika and functionally atheist — and the tidy list of six is a late systematization laid over a far messier history in which schools paired off (Nyāya with Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya with Yoga), absorbed one another, and defined themselves chiefly through combat across the āstika/nāstika line. The doxographic frame is a datum about the tradition's self-understanding, not a map of its live fault-lines.

The genuinely shared framework is epistemological. Every school had to declare its **pramāṇas** — the instruments of knowledge it accepted: perception, inference, testimony, analogy, and others, in inventories ranging from one (Cārvāka: perception alone) to six (Advaita and Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā). Because a debate could be lost on epistemology before metaphysics was reached, pramāṇa theory became the battleground on which all schools met, with agreed rules of engagement and a technical vocabulary refined over fifteen centuries. Whether testimony is an irreducible source of knowledge, whether perception is ever conceptually structured, what distinguishes genuine inference from its fallacious look-alikes — these were fought out at a level of precision that modern epistemology has only intermittently matched. This is why the tradition must not be read as "spirituality": its central genre is the technical treatise on knowledge.

Three debates organize the terrain. The longest is over the **self**: Nyāya defended a substantial ātman as the necessary owner of memory, desire, and recognition — how can what I remember have been experienced by no one? — against the Buddhist reduction of persons to causally continuous bundles of momentary events; a thousand years of increasingly sophisticated argument ran between them. The second is the **reality of universals**: Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika realism about cowhood confronted the Buddhist apoha ("exclusion") theory, on which general terms mark only the exclusion of everything non-cow — a nominalism with no clean Western twin. The third concerns **consciousness and its objects**: whether awareness illuminates itself or requires a further act to be known, and whether its objects are external, internal (Yogācāra), or ultimately non-dual with it (Advaita).

These debates were conducted in a distinctive dialectical genre. A classical treatise proceeds by **pūrvapakṣa**: the opponent's position is stated first, at full strength, often across many pages, before the author's siddhānta answers it. The steelman is not a virtue here but a formal requirement — a school's texts are among the best sources for its rivals' arguments.

Modern reception has been vexed. Colonial-era scholarship filed the darśanas under "Eastern religion"; twentieth-century "comparative philosophy" often reversed the disrespect by promoting Indian thinkers as anticipators of Hume, Kant, or Wittgenstein — flattery that concedes the Western frame sets the questions. The current generation (Matilal, Mohanty, Ganeri, and successors) argues instead for treating Sanskrit philosophy as philosophy simpliciter: a parallel, rigorous tradition whose different carving of the problems is itself evidence about which carvings are natural. The politics of that argument — who gets counted a philosopher, and by whose criteria — remains live.

## Children

- `nyaya-vaisesika` — **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika** — realist metaphysics of substances, universals, and atoms wedded to the tradition's canonical logic and epistemology. *(anchor)*
- `mimamsa` — **Mīmāṃsā** — Vedic hermeneutics as a philosophy of language, testimony, and ritual obligation, atheist in all but name. *(standard)*
- `vedanta` — **Vedānta** — the Upaniṣadic schools from Śaṅkara's non-dualism to Rāmānuja and Madhva: the self–Brahman relation contested. *(anchor)*
- `samkhya-yoga` — **Sāṃkhya-Yoga** — dualism of consciousness and nature, with Yoga as its practical discipline. *(standard)*
- `buddhist-schools` — **Buddhist Schools in India** — pointer: the debates seen from the other side live at `traditions/buddhist-philosophy`. *(satellite)*
- `jain-philosophy` — **Jain Philosophy** — anekāntavāda: many-sidedness as metaphysics and the sevenfold predication as its logic. *(standard)*
- `grammarians` — **The Grammarians** — Bhartṛhari and the Pāṇinian tradition: language as the deep structure of reality. *(satellite)*
- `navya-nyaya` — **Navya-Nyāya** — the "new logic" from Gaṅgeśa onward: a technical language for cognition rivaling anything before Frege. *(standard)*
