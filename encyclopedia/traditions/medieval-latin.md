# Medieval Latin Philosophy

Scholasticism names a method before it names any doctrine. The word comes from the *schola*, and what the schools of Latin Europe built between roughly 1100 and 1400 was a technology for arguing. The **quaestio** states a problem, marshals the authorities and arguments on each side, resolves it, and then answers every objection in turn; the **disputatio** is its live form, a master defending theses against all comers, and in the *quodlibet* on any topic whatever. Aquinas's *Summa* is not a treatise but thousands of quaestiones; Scotus and Ockham survive largely as commentaries on Peter Lombard's *Sentences*, the textbook every theologian was obliged to think through. The form made the steelman a requirement — objections came first — and rewarded the drawing of distinctions, which is why scholastic prose reads as it does. Its institutional carrier was new: the university, formed at Bologna, Paris, and Oxford around 1200, with an arts faculty that taught logic and Aristotle to everyone and a theology faculty whose problems set the metaphysical agenda.

The arc begins before the schools. Boethius (d. 524) planned to translate all of Plato and Aristotle and completed only the logical works; for six centuries the *logica vetus* — the *Categories*, *On Interpretation*, Porphyry's *Isagoge* — was nearly all the Aristotle the West possessed, and Boethius's own treatises fixed the problem of universals in the form Porphyry had posed it. Anselm of Canterbury (d. 1109) worked inside that inheritance with a confidence in unaided reason, *sola ratione*, that produced the ontological argument of the *Proslogion*: that than which nothing greater can be conceived cannot exist in the understanding alone. The twelfth-century renaissance made the argument culture self-aware. Peter Abelard's *Sic et Non* set contradictory authorities side by side as an exercise; his logic developed a nominalism on which universals are significant words (*sermones*) rather than things; and his *Ethics* located sin in consent rather than in act — a morality of intention the tradition would wrestle with for centuries.

The influx changed everything. Between about 1150 and 1270, through translators at Toledo and in Sicily, the Latin schools acquired the whole Aristotle — *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *De Anima*, the *Ethics* — and, packaged with it, the Arabic commentators: Avicenna's distinction of essence from existence, Averroes' reading of the intellect as one for all humans. Islamic philosophy was not one influence among others but the channel through which Aristotle arrived already as a system (see `traditions/islamic-philosophy`). The thirteenth century answered with synthesis under strain. Aquinas took Aristotle as philosophy's settled form and argued that nature so understood could be completed by grace without contradiction: essence and existence really distinct in creatures, God as the pure act of being, the human being one substance whose soul is the form of the body. Bonaventure, his Franciscan contemporary, kept an Augustinian frame in which illumination and the divine ideas did real work and Aristotle was a guest. In 1277 the bishop of Paris condemned 219 propositions, many of them Averroist, some close enough to Aquinas to sting. Historians dispute the effect; the standard view is that the condemnation loosened the tradition — if God's power is not bound by Aristotle's necessities, nature could have been otherwise, a thought with a long future.

The fourteenth century turned. Duns Scotus (d. 1308) argued that "being" is said univocally of God and creatures, or theology could not reason at all; that each individual has a *haecceitas*, a thisness irreducible to its common nature; and that the will is a power for opposites, not determined by the intellect's judgment. William of Ockham (d. 1347) drew the harder line: only individuals exist, universals are concepts, and common natures, real relations, and formal distinctions are entities multiplied beyond necessity. The razor was an ontological policy rather than a maxim, paired with an epistemology on which intuitive cognition grounds all contingent knowledge. Nominalism became the *via moderna*. Jean Buridan, at Paris, then turned the apparatus on nature — impetus against Aristotle's account of projectile motion — and Nicole Oresme graphed intensities of qualities and argued that the earth's daily rotation could not be ruled out, both without leaving the scholastic frame.

Logic was the tradition's most original product. Supposition theory analyzed what a term stands for in a given proposition — personal, simple, material — and built a contextual semantics with no ancient source; the *consequentiae* generalized inference beyond the syllogism; *obligationes* turned disputation into rule-governed games; the *insolubilia* literature attacked the liar. This work bridges `logic.history` and is the period's clearest claim on the problem tree.

The "dark ages" were a Renaissance and Enlightenment invention, useful for making humanism and the new science look like beginnings. The correction has been under way for a century, from Grabmann and Gilson through Kretzmann and the *Cambridge History*: the period was a continuous, institutionally supported, technically demanding argument culture, and the seventeenth century's founders were its students before they were its critics — Descartes was schooled at La Flèche, and Suárez stands behind Leibniz.
