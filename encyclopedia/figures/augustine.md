# Augustine of Hippo

*354–430 · Latin Christian philosopher and bishop of Roman North Africa; Platonist by formation, theologian by office, and founder of the Western tradition's interior turn*

## Why they matter

Augustine is the hinge on which ancient philosophy turns into medieval and, by a longer arc, into modern. He received Plato through Plotinus and Cicero and rebuilt that inheritance around three things Greek thought had scarcely needed: a will that can fail to do what it knows, a self that finds God and itself by turning inward, and a history that goes somewhere. Evil as privation fixed the terms of theodicy; the quarrel with Pelagius made the will a problem the West has argued about for sixteen centuries; the *Confessions* found in memory and time-consciousness a subject matter phenomenology would later claim; the *si fallor, sum* anticipated Descartes closely enough that Descartes had to answer the charge of borrowing; *De Magistro* supplied the picture of language Wittgenstein spent the *Investigations* dismantling; and the *City of God* founded political theology. No thinker between Aristotle and Aquinas is cited more in this encyclopedia's problem tree.

## Life

Augustine was born in 354 at Thagaste in Numidia (now Souk Ahras, Algeria), to a pagan minor official, Patricius, and a Christian mother, Monica. He was turned to philosophy at nineteen by Cicero's lost *Hortensius* and spent nine years as a Manichean "hearer." He took a concubine, with whom he had a son, Adeodatus, and taught rhetoric at Carthage and Rome before winning the imperial chair at Milan in 384. There Ambrose's allegorical preaching removed his objections to Scripture, the "books of the Platonists" (Plotinus and Porphyry in Latin) gave him an immaterial God and a non-substantial evil, and a crisis in a Milanese garden in August 386 ended in conversion. He resigned, wrote dialogues in retreat at Cassiciacum, was baptized at Easter 387, and lost Monica at Ostia that year. Back in Africa he was seized by the congregation of Hippo Regius and ordained priest in 391, becoming bishop about 395. The remaining decades were a bishop's: sermons, letters, and three controversies — against the Manicheans, the Donatist schism, and Pelagius and his heirs. He died on 28 August 430 with the Vandals besieging Hippo.

## Works

- **The Cassiciacum dialogues** (386–87) — *Against the Academicians*, *Soliloquies*: skepticism refuted.
- ***De Magistro*** (*The Teacher*, 389) — a dialogue with Adeodatus on signs and whether anything can be taught.
- ***On Free Choice of the Will*** (388–95) — evil's origin in the will, the proof of God from the eternity of truth, foreknowledge reconciled with freedom.
- ***Confessions*** (397–401) — autobiography as philosophy: the divided will (VIII), memory (X), time and creation (XI–XIII).
- ***On Christian Teaching*** (396–426) — the general theory of signs and things, use and enjoyment.
- ***On the Trinity*** (399–c. 420) — its second half a philosophy of mind: the self's knowledge of itself.
- ***The City of God*** (413–26) — the two cities, the critique of Roman virtue, the philosophy of history.
- **The anti-Pelagian writings** (412–30) — *On the Spirit and the Letter*, *On Nature and Grace*, *On the Predestination of the Saints*.

## Thought

### The divided will

Augustine's most consequential discovery is narrated rather than argued. In *Confessions* VIII he describes wanting to convert and not doing so, and asks what such a state can be. The body obeys the mind's command instantly; the mind commands itself and is not obeyed. His diagnosis rejects both available explanations. It is not, as the Manicheans held, that two natures contend inside him. Nor is it, as Socratic intellectualism implied, that he lacks knowledge of the good; he knows it perfectly well. The will is *one* faculty divided against itself: it does not will wholly, and a partial will is not a defective belief but a distinct kind of failure. This is the moment the will becomes a philosophical topic in its own right — no longer Aristotle's rational desire, but a power that can withhold itself from what reason approves. Whether he invented the will (Dihle, Arendt) or assembled it from Stoic parts is contested; either way, after him weakness of will is a problem about the will, not the intellect, and the interior turn — *truth dwells in the inner man* — becomes the standard Western route to foundations.

### Memory and time: Confessions X–XI

Book X is a phenomenology of memory as the whole interior field: the "vast palaces" holding sensory images, but also the truths of number, never sensed and so somehow present before they were found; even forgetting, which must be remembered to be recognized. The mind is too narrow to contain itself — the self is not transparent to introspection. Book XI turns to the medium. A Manichean asks what God was doing before creation; the question is confused, Augustine replies, since time is a creature, made with the world, and there is no "before." That forces the question he famously confesses he cannot answer when asked. The past no longer exists, the future does not yet, and the present has no duration — so how is time measured, and what is measured? His answer relocates time's extension into the mind: we measure the impression things leave, not the vanished things; the "present of past things" is memory, of present things attention, of future things expectation; and the recitation of a psalm, in which expectation drains into memory through a moving point of attention, is the model of every temporal whole. This *distentio animi*, the soul's stretching-out, is not idealism about time — time is created and real — but a claim that its measurable extension exists only for a mind. Husserl opened his lectures on inner time-consciousness with it, and Ricoeur built *Time and Narrative* on it. Its descendants are the A-theorist's moving present and the phenomenologist's retention–protention structure.

### Against the Manicheans: evil as privation

Manichaeism explained evil by dualism: two coeternal principles, light and darkness, whose mixture is the world. Augustine's objection, owed to Plotinus, is that this makes God a bounded, corruptible substance and evil a thing with a nature. His alternative: everything that exists is good insofar as it exists; evil is not a substance but a **privation** — the absence of a good that ought to be present, as blindness is absence of sight in something meant to see. Metaphysically the doctrine lets a good creator make a world containing evil without making evil, since he made no *thing* that is evil. Morally it relocates evil's origin in the will: sin is the will turning from higher goods to lower, a disordered love, and the turning has no efficient cause — seeking what caused the will's defection is like trying to see darkness. Two objections have followed the doctrine ever since: that pain seems a positive reality, not an absence, and that a will able to defect without cause is either a brute fact that explains nothing or a flaw in the design. Augustine's answer to the second, in *On Free Choice*, is the ancestor of every free-will theodicy: a will that can go wrong is a greater good than none, and its misuse is chargeable to the user.

### Against the Pelagians: original sin and grace

Pelagius, an ascetic in Rome, read Augustine's prayer *give what you command, and command what you will* as an alibi for laxity. His position: human nature retains the capacity not to sin; grace is the law, forgiveness, and Christ's example; sin is imitated, not inherited. Augustine's reply is the doctrine that made the will a problem for a millennium. Adam's sin damaged human nature itself, transmitted through generation as **concupiscence**, so that all are born into a condemned mass; the fallen will is free in that it acts from itself, but not free to will the good without prevenient grace, given rather than earned, and given to some rather than others by a predestination whose reasons are hidden. He schematized four states: able not to sin before the fall, unable not to sin after it, able not to sin under grace, unable to sin in glory. The philosophical core is a distinction between *liberum arbitrium*, the will's self-movement, and *libertas*, the power to do good, which grace restores — a compatibilism before the name, in which freedom is not the two-way power the young Augustine had defended but the will's being rightly ordered. The Pelagians replied that a will unable to do otherwise cannot be blamed, and that Augustine had smuggled Manichean pessimism back into the faith; the councils sided with Augustine, but the moderated version adopted at Orange in 529 left the argument open, and it has never closed.

### Certainty and illumination

Cicero's Academics had taught Augustine that nothing can be known with certainty, and the Cassiciacum dialogues are his escape. The core argument is that even if I am deceived, I exist — deception requires someone deceived. Extended: I know that I am, that I live, and that I understand, and no skeptical scenario touches these because they are not judgments about a world beyond the mind. Unlike Descartes, he founds no method on this: the truths that matter to him are not private. That the truths of number and wisdom are necessary, unchanging, and common to all minds is for him proof of something above the mind, and the epistemology built to explain our access to them is **illumination**: the mind sees intelligible truths in a divine light as the eye sees bodies in the sun. Its meaning is disputed, from the strong reading (we see the ideas in God's mind — Malebranche's later position) to the weak (God guarantees the mind's a priori capacities). Historically it is the rival to abstraction: where Aristotle and Aquinas derive intelligibles from sense, Augustine derives them from an interior source, and the Franciscans held that line against the Dominicans for a century.

### Signs and the inner teacher

Augustine wrote the Latin tradition's first general theory of signs. *On Christian Teaching* divides everything into things and signs, signs into natural (smoke) and given (words). *De Magistro* draws a skeptical conclusion. Words, Adeodatus is led to agree, teach nothing: to learn a word is to know already the thing it signifies, and to learn a thing from words is impossible, since signs only prompt us to look. Pointing does not help, because a gesture is itself a sign that must be interpreted. We learn by consulting either things themselves or the truth within, where the inner teacher, Christ, speaks. Wittgenstein opened the *Philosophical Investigations* with Augustine's description in *Confessions* I of learning to speak by watching adults name objects, and made it the type of a picture in which every word names and meaning is the thing named. The irony scholars have noted is that *De Magistro* already contains the *Investigations*' argument that ostension underdetermines meaning. Augustine concluded that meaning must come from within, Wittgenstein that it must come from use.

### The two cities, political order, and just war

The *City of God*, written after Alaric's sack of Rome in 410, became a philosophy of history. All humanity belongs to one of two cities, defined not by institutions but by loves: the earthly city by love of self to the contempt of God, the heavenly by love of God to the contempt of self. Both are mixed in every institution, the Church included, so political order is neither sacred nor damned but provisional. The critique of Cicero is the book's sharpest argument. Cicero defined a commonwealth as a people bound by agreement about right; but there is no right without justice and no justice where God is not given his due, so by that standard Rome never was one. Augustine substitutes a value-neutral definition — a people is a multitude united by agreement about the objects of its love — so that states are judged by what they love, and a kingdom without justice differs from a robber band only in scale. Earthly peace is a genuine good both cities share; coercive government is a remedy for sin, not part of the original order. From this he also drew the legitimacy of coercing the Donatists — the founding text of Christian religious persecution. His scattered remarks on war became, through Gratian and Aquinas, the just war tradition: war may be waged only by legitimate authority, for a just cause such as repairing injury, and with right intention, peace as its aim; the real evils of war are cruelty and lust for domination, not killing as such.

## Influence and reception

For the Latin Middle Ages Augustine was less an author than the atmosphere. Anselm's *faith seeking understanding* is his; Peter Lombard's *Sentences* is largely Augustine excerpted; Bonaventure and the Franciscans made illumination and the primacy of will a program against the Aristotelian Dominicans, while Aquinas translated Augustinian conclusions into Aristotelian premises. The anti-Pelagian Augustine passed through Bradwardine and Gregory of Rimini to **Luther**, an Augustinian friar whose bondage of the will is the anti-Pelagian doctrine radicalized, and to Calvin, who cited him more than any other author. The Reformation has been called the victory of Augustine's doctrine of grace over his doctrine of the church; Jansenism, Pascal's party, tried to keep both inside Catholicism.

Modern philosophy's debt runs through **Descartes**, whose *cogito* Arnauld at once compared to the *si fallor, sum*; Descartes replied that Augustine had used the argument to prove the soul immaterial while he used it to found a method — a real difference, though Menn and others have shown how Augustinian the *Meditations* is in structure. In the twentieth century three revivals converged. **Husserl** and Heidegger found in *Confessions* X–XI a phenomenology of time and of the self's restlessness, and Heidegger's 1921 course on Augustine fed *Being and Time*. **Arendt**'s 1929 dissertation on love in Augustine extracted the idea of natality that anchored her political thought, and she later named him the first philosopher of the will; the existentialist Augustine — divided self, anxiety, the choice that makes a life — became a standard reading. **Wittgenstein** made him the *Investigations*' opening interlocutor and returned to Book XI in the *Blue Book*.

The pushback is as old as the doctrines. Julian of Eclanum called original sin residual Manichaeism and never received a satisfying reply; Nietzsche took him as the type of a morality that makes sickness a virtue. The strongest modern criticism targets the knot of concupiscence, sexuality, and guilt, which Pagels and others hold burdened Western culture, and the justification of coercion from which the tradition of persecution descends.

## Reading path

Begin with the ***Confessions*** (Chadwick's or Boulding's translation), with X and XI as the philosophical core. Then ***On Free Choice of the Will*** (Williams's translation): Book I on evil's origin, II for the proof of God and illumination, III for foreknowledge. ***The Teacher*** (King's translation) is short and belongs beside the opening of the *Investigations*. For politics, ***City of God*** Books XIV and XIX (Dyson's translation). Secondary: Chadwick's *Very Short Introduction* to orient; Peter Brown's *Augustine of Hippo* as the biography; Kirwan's *Augustine* for the analytic treatment; Wetzel's *Augustine and the Limits of Virtue* on the will; Sorabji's *Time, Creation and the Continuum* for Book XI in context.

## Assessment

Editorially: Augustine is the canon's clearest case of a philosopher whose worst doctrines and best discoveries share a root. The interior turn that produced the phenomenology of time and the first real account of a divided will is the same turn that, pressed by controversy, produced a will so damaged only arbitrary grace could move it. Later thinkers have tried to keep the first without the second, and the persistence of the attempt — Descartes, Husserl, Arendt, Wittgenstein each taking one piece — measures how much of the modern self is his.
