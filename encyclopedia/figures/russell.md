# Bertrand Russell

*1872–1970 · British logician, philosopher, and public moralist; with Frege and Moore a founder of the analytic tradition, and its first exemplar of philosophy as logical analysis*

## Why they matter

Russell is the figure through whom Frege's logic became a philosophical method. Frege built quantificational logic to ground arithmetic; Russell found the contradiction in that grounding, rebuilt the foundations in *Principia Mathematica*, and then turned the instrument outward, arguing that the grammatical form of a sentence systematically misleads about its logical form and that most classical metaphysical puzzles are artifacts of the mismatch. The theory of descriptions was the demonstration piece — Ramsey called it a paradigm of philosophy, and for half a century it was — and its habit of trading inferred entities for logical constructions ran through everything Russell later wrote on perception, matter, and mind. He was also, unusually for a technical philosopher, one of the century's public consciences: jailed twice, dismissed from Cambridge, barred from a New York chair, and a Nobel laureate in literature, all for holding in public the same view of belief and evidence that his epistemology held in private.

## Life

Born in 1872 into the Whig aristocracy — his grandfather Lord John Russell had twice been prime minister — Russell was orphaned by four and raised at Pembroke Lodge by a devout, formidable grandmother. Euclid, taught by his brother at eleven, was, he later wrote, as dazzling as first love. He read mathematics and then moral sciences at Trinity College, Cambridge, joined the Apostles, fell under Moore's influence in the revolt against British idealism, and won a fellowship in 1895. The turning point was the Paris congress of 1900, where he met Peano and grasped what symbolic logic could do; within a year he had discovered the paradox of the class of all classes not members of themselves, and within three had published *The Principles of Mathematics*. The decade of *Principia* (with Whitehead, 1910–13) nearly broke him; Wittgenstein's arrival in 1911 finished the job by demolishing his theory of judgment.

The First World War made him a public figure. He organized for the No-Conscription Fellowship, was fined in 1916 and stripped of his Trinity lectureship, and in 1918 served six months in Brixton for an article deemed likely to prejudice relations with an ally; he wrote *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy* in his cell. Between the wars he visited Bolshevik Russia and China, ran an experimental school with his second wife Dora, and lived by journalism and popular books. In 1940 a New York court annulled his appointment at City College as morally unfit, on the strength of *Marriage and Morals*; the lectures he then gave for the Barnes Foundation became *A History of Western Philosophy* (1945), which made him solvent for life. He received the Order of Merit in 1949 and the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950. His last two decades were given to the nuclear question: the Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955), the presidency of the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, the Committee of 100's civil disobedience, and a second week in Brixton in 1961 at eighty-nine. He died in Wales in 1970.

## Works

- ***The Principles of Mathematics*** (1903) — logicism announced for the whole of mathematics; the paradox confessed in an appendix, with a first sketch of types.
- **"On Denoting"** (1905) — the theory of descriptions; the most influential twenty pages in the philosophy of language.
- ***Principia Mathematica*** (with Whitehead, 1910–13) — the formal execution: ramified type theory, the no-class theory, and mathematics derived from logic plus three contested axioms.
- ***The Problems of Philosophy*** (1912) — the "shilling shocker": acquaintance and description, sense-data, induction, universals, and the value of philosophy, in prose anyone can read.
- ***Our Knowledge of the External World*** (1914) and **"The Philosophy of Logical Atomism"** (1918) — the metaphysics of facts and the method of logical construction.
- ***Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy*** (1919) — *Principia* without symbols, written in prison.
- ***The Analysis of Mind*** (1921) and ***The Analysis of Matter*** (1927) — neutral monism and the structuralist account of physics.
- ***An Inquiry into Meaning and Truth*** (1940) and ***Human Knowledge: Its Scope and Limits*** (1948) — the late epistemology.
- ***A History of Western Philosophy*** (1945), ***Why I Am Not a Christian*** (1927/1957), and the ***Autobiography*** (1967–69) — the public Russell.

## Thought

### The theory of descriptions: the paradigm of philosophy

The problem Russell inherited was ontological inflation. If every grammatical subject stands for something — and *The Principles of Mathematics* had held that whatever can be mentioned has *being* — then "the golden mountain" and "the round square" name entities of some diminished sort, as Meinong maintained. "On Denoting" dissolves the commitment by denying that definite descriptions are names at all. "The F is G" is analyzed as a quantified claim: there is at least one F, at most one F, and whatever is F is G. The description disappears under analysis; it is an **incomplete symbol**, with no meaning in isolation and a contribution only to the sentences it occurs in. Three puzzles are then solved at once. "The present King of France is bald" is straightforwardly false rather than truth-valueless, and its negation ambiguous between a **primary** and a **secondary occurrence** of the description — the scope distinction that now runs through all of formal semantics. George IV could wonder whether Scott was the author of *Waverley* without wondering whether Scott was Scott, because the second sentence contains no description to quantify over. And "the golden mountain does not exist" is true without a golden mountain to be true of. Russell's own view was that Frege's sense–reference distinction had been an unnecessary detour: quantificational structure alone handles informativeness and empty terms, and the theory is the standing alternative to Frege in the theory of reference.

Ramsey's phrase "paradigm of philosophy" named what the theory taught by example: that surface grammar hides logical form, that a metaphysical puzzle can be a symptom of misreading it, and that analysis — not introspection or system-building — is philosophy's characteristic act. The paradigm has costs. Strawson objected in 1950 that a description presupposes rather than asserts its object's existence, so that the King of France sentence lacks a truth-value; Donnellan distinguished referential from attributive uses, which Russell's theory captures only for the latter. Kripke defended Russell against both by locating their phenomena in pragmatics, and the theory of generalized quantifiers absorbed descriptions as one determiner among many — a vindication in form, if not in ontological ambition.

### Logicism, the paradox, and the theory of types

Russell's logicism was more ambitious than Frege's — all of pure mathematics, geometry included, was to be derived from logic — and it was Russell who broke it. Reflecting on Cantor's proof that there is no greatest cardinal, he asked in 1901 whether the class of all classes that are not members of themselves is a member of itself, and found that it is if and only if it is not. The letter to Frege of June 1902 is the most consequential in the history of logic; Frege's Basic Law V, which gives every concept an extension, licenses exactly this class. The cure Russell settled on was the **theory of types**, motivated by the **vicious-circle principle** taken from Poincaré: nothing may be defined in terms of a totality to which it would belong. Propositional functions are stratified, so that a function can take as argument only entities of the type below it, and "the class of all classes not members of themselves" is not false but ungrammatical. Classes themselves are then eliminated as logical fictions — the **no-class theory** is the theory of descriptions applied to mathematics, incomplete symbols once more.

The cost is the story of *Principia*. Because the vicious-circle principle stratifies not only types but *orders* within a type — the **ramified** hierarchy — the definitions that real analysis needs, above all the least upper bound, become inexpressible. Russell's remedy was the **axiom of reducibility**, which asserts that every higher-order function has an equivalent at the lowest order; it does the work, but nobody, Russell included, could see it as a truth of logic. Two further axioms compounded the embarrassment: the **axiom of infinity**, which posits enough individuals to make arithmetic non-trivial, and the **multiplicative axiom** (choice). Logicism thus survived in a hedged form — mathematics is derivable from logic *plus* hypotheses that logic cannot certify — and Russell candidly presented the theorems as conditional on them. Ramsey showed in 1925 that the simple theory of types suffices for mathematics if the semantic paradoxes are separated from the set-theoretic ones; Gödel showed in 1931 that no system of *Principia*'s kind can be both complete and consistent. Zermelo's axiomatic set theory, which restricts comprehension rather than stratifying it, won the working mathematician; types found a second life as the foundation of programming languages.

### Logical atomism

The 1918 lectures give the metaphysics that the method presupposed. The world consists of **facts**, not things: an atomic fact is a particular's having a quality or particulars' standing in a relation, and an atomic proposition — in a logically perfect language whose structure mirrors the world's — is true just if such a fact obtains. Molecular propositions are truth-functions of atomic ones; the logical constants correspond to nothing in the world. Russell, unlike the Wittgenstein of the *Tractatus*, argued that **negative facts** and **general facts** must be admitted as well, since no collection of atomic facts entails that there are no others. Analysis terminates in **simples**, the particulars and universals with which we are acquainted, and the supreme maxim of scientific philosophizing — replace inferred entities with **logical constructions** out of what is given — is the program's engine: numbers, classes, points, instants, and physical objects are in turn reconstructed rather than posited. The difference from Wittgenstein's version is instructive rather than merely biographical. Russell's atomism was empiricist and epistemic, an inventory of what we could know; Wittgenstein's was an a priori argument about what any language must presuppose. The first is refutable by the discovery that nothing in experience is simple; the second, Wittgenstein later concluded, was refutable by looking at how language works.

### Knowledge by acquaintance and by description

The epistemology behind the atomism turns on one relation. **Acquaintance** is direct presentation of an object to a mind — of sense-data, of universals, of one's own mental states, and (Russell wavered) of the self — and it is not knowledge of truths but of things. Everything else is **knowledge by description**: we know Bismarck, or the center of the earth, only as the thing satisfying a certain description. The **principle of acquaintance** — every proposition we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted — links the two, and it is what makes ordinary proper names abbreviated descriptions: since we are not acquainted with Bismarck, "Bismarck" cannot be a genuine name for us. *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912) makes the picture into the century's most-read introduction to epistemology. The table is known through sense-data whose changing appearance leaves the physical object an inference — the simplest hypothesis, never a certainty. The chicken fed daily until the day its neck is wrung states the problem of induction with a memorable savagery; Russell's tentative answer, that the inductive principle must be accepted on its intrinsic evidence or all science falls, prefigures the postulates of 1948. Universals are argued for from the reality of relations. And the closing chapter's claim that philosophy's value lies in the uncertainty it enlarges, and the impartiality it cultivates, is the ethics of the public life stated as epistemology. Acquaintance was the sense-datum tradition's founding concept (Price, Ayer) and the target of its great critics — Austin's phenomenology of ordinary perception and Sellars's Myth of the Given, which argues that nothing given without conceptual articulation can justify a belief. Recent philosophy of perception and of phenomenal knowledge has revived the relation under Russell's own name.

### Neutral monism and the analysis of matter

*The Analysis of Mind* (1921) abandons the acquaintance relation, and with it the subject that stood at one end of it. Following James, Russell now held that mind and matter are not two kinds of stuff but two ways of grouping a single **neutral** material — sensations, or events — organized by physical laws into the series we call objects and by mnemic causation and habit into the series we call minds. Introspection reveals no ego; the self is a construction like the table. *The Analysis of Matter* (1927) extends the view to physics with a **structuralist** thesis: perception acquaints us with the intrinsic character of percepts, which are events in our own brains, while of the external world we know only its structure — the pattern of relations that physics describes. The mathematician Max Newman objected in 1928 that structure so construed is trivial: any collection of the right cardinality carries any structure whatever, so the claim reduces to a claim about how many things there are. Russell conceded the point and never fully repaired it. The position has had an afterlife its author did not foresee: **Russellian monism** in the current philosophy of consciousness — the thesis that the intrinsic properties physics leaves unspecified are, or ground, the phenomenal — draws its name and its central move from these two books.

### The later epistemology: *Human Knowledge* (1948)

Russell's last major work asks how scientific knowledge is possible given that its content outruns any possible experience. Hume's problem is taken at full strength: no deductive argument reaches from the observed to the unobserved, and probabilistic induction presupposes the very uniformity it is meant to establish. Russell's answer is a set of five **postulates of scientific inference** — of quasi-permanence, of separable causal lines, of spatiotemporal continuity, of common causal origin for similar structures, and of analogy — which are not self-evident, not provable, and not known a priori, but are presupposed by every inference from percept to physical object and are, he argued, the animal's habits made explicit. The book's conclusion is deliberately uncomfortable: either we know something independently of experience, or empiricism, taken strictly, cannot underwrite science. It also separates two notions of probability, frequency and degree of credibility, and reworks the theory of perception around the causal chain from object to percept. The reception was cold: the postwar profession had turned to ordinary language and to the later Wittgenstein, and a naturalist epistemology anchored in animal inference looked like a relic. It now reads as an early statement of the view that justification may rest on reliable dispositions the believer cannot vindicate from the inside.

### Russell and Wittgenstein

The relation ran hard in both directions. Wittgenstein arrived from Frege in 1911 and within two years had convinced Russell that the **multiple-relation theory of judgment** — belief as a relation among a mind and the constituents of a would-be fact — could not explain why the constituents formed a *possible* proposition rather than a list. Russell abandoned the 1913 *Theory of Knowledge* manuscript in a state he described as paralysis, and his 1918 lectures open by crediting the ideas to his former pupil. Russell in turn gave Wittgenstein his problems, his logic, and his publisher: the introduction to the *Tractatus* (1922) secured its printing while, on Wittgenstein's view, misunderstanding it — Russell's proposal of a hierarchy of languages to escape the saying–showing doctrine was precisely the theory of types re-applied, and Wittgenstein wanted nothing of the kind. Russell examined the *Tractatus* as a doctoral thesis in 1929 and secured Wittgenstein's return to Cambridge. Thereafter the divergence was total: Russell judged the later philosophy an abandonment of serious thinking, and Wittgenstein held Russell's popular writing in contempt. The estrangement is not merely personal. The two men fixed the tradition's two poles — philosophy as a science continuous with logic and physics, and philosophy as a therapy for the confusions such ambitions breed — and every subsequent analytic philosopher has had to choose.

### The public philosopher

Russell's politics were not a sideline but the same conviction differently applied: that belief should be proportioned to evidence and that the refusal to do so, in war, in religion, and in sexual morality, was the root of most avoidable suffering. His First World War pacifism was consequentialist rather than absolute — he supported the war against Hitler — and rested on a judgment that the war's costs exceeded any attainable good, a judgment he defended in print and paid for in Brixton. *The Practice and Theory of Bolshevism* (1920) was among the earliest liberal critiques of the Soviet state from a sympathetic visitor. *Why I Am Not a Christian* and *Marriage and Morals* apply the evidential ethic to faith and to sex, and cost him the New York chair. The Nobel citation of 1950 named his championing of humanitarian ideals and freedom of thought, which is to say it rewarded the epistemology's public face. The nuclear campaigns of his last years — the Manifesto with Einstein, the founding of Pugwash, the Committee of 100's sit-downs, and the tribunal on American conduct in Vietnam — took the same shape: a philosopher's insistence that the calculation be done in public. There is a tension he acknowledged. His metaethics moved from Moore's objectivism to a subjectivism on which moral judgments express desire, and he confessed he could not believe that his loathing of cruelty was only a preference. He never resolved the difficulty; he acted as though objectivism were true.

## Influence and reception

Russell's influence is diffuse because it became the tradition's ambient method. Carnap's *Aufbau* (1928) is *Our Knowledge of the External World* carried out in detail, and the Vienna Circle took the program of logical construction as its charter. Quine learned logic from *Principia*, took the theory of descriptions as the tool for eliminating names from ontology, and inherited the naturalism of *Human Knowledge* without acknowledging it. Ramsey simplified the types; Gödel closed the logicist road; Tarski's hierarchy of languages formalized what Russell had suggested against Wittgenstein. The pushback was equally formative. Strawson's "On Referring" launched the ordinary-language critique of the whole analytic style, not just the description theory. Kripke rescued Russell on descriptions while destroying the descriptivism about names that the principle of acquaintance had made compulsory. Sellars's assault on the given and Newman's objection to structuralism are the two technical wounds that never healed. Recent scholarship — Hylton on the escape from idealism, Griffin on the early logic, Landini and Linsky on the substitutional and *Principia* periods, and the *Collected Papers* — has restored the working philosopher behind the popularizer; Monk's biography restored the man, and did not flatter him.

## Reading path

Begin with *The Problems of Philosophy*, which is short and presupposes nothing; then "On Denoting" (in *Logic and Knowledge*, ed. Marsh), read alongside Strawson's "On Referring" and Kripke's "Speaker's Reference and Semantic Reference" to see the debate it opened. *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy* gives the logicism and the types without symbols. "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism" (also in Marsh) is the metaphysics; read it with the *Tractatus* to feel both sides of the relation. *Human Knowledge*, Part VI, contains the postulates. For the public Russell, *Why I Am Not a Christian* and the *Autobiography*. Secondary: Hylton's *Russell, Idealism, and the Emergence of Analytic Philosophy* for the origins; Sainsbury's *Russell* for the doctrines; Monk's two-volume life for the man.

## Assessment

Editorially: Russell's technical reputation rests on two achievements he did not think were his best work, "On Denoting" and the paradox, and his own favorite, the epistemology of constructions, has aged worst. That is not a paradox. His genius was diagnostic — finding the contradiction, finding the misleading form — and diagnosis is what analytic philosophy was for; the systems he built with the results were provisional by design, and he abandoned them with a candor that his critics rarely matched. The public life belongs in the same ledger. He was often wrong, sometimes badly, but he submitted his errors to the same standard he asked of everyone else, and the demand that belief answer to evidence is a legacy that outlasts every doctrine it produced.
