# David Hume

*1711–1776 · Scottish empiricist and naturalist; the Enlightenment's most consequential skeptic*

## Why they matter

Hume set out to do for the mind what Newton had done for matter: found a science of human nature on observation and experiment rather than rational insight into essences. Executed with an empiricism more consistent than Locke's or Berkeley's, the project turned corrosive, and the corrosion is his legacy. He argued that our most basic commitments — that the future will resemble the past, that causes necessitate their effects, that there is a self who has these thoughts — cannot be certified by reason at all, and rest instead on custom and feeling; then, in the naturalist turn that distinguishes him from every mere doubter, he treated that result not as catastrophe but as the first datum of the new science. The problem of induction, the regularity analysis of causation, the bundle theory of the self, morals from sentiment, and the evidential critique of religion all enter philosophy in nearly their modern form through him, and "Humean" remains the name for a whole way of seeing the world — a mosaic of particular matters of fact, with necessity, value, and self projected onto it by minds like ours.

## Life

Born in Edinburgh in 1711 to minor Borders gentry, Hume abandoned law for obsessive study and wrote *A Treatise of Human Nature* in France in his mid-twenties. The book, by his own rueful report, "fell dead-born from the press." He never held an academic post — candidacies at Edinburgh (1745) and Glasgow (1751) were defeated by clerical opposition to his reputed atheism — and lived instead as tutor, military secretary, librarian of the Advocates Library, where he wrote the *History of England* that made him famous and rich, and diplomat in Paris, where the salons adored *le bon David*. He faced his death in 1776 with a composure that unnerved Boswell, who came looking for a deathbed conversion and found cheerful unbelief; Adam Smith's tribute to his friend's nearly perfect wisdom and virtue scandalized the devout more than the *Treatise* ever had.

## Works

- ***A Treatise of Human Nature*** (1739–40) — the full system in three books (understanding, passions, morals), at its boldest and roughest; the anonymous *Abstract* (1740) is his own map of its nerve.
- ***An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*** (1748) — the *Treatise*'s first book recast for readers: shorter, sharper on induction, now containing "Of Miracles."
- ***An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*** (1751) — the moral theory refined; in Hume's own judgment, "incomparably the best" of his writings.
- ***Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary*** (1741–77) — politics, economics, and taste for a public audience; political economy before Smith.
- ***The Natural History of Religion*** (1757) — religion explained causally, as a product of fear and ignorance, rather than evaluated evidentially.
- ***Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*** (1779, posthumous) — the design argument dismantled in the finest philosophical dialogue in English; withheld during his life on his friends' advice.

## Thought

### The science of man

The *Treatise*'s subtitle announces "an attempt to introduce the experimental method of reasoning into moral subjects." The ambition is architectural: since every science is practiced by human minds, a science of human nature is the "capital" from which all others must be secured. The method is Newtonian twice over: it renounces hypotheses about ultimate essences — inquiry stops at the most general principles observation supports — and it seeks a few simple laws of mental gravitation, found in the **association of ideas**: resemblance, contiguity, and cause and effect, the "gentle force" by which thoughts attract one another. The radical move is the reversal this licenses: where the rationalist tradition asks what reason can demonstrate and treats the rest as failure, Hume asks what the mind actually does and treats reason as one natural operation among others — weaker, in most of life, than habit and passion. Skepticism and naturalism are thus two faces of one project: the skeptical arguments demolish reason's pretensions precisely so that the experimental account of belief-formation can take over the explanatory work.

### Impressions, ideas, and the copy principle

Hume's inventory of the mind contains only **perceptions**, divided by force and vivacity: **impressions** (sensations, passions, emotions, as they first strike us) and **ideas** (their fainter copies in thought). The first principle of the science is the **copy principle**: every simple idea derives from a corresponding simple impression. Hume treats this as an empirical generalization — shrugging off his own counterexample, the missing shade of blue, as too singular to matter — but wields it as an analytical weapon. Confronted with a philosophical term ("substance," "power," "self"), the Humean question is: *from what impression is this idea derived?* If none can be produced, the term is either meaningless or means something humbler than its users suppose — the ancestor of every later empiricist criterion of significance, and the instrument behind his two most famous results. Paired with it is **Hume's fork**: all objects of inquiry are either *relations of ideas* (demonstrable, necessary, uninformative about existence) or *matters of fact* (informative, never demonstrable, since the contrary of any matter of fact is conceivable). Conceivability entails possibility; hence no fact about the world is necessary, and whatever the mind takes to be necessary in nature will need a psychological rather than a metaphysical explanation.

### Causation and induction

The centerpiece. Causation is the only relation that carries the mind beyond present perception and memory, so the science of man must ask what the inference rests on. The **negative argument** has two prongs. First, examine any single case of causation as closely as you like — one billiard ball striking another — and you find contiguity, succession, and nothing more: no impression of **necessary connexion**, no perceived power by which the cause *produces* the effect; a priori, anything might follow anything. Second, inference from observed to unobserved cases presupposes that nature is uniform — that the future resembles the past — and this can be justified neither demonstratively (a change in nature's course is perfectly conceivable) nor probably (any argument from experience for uniformity already assumes it, and so moves in a circle). This is the **problem of induction**, against which every later response — Kantian, pragmatist, Popperian, Bayesian, reliabilist — is still measured. The **positive account** then explains what reason cannot justify: after repeated conjunctions of like objects, **custom** carries the mind from the one to the idea of the other, and belief is nothing but the peculiar force and vivacity of an idea so produced. The felt transition is itself the missing impression: the idea of necessity is copied not from anything in the objects but from a determination of the mind, which the mind then "spreads" upon the world. Hume closes with **two definitions of cause** — one in terms of regular succession among similar objects, one in terms of the mind's being carried from the appearance of one to the thought of the other — and notoriously does not say which is fundamental. The interpretive war is three-cornered: the classical reading makes him a **regularity theorist** — causation in the objects just is constant conjunction; the **projectivist** reading (Blackburn, following Kemp Smith) takes the spreading metaphor seriously — causal talk expresses our inferential habits rather than describing regularities or powers; the **"New Hume"** of skeptical realism (Wright, Strawson) argues from his repeated references to nature's "secret springs and principles" that he believed in real powers while denying we can form any adequate idea of them. Each side has proof-texts, and the dispute doubles as a proxy for the live contest between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of laws and powers.

### Personal identity: the bundle and the Appendix

Apply the copy principle to the self and the result is Hume's most startling negative finding: there is no impression of a simple, continuing self — whenever he enters most intimately into what he calls himself, he stumbles only on particular perceptions, and never catches *himself* without a perception. The mind is a **bundle** of perceptions in perpetual flux, "a kind of theatre" without a stage — no place where the scenes are played, only the scenes. Felt identity over time is explained, not vindicated: resemblance and causation among perceptions make the imagination slide smoothly along them, and we mistake the smooth transition for the survey of an unchanging thing — the same fiction by which we ascribe identity to a ship rebuilt plank by plank. But in the **Appendix** to the *Treatise*, Hume famously recanted — not the negative argument, which he reaffirmed, but the explanation: two principles he could neither renounce nor reconcile — all perceptions are distinct existences; the mind never perceives real connexion among distinct existences — left him unable to say what *unites* the perceptions in one bundle rather than another. What exactly the labyrinth is remains disputed, but the honesty is the point: the bundle theory enters philosophy already flagged, by its author, as incomplete. Its descendants run through Parfit's reductionism about persons; its closest analogue — noted by Hume's readers, unknowable to Hume — is the Buddhist doctrine of no-self.

### Reason, passion, and morals from sentiment

Book 2 of the *Treatise* subordinates reason in the practical sphere as Book 1 had in the theoretical: reason is, and ought only to be, the **slave of the passions**. The argument is austere: reason judges of truth and falsehood, but a passion is an "original existence" with no representative quality, so it can be neither true nor false; reason alone can never produce or prevent an action, only inform the passions about means and facts. "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger" — the thesis that ends are set by sentiment, drawn shockingly to its conclusion. Morals follow: since morality moves us and reason alone cannot, moral distinctions are felt, not inferred — virtue is whatever mental quality produces the sentiment of **approbation** from a general, spectatorial point of view; and what elicits approbation, the mature theory adds, is whatever is *useful or agreeable* to the agent or to others. The celebrated **is/ought paragraph** observes that moralists slide from "is" premises to "ought" conclusions without explaining the new relation. What it establishes should be stated carefully: Hume demands an explanation none had given — a challenge, not the tidy twentieth-century "law" that no such inference could ever be valid; its work in context is to embarrass rationalist moral systems, and its conscription as an autonomy-of-ethics axiom came later. Finally, **natural versus artificial virtues**: benevolence and gratitude draw approval immediately, but justice — property, promise, allegiance — is approved only via **convention**, a system of mutually referential expectations arising gradually because each party benefits from general compliance, like two rowers falling into stroke without a promise: the first great naturalistic account of norms as emergent solutions to coordination and scarcity, ancestor of contractarianism without the contract and of convention theory from Lewis onward.

### Religion: miracles and the Dialogues

Hume's philosophy of religion is the most consequential body of writing in the field. **"Of Miracles"** supplies its evidential principle: a wise man proportions belief to evidence; a miracle is a violation of a law of nature, against which stands, by definition, the firmest experience possible; so no testimony establishes a miracle unless its falsehood would be *more* miraculous than the event it reports — and the essay's empirical second part argues that no actual religious testimony comes close. Whether the argument proves too much — freezing science's own encounters with the anomalous, a priori dogmatism dressed as empiricism (Earman's charge) — is a live Bayesian debate; the essay set its terms. The posthumous **Dialogues** subject natural theology's best case, the design argument, to the most patient destruction in the literature. Philo's cumulative case: the analogy between world and artifact is remote; like effects prove like causes, so an imperfect, finite world licenses at most an imperfect, finite designer — or an infant deity's first rude essay; order might as well be internal to matter as imposed on it, since a designing mind merely relocates the order to be explained; and the world's suffering, whatever its logical compatibility with theism, blocks any *inference* from nature to a benevolent God. The famous puzzle is Philo's apparent capitulation in Part XII — the cause of order "probably bears some remote analogy to human intelligence" — read variously as irony, prudence, or a sincere residue of attenuated deism emptied of religious content. With the *Natural History*'s causal story of religion's origin in fear, the pincer closed: religion explained as a natural phenomenon on one flank, deprived of rational credentials on the other.

## Influence and reception

The first reception was hostile or absent. The *Treatise* was ignored; the *Enquiries* earned infamy rather than engagement; and the ablest contemporary pushback came from **Thomas Reid**, whose common-sense philosophy argued that Hume had honestly bankrupted the theory of ideas shared since Descartes — if impressions and ideas are all the mind contains, Hume's conclusions follow; so much the worse for the premise. Through a German digest of Beattie's cruder polemic, Hume reached **Kant**, who credited the analysis of causation with interrupting his dogmatic slumber; the *Critique of Pure Reason* is in large part an answer to Hume — causality rescued as an a priori condition of experience, at the price of confining knowledge to appearances. The nineteenth century read him mainly as historian and infidel; the twentieth made him a patron saint twice over. The **logical positivists** canonized the fork and the flames passage — emotivism, verificationism, and the regularity view of law were all advertised as Humean. Then **Kemp Smith** (1941) overturned the purely skeptical reading, establishing the naturalist Hume for whom belief is a matter of feeling rather than reason, and scholarship has orbited the skeptic–naturalist tension since. Contemporary metaphysics conducts a standing referendum on him: **Lewis's Humean supervenience** — everything supervening on the spatiotemporal mosaic of local matters of fact — is Hume's world-picture given modern form, defended in best-system accounts of laws and chance, attacked by friends of powers and primitive necessity. In metaethics, **Blackburn's quasi-realism** builds an entire program on Humean projectivism — earning moral talk its realist-sounding surface while grounding it in sentiment — and "Humean" names the dominant belief–desire theory of motivation. Bayesian epistemology re-fights "Of Miracles"; cognitive science claims the associationist psychology as an ancestor; and the sentimentalist revival, led by Annette Baier, reclaimed the moral philosophy from its long eclipse by Kant.

## Reading path

Begin with *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* — short, polished, containing the essential arguments on induction, custom, necessary connexion, and miracles — then the second *Enquiry* for the morals. Then the *Treatise* at full strength: Book 1 Parts 3–4 (causation; skepticism and personal identity) with the Appendix, and Book 3 Part 1 (is/ought, morals from sentiment); the *Abstract* is Hume's own map. The *Dialogues* should be read whole. Secondary: Stroud's *Hume* as the best single commentary; Kemp Smith for the naturalist turn; Garrett's *Cognition and Commitment in Hume's Philosophy* and Millican's collections for the interpretive state of the art; Baier's *A Progress of Sentiments* for the *Treatise* read whole; Mossner's biography for the life.

## Assessment

Editorially: Hume is the modern philosopher with the best ratio of problems opened to machinery required. With one instrument — trace the idea to its impression — and one honest refusal — to claim more for reason than observation of the mind supports — he generated problems that remain open under their original names. The temptation he resists better than his heirs is system: where the positivists turned his fork into a doctrine and promptly cut themselves on it, Hume held his skepticism and his naturalism in a tension he declined to resolve, dining and playing backgammon while the arguments stood. That equanimity is itself a philosophical position — arguably the hardest in his corpus to refute.
