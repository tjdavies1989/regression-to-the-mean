# Nāgārjuna

*c. 2nd–3rd century CE · Indian Buddhist philosopher; founder of the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school of Mahāyāna Buddhism*

## Why they matter

Nāgārjuna is the most consequential Buddhist philosopher after the Buddha, and the reason is a single reversal of direction. The Abhidharma schools had taken the teaching that everything arises in dependence on conditions and analyzed the world down to its ultimate constituents — the dharmas, momentary event-particulars that alone exist in their own right. Nāgārjuna argued that the analysis had stopped one step short: whatever arises dependently is empty (śūnya) of intrinsic nature (svabhāva), and that includes the dharmas. Emptiness, on his account, is not a denial of dependent origination but its meaning — the claim that nothing exists from its own side, and that this is exactly what allows things to arise, change, and cease. Everything that followed in Mahāyāna philosophy is an elaboration of this claim, a defense of it against the charge of nihilism, or an attempt to state it without falling into the trap it sets for any statement. His methods — the four-cornered analysis, refutation by unwanted consequence, the declaration that he holds no thesis — made him the tradition's model of how philosophy can dismantle metaphysics without proposing a rival one, and the problem of what such philosophy positively says has kept his readers arguing for eighteen centuries.

## Life

Almost nothing is known. The Buddhist traditions agree that Nāgārjuna was a South Indian Brahmin who converted, flourished under a Sātavāhana king (the *Ratnāvalī* and the *Suhṛllekha* are letters of counsel addressed to a ruler), and was connected with the Andhra coast, where a later monastic site bears his name. Modern scholarship places him roughly between 150 and 250 CE, on the strength of the Abhidharma positions he attacks and the earliest Chinese notices of his work. Everything more specific is hagiography: Kumārajīva's fifth-century biography and the Tibetan histories have him recovering the Perfection of Wisdom sūtras from the undersea realm of the nāgas (hence the name), practicing alchemy, living for centuries, and dying by decapitation at a prince's request — legends that secure his rank as the "second Buddha" of the Mahāyāna and tell us nothing checkable. The problem is compounded by a plurality of Nāgārjunas: a tantric author, an alchemist, and a medical writer share the name in later centuries, and works of all three were absorbed into the single figure. The philosopher of this article is the author of the analytic corpus below, whoever he was.

## Works

- ***Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*** (*Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way*, MMK) — twenty-seven chapters, some 450 verses, each examining a category (conditions, motion, the sense-faculties, the aggregates, agent and action, self, time, the Tathāgata, the four noble truths, nirvāṇa) and showing it cannot be what it would have to be if it had intrinsic nature. The foundational text of Madhyamaka and among the most commented-on works in Asian philosophy.
- ***Vigrahavyāvartanī*** (*Dispeller of Disputes*) — seventy verses with autocommentary, answering a Nyāya-flavored objector who charges that the doctrine of emptiness is self-refuting and that a critic of the means of knowledge cannot establish anything. The key text on method.
- ***Śūnyatāsaptati***, ***Yuktiṣaṣṭikā***, ***Vaidalyaprakaraṇa*** — shorter analytic treatises: seventy verses on emptiness, sixty on reasoning, and a demolition of Nyāya's sixteen categories of debate. With the MMK and the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* these form the "reasoning corpus" the Tibetan tradition treats as securely authentic.
- ***Ratnāvalī*** (*Precious Garland*) and ***Suhṛllekha*** (*Letter to a Friend*) — advice to a king on ethics, governance, and the bodhisattva path; emptiness as lived rather than argued.
- Contested attributions: the *Akutobhayā*, an early MMK commentary sometimes taken as Nāgārjuna's own; the vast *Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa*, preserved only in Chinese; hymns (the *Catuḥstava*) and tantric works. The philosophical portrait relies on the analytic corpus; the MMK and the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* are enough to carry it.

## Thought

### Emptiness as the meaning of dependent origination

Nāgārjuna's target is precise. Abhidharma metaphysics grants that persons and chariots are conceptual constructions but holds that the dharmas beneath them exist ultimately, each bearing its own **svabhāva** — an intrinsic nature that is what it is independently of anything else, unfabricated, not borrowed from causes or from the mind that conceives it. Nāgārjuna's argument is that nothing satisfying that description could participate in dependent origination, and that dependent origination is the one thing a Buddhist cannot give up. The MMK's first chapter opens the campaign: nothing arises from itself (it would already exist), from another (with no intrinsic connection, anything could arise from anything), from both, or from neither. The Abhidharma's four conditions cannot be conditions in their own right, since a condition is a condition only relative to what it conditions — and that thing does not yet exist. The second chapter dismantles motion: there is no moving on the path already traversed, none on the path not yet traversed, and the "being-traversed" that should be left over cannot be located without a mover distinct from the moving, who then moves twice or not at all. Chapter after chapter repeats the pattern for agent and action, fire and fuel, seer and seen, the self and its aggregates: each pair turns out to be defined by mutual dependence, so that neither member can have the self-standing nature that would make it ultimately real.

The positive doctrine is stated in the fifteenth and twenty-fourth chapters. Intrinsic nature would be unproduced and unchanging, so if anything had it nothing could arise or cease; conversely, whatever arises dependently must lack it. Emptiness is therefore not a further fact discovered behind dependent origination but the same fact stated in the mode of negation: to be empty is to be dependently originated, and to be dependently originated is to be empty. This is the "middle way" of the school's name — between eternalism, which posits enduring intrinsic natures, and annihilationism, which holds that things do not exist at all. Both extremes, Nāgārjuna argues, share the presupposition that to exist is to exist intrinsically; one affirms it, the other despairs of it. In the encyclopedia's own terms the position is a thoroughgoing anti-foundationalism: there is no fundamental level, no ungrounded ground, and the dependence relations run without terminus in every direction — a structure the grounding literature's infinitist and coherentist rivals to well-foundedness have only recently begun to take seriously.

### The two truths

The doctrine that makes emptiness livable is the distinction between two truths. **Conventional truth** (saṃvṛti-satya — the term also connotes concealment) is the domain of ordinary assertion: chariots carry people, fire burns, actions have consequences. **Ultimate truth** (paramārtha-satya) is what is found when the conventional is analyzed for intrinsic nature: nothing. Nāgārjuna insists on both: those who do not grasp the distinction do not grasp the Buddha's teaching, the ultimate cannot be taught without recourse to the conventional, and without reaching the ultimate nirvāṇa is not attained. What the distinction is *not* is a two-tier ontology. The Abhidharma had also used the two truths, with the dharmas as the ultimately real substrate beneath conventional fictions; Nāgārjuna keeps the vocabulary and removes the substrate. The ultimate truth about anything is that it lacks an ultimate nature, which is to say that there is nothing over and above the conventional for the conventional to conceal. The twenty-fifth chapter draws the famous consequence: there is not the slightest difference between saṃsāra and nirvāṇa, since neither is a place or a state with a nature of its own. Interpretation divides here between readings on which the ultimate is an ineffable reality glimpsed beyond conceptuality and readings on which the ultimate truth is a truth *about* the conventional — that it is all there is — with no further object. The latter, deflationary reading dominates contemporary philosophical exegesis; the former has deep roots among the tradition's own commentators.

### The catuṣkoṭi and refutation by consequence

Two instruments run through the corpus, and they must be understood as Indian dialectical practice before any Western logic is laid over them. The **catuṣkoṭi**, or four-cornered analysis, is a schema for exhausting the positions available on a question: a thing is F, is not F, is both, is neither. It predates Nāgārjuna; the Buddha is said to have declined all four answers to questions such as whether an enlightened one exists after death. Nāgārjuna's use is overwhelmingly negative: on nirvāṇa, on the Tathāgata, on the self, all four corners are rejected. The method is intelligible on one premise, which the text supplies: each corner is a claim made *with* the presupposition of intrinsic nature. To deny that a thing intrinsically exists and to deny that it intrinsically does not exist is no contradiction, because the denials remove a shared presupposition rather than affirming incompatible predicates. The commentarial tradition later fixed this as the distinction between two kinds of negation — the non-implicative (prasajya) negation that merely rejects, and the implicative (paryudāsa) negation that rejects one thing by asserting its complement. Nāgārjuna's negations are of the first kind: rejecting all four corners commits him to no fifth.

The second instrument is **prasaṅga**, refutation by drawing out an unwanted consequence. Nāgārjuna does not, in the main, argue *for* emptiness from premises of his own; he takes the opponent's commitment to intrinsic natures and shows that it entails what the opponent must deny — that nothing changes, that causation is impossible, that the Buddhist path cannot be walked. The argument leaves its author with nothing to defend. This is why the two instruments belong together: the catuṣkoṭi maps the space of svabhāva-committed positions, and prasaṅga clears every cell of it. Whether what remains is a position, a method, a therapy, or a silence is the question the tradition has never settled — and the reason the later school split over whether a Mādhyamika may use ordinary inference at all.

### The nihilism charge and the emptiness of emptiness

The twenty-fourth chapter stages the objection that has followed the school through its history. If everything is empty, the objector says, nothing arises and nothing ceases; the four noble truths, the path, the community, and the Buddha himself are abolished; emptiness is the ruin of Buddhism. Nāgārjuna replies that the objector has misunderstood emptiness, its purpose, and its meaning, and turns the charge around. It is the theorist of intrinsic natures who cannot accommodate the four truths: if suffering had intrinsic nature it could not have arisen and could not be ended; if the path existed from its own side it could not be cultivated; if the Buddha had a nature he could not have become one. Only empty things can be produced, transformed, and extinguished. Emptiness is not the negation of the conventional world but the condition of its working.

The deeper move is that emptiness is itself empty. Emptiness is not a property things possess intrinsically, nor a hidden essence — the ultimate nature of things — that survives the analysis; it is, in Nāgārjuna's phrase, a dependent designation, a way of describing dependent origination that is as conventional as anything it describes. The thirteenth chapter warns that those who make emptiness into a view are incurable: emptiness was taught as the relinquishment of all views, and to hold it as one more is to reinstate the reification it was meant to cure. This is what separates Madhyamaka from the nihilism it is accused of. The nihilist holds a thesis — that nothing exists — and the thesis is one more claim about how things ultimately are. Nāgārjuna's claim is that there is no way things ultimately are, and that this claim, too, is empty. The cost is real and the tradition felt it: a doctrine that refuses to be a doctrine invites the reply that it says nothing, and the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* exists because the reply was made.

### The Vigrahavyāvartanī: can the critic of the pramāṇas have a thesis?

The opponent of the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* — a Naiyāyika in all but name — presses two charges. First, self-refutation: if all things are empty, the statement "all things are empty" is empty, and an empty statement, lacking any nature, can negate nothing; if the statement is not empty, the thesis is false by its own counterexample. Second, the means of knowledge: if Nāgārjuna denies that the pramāṇas — perception, inference, testimony, comparison — establish their objects, by what means does he establish his denial? Nāgārjuna's answers are the most examined verses in the corpus. To the first he replies that emptiness does not deprive things of their function: an empty cart still carries, and an empty statement still communicates, as a conjured person may prevent another conjured person's deed. Then, famously, he declares that he has no thesis (pratijñā): if he had one, the alleged fault would attach to it; since he has none, there is no fault. To the second he turns the regress. If the pramāṇas establish their objects, what establishes the pramāṇas? If nothing, the demand for establishment has been abandoned. If other pramāṇas, there is a regress. If they establish themselves, the opponent's analogy — a lamp illuminating both itself and other things — fails, since a lamp does not illuminate itself in any sense in which it dispels darkness. And if the objects establish the pramāṇas, the relation is circular, each relying on the other and neither established.

What "I have no thesis" means is the interpretive crux of the whole corpus. On the strongest reading, Nāgārjuna asserts nothing at all — his sentences are instruments for removing the opponent's convictions, without truth-value of their own. On the weaker and now more common reading, he has no thesis of the kind the opponent requires: no claim backed by intrinsic natures and established by foundational means of knowledge. He can assert, conventionally, that things are empty, as one asserts anything, and the point of the text is that this is the only kind of assertion anyone ever makes. Read this way the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* is an early and radical argument that the epistemic regress has no foundationalist stopping point — the pramāṇas and their objects are established together, if at all, and mutual establishment is what establishment is.

## Influence and reception

Nāgārjuna's disciple Āryadeva extended the method in the *Catuḥśataka*, and by the sixth century the commentarial tradition on the MMK had produced the school's defining quarrel. **Buddhapālita** read Nāgārjuna as a pure practitioner of prasaṅga: every argument exposes a consequence the opponent must reject, and the Mādhyamika asserts nothing on his own account. **Bhāviveka** objected that this is not argument at all: a refutation must be cast as a formal inference with thesis, reason, and example of the kind Dignāga had just codified, and the Mādhyamika owes his opponent independent (svatantra) inferences establishing emptiness on grounds both parties accept. **Candrakīrti**, in the *Prasannapadā* and the *Madhyamakāvatāra*, defended Buddhapālita and made the reply a principle: there are no grounds both parties accept, since the opponent's presuppose intrinsic natures and the Mādhyamika's do not, so consequences are all one can offer. The labels **Prāsaṅgika** and **Svātantrika** are Tibetan coinages for this division, but the division is real, and it concerns how far the emptiness of emptiness reaches into the logic of one's own speech. Alongside it, the Yogācāra-Madhyamaka synthesis of Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla read the two Mahāyāna schools as stages of a single ascent, and it was this synthesis that first reached Tibet.

The push-back from outside was immediate and permanent. The *Nyāyasūtra* itself answers arguments recognizably Nāgārjuna's about the pramāṇa regress, and Vātsyāyana's commentary rebuilds the lamp analogy; Nyāya and Madhyamaka thereafter argued the foundations of knowledge across the āstika–nāstika line for a millennium. Śaṅkara declined to argue, dismissing the Mādhyamika as a nihilist unworthy of refutation — while Gauḍapāda's early Advaita, as later scholars noticed, had borrowed the catuṣkoṭi and the unbornness of the world nearly wholesale.

In Tibet Candrakīrti's reading became orthodoxy, but an orthodoxy at war with itself. **Tsongkhapa**'s Geluk Prāsaṅgika made Nāgārjuna's emptiness compatible with a robust conventional world: the object of refutation is precisely and only intrinsic nature, the negation is non-implicative, and conventional existence — causation, ethics, the path — stands untouched, even secured, by the analysis. The Sakya master Gorampa answered that this domesticates Nāgārjuna: the ultimate is freedom from all four corners, beyond conceptual elaboration altogether, and Tsongkhapa's careful conventionalism reinstates the reification it claims to avoid. That quarrel, still conducted in monastic debate, is the sharpest available commentary on the corpus. In China Kumārajīva's translations of the MMK with an early commentary, of Āryadeva, and of the *Twelve Gate Treatise* founded the **Sanlun** ("Three Treatise") school, whose master Jizang read the two truths as a ladder of successively emptied dichotomies; it passed to Japan as **Sanron**. The Tiantai founder Zhiyi drew from a single MMK verse the three truths — empty, provisional, and middle — that organize his system, and Chan's paradoxes are unimaginable without the Madhyamaka background they decline to spell out.

Modern reception has been a history of mirrors. Stcherbatsky made Nāgārjuna a Kantian for whom the ultimate is noumenal; Murti made him an absolutist with an ineffable ground; Kalupahana made him an empiricist restoring the early canon; readers of the 1970s and 1980s found in him Wittgenstein's therapy or Derrida's deconstruction. The current philosophical reading, associated with **Jay Garfield** and Mark Siderits, is deflationary and anti-foundationalist: emptiness is the rejection of any ultimate nature, including a nature of ultimate reality; the two truths are two ways of taking one world; and the catuṣkoṭi respects classical logic once the presupposition of intrinsic nature is made explicit — a reading Jan Westerhoff's reconstruction develops in detail. Against it stands **Graham Priest**'s dialetheist reading, developed with Garfield, on which Nāgārjuna's ultimate truth — that there is no ultimate truth — is a genuine contradiction he embraces, and the rejection of all four corners requires a fifth, ineffable value best modeled in a paraconsistent many-valued logic. This reading is contested on philological and philosophical grounds alike: Tom Tillemans argues that Mādhyamikas reason with an ordinary commitment to consistency and that the apparent contradictions dissolve under the two-negation analysis, and Siderits holds that presupposition failure leaves nothing for a dialetheist to be right about. The register's warning applies: the comparison with paraconsistent logic illuminates the structure of the limit-contradictions, if they are such, and flattens Nāgārjuna into a precursor if taken as more than that. A parallel dispute — whether Nāgārjuna argues at all, or performs a dismantling that argument-reconstruction betrays — has divided C. W. Huntington from Garfield, and shows that the Buddhapālita–Bhāviveka quarrel is still being fought in English.

## Reading path

Begin with the MMK in the Siderits and Katsura translation (2013), which works from the Sanskrit and supplies chapter-by-chapter commentary keyed to the Indian commentators; Garfield's *The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way* (1995), from the Tibetan with a philosophically engaged commentary, is the alternative and the more widely read. Read chapters 1, 2, 15, 18, 24, and 25 first. Then the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* in Westerhoff's translation and commentary (2010): short, and the place where the method explains itself. The *Ratnāvalī* shows the ethics. For the commentators, Candrakīrti's *Prasannapadā* is partly available in Sprung's translation; Tsongkhapa's *Ocean of Reasoning*, translated by Garfield and Ngawang Samten, is the great Tibetan commentary. Secondary: Westerhoff's *Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka: A Philosophical Introduction* (2009) is the standard analytic study; Siderits's *Buddhism as Philosophy* supplies the context in a chapter; Ruegg's *The Literature of the Madhyamaka School of Philosophy in India* maps the tradition; the Cowherds' *Moonshadows* (2011) is the state of the debate on conventional truth. For the dialetheist reading and its critics, Garfield and Priest's "Nāgārjuna and the Limits of Thought" and Tillemans's "How Do Mādhyamikas Think?" should be read together.

## Assessment

Editorially: Nāgārjuna is the purest case in this encyclopedia of a philosopher whose position is a method, and the question "what does he actually hold?" resists answer not through obscurity but by design — the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* is built to make the question misfire. The deflationary reading is the best available, because it lets the corpus be argument rather than gesture; but it pays for that by attributing to Nāgārjuna a doctrine of presupposition he never states, and the dialetheist alternative is right that something at the limit of the analysis does not sit comfortably in classical form. The safest verdict is the tradition's own: the quarrel between Tsongkhapa and Gorampa is not a failure of interpretation but the doctrine working as intended.
