# Plato

*c. 427–347 BCE · Athenian philosopher; student of Socrates, founder of the Academy, and builder of philosophy's first system*

## Why they matter

Plato is the first philosopher from whom we have a complete body of work, and the first to treat the discipline's problems as one problem: what is real, what can be known, and how should we live turn out, in his hands, to be a single question with a single hinge — whether there are stable, intelligible objects behind the flux of appearance. His answer, the theory of Forms, is Western philosophy's founding metaphysical posit, and nearly every later position defines itself for or against it: realism about universals, mathematical platonism, rationalism about knowledge, the distrust of art, the dream of rule by expertise. He also invented the institutional and literary machinery: the Academy is the ancestor of every university, and the dialogue form — philosophy as staged argument rather than delivered doctrine — built self-criticism into the subject at its origin. Whitehead's famous remark that European philosophy is "a series of footnotes to Plato" is often quoted as a ranking; it was offered as a description of scope — Plato scattered the seeds of nearly every subsequent debate — and in that form it is simply accurate.

## Life

Plato was born around 427 BCE into an aristocratic Athenian family; relatives of his mother, Critias and Charmides, became leading members of the Thirty Tyrants, the junta that ruled Athens after its defeat by Sparta. The decisive events of his youth were negative: the collapse of Athenian political life in the Peloponnesian War, the oligarchs' brutality, and above all the restored democracy's execution of Socrates in 399 — the moment, on his own later telling, that convinced him existing cities were beyond piecemeal cure. He traveled afterward to Megara and to southern Italy and Sicily, where contact with Pythagoreans, especially Archytas of Tarentum, left permanent marks: the mathematics, the immortal soul, philosophy as a shared, disciplined life. Around 387 he founded the Academy outside Athens, a community of research whose members included Eudoxus, Theaetetus, and Aristotle, who studied there for twenty years. His one sustained attempt at practical politics — two returns to Syracuse, in 367 and 361, to make a philosopher of the young tyrant Dionysius II — failed humiliatingly; the *Seventh Letter* (if genuine) records his own post-mortem. He died in 347, writing to the end; the *Laws* was left unrevised.

## Works

Everything Plato wrote for publication survives — a fortune granted to no other ancient philosopher — in roughly twenty-six dialogues plus letters of disputed authenticity. Plato appears in none of them as a speaker. Scholarship groups them, on stylistic and doctrinal evidence, into three loose phases:

- **Early ("Socratic") dialogues** — *Apology*, *Crito*, *Euthyphro*, *Laches*, *Charmides*, *Protagoras*, *Gorgias*: short, combative, dominated by Socrates' method of cross-examination (*elenchus*), typically ending in avowed perplexity about a virtue's definition.
- **Middle dialogues** — *Meno*, *Phaedo*, *Symposium*, *Republic*, *Phaedrus*: the constructive system — Forms, recollection, the immortal tripartite soul, the ideal city — with Socrates now the mouthpiece of positive doctrine.
- **Late dialogues** — *Parmenides*, *Theaetetus*, *Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Philebus*, *Timaeus*, *Laws*: self-criticism, new method, and technical metaphysics; Socrates recedes, replaced by an Eleatic Stranger, the aged Parmenides, or the Athenian of the *Laws*.

The load-bearing texts for the system are the *Meno*, *Phaedo*, *Symposium*, and *Republic*; for the self-criticism and late turn, the *Parmenides*, *Sophist*, and *Timaeus*.

## Thought

### The Forms: one over many

The theory of Forms answers a clutch of problems with a single posit. Many distinct things are beautiful, or equal, or just; what is *the* beautiful, in which they all share? Sensible instances are compresent with their opposites — the same act just in one context, unjust in another; equal sticks, measured differently, unequal — so no sensible thing can be what justice or equality *is*. And knowledge, as opposed to shifting opinion, needs objects that hold still. Plato's answer: over every such many stands one **Form** — Beauty itself, Equality itself — an eternal, changeless, non-sensible object that *is* what its instances merely *have*. Particulars are what they are by **participation** in Forms; Forms are at once the causes of things' characters, the objects of genuine knowledge, and the standards against which instances fall short. The *Republic*'s **divided line** arranges reality and cognition in matching tiers: images and the guesswork aimed at them; sensible things and mere belief (*pistis*); mathematical objects and the hypothesis-driven thinking (*dianoia*) of the geometers; at the top the Forms, grasped by intellection (*noēsis*) through dialectic, the whole ordered under the Form of the Good — which, in the sun analogy, does for intelligibility what the sun does for sight. The scheme is the ancestor of every two-worlds metaphysics and every realism about universals, and it fixed permanently the thought that mathematics is the natural bridge from sense to intellect.

### The Parmenides: self-criticism as method

The *Parmenides* stages the most powerful objections ever raised against the Forms — raised by Plato, against himself, a generation before Aristotle repeated them. The young Socrates presents the theory; the aged Parmenides dismantles it. Which things have Forms — mud and hair too? Does a participant get the whole Form (dividing it from itself) or a part (so a small thing partakes of a fragment of Largeness)? Sharpest is the argument later called the **Third Man**: if the many large things are large in virtue of one Form, Largeness, and Largeness is itself large — as Plato's talk of Forms as perfect exemplars suggests — then the large things *plus* Largeness form a new many, requiring a further Form over them, and so on without end. The regress runs on two tacit premises, self-predication (the Form of F is itself F) and non-identity (nothing is F in virtue of itself), and the modern literature is largely a taxonomy of which to abandon. What matters interpretively is that Plato prints the objections without printed answers, has Parmenides insist that philosophy is impossible *without* Forms all the same, and prescribes a grueling dialectical training. This is not embarrassment; it is the founding demonstration that a theory is held responsibly only together with the strongest case against it — and the late dialogues' revised machinery is best read as his answer.

### Knowledge as recollection

The *Meno* poses a paradox that still frames the theory of inquiry: you cannot seek what you know (you have it already) nor what you do not know (you would not recognize it if found) — so inquiry is either idle or impossible. Plato's answer is **recollection** (*anamnēsis*): learning is the recovery of knowledge the soul possessed before birth. In the famous demonstration, Socrates elicits from an uneducated enslaved boy, by questions alone, the truth that the square on a diagonal doubles the original square — evidence, Plato argues, that the truth was drawn out of him, not put in. The *Phaedo* tightens the argument: we judge sensible equals to be deficient approximations of Equality itself, and one cannot measure a shortfall against a standard one has never met; so acquaintance with the Forms must predate experience. Stripped of the reincarnation mythology, this is the first theory of a priori knowledge — some knowledge, paradigmatically mathematical, is native to the mind, triggered rather than taught — and its descendants run through Augustine's illumination, Descartes' and Leibniz's innate ideas, and contemporary rationalism. The paradox itself survives its Platonic solution as a standing constraint on any account of inquiry: partial knowledge, knowing-which-question, must be possible.

### The Republic: soul, city, and the cave

The *Republic* asks why one should be just when injustice pays, and answers with a psychology and a politics built in parallel. The **soul has three parts** — reason, which calculates and seeks truth; spirit (*thumos*), the seat of anger, honor, and shame; and appetite — a division argued from inner conflict: the thirsty man who refuses to drink must contain a part that pulls and a part that restrains. **Justice** in the soul is each part doing its own work under reason's rule, a kind of psychic health; and since a healthy soul is worth having whatever its reputation, justice pays intrinsically — Plato's answer to Glaucon's challenge, put with the tale of Gyges' ring, that anyone who could sin invisibly would. The **city-soul analogy** scales the structure up: the just city's three classes — rulers, auxiliaries, producers — each do their own work, and the notorious conclusion is that cities will not flourish until philosophers rule, since only knowers of the Form of the Good know what institutions are *for*. The **cave** condenses the whole: prisoners bound before shadows mistake them for reality; release, the painful ascent to daylight, and the reluctant return to govern those still bound allegorize education, the philosopher's condition, and the cost of enlightenment among the unenlightened. The *Republic* thereby founded at a stroke moral psychology, ideal political theory, the critique of democracy as rule by appetite, and — in expelling the imitative poets — the philosophy of art as a problem.

### Eros and ascent: the Symposium

The *Symposium* takes a phenomenon the *Republic* mostly polices — desire — and makes it the engine of philosophy. In Diotima's speech, reported by Socrates, **eros** is neither god nor mortal but an intermediate spirit, the child of Resource and Poverty: love is precisely the condition of lacking and reaching. What lovers ultimately want is to "give birth in beauty" — to generate, whether children, deeds, poems, or ideas, and thereby touch immortality. Hence the **ladder of ascent**: from love of one beautiful body, to bodily beauty as such, to the beauty of souls, of practices and laws, of knowledge, until the lover suddenly beholds Beauty itself — the Form, unmixed and unchanging, of which every lower beauty was a partial reflection. Two things distinguish the doctrine from the mysticism it superficially resembles. First, the ascent does not renounce the lower rungs so much as reinterpret them: particular beauties are genuine, if partial, disclosures of the Form, and desire for them is the same desire that dialectic completes. Second, the ladder makes the philosopher's motivation continuous with everyone's: philosophy is not a specialist taste but ordinary longing, correctly aimed. Alcibiades' drunken irruption — his portrait of the Socrates he loved and could not have — is Plato's dramatized concession of what the theory costs in human particularity, a tension the literature still works over.

### The late turn: division, and the Sophist on non-being

The late dialogues replace the elenchus with a new instrument: **collection and division** — gathering a scattered kind into view, then dividing it along its natural joints (an image the *Phaedrus* makes canonical) until the target is defined by its position in a network of kinds. The method's masterpiece is the *Sophist*, which, hunting a definition of the sophist as a dealer in falsehoods, collides with Parmenides' ancient interdiction: to speak falsely is to say *what is not*, and what is not, Parmenides held, cannot be said or thought at all. Plato's Eleatic Stranger commits what he calls parricide against "father Parmenides" by distinguishing senses of "is not": non-being is not the impossible contrary of being but **difference** — "Theaetetus flies" is false not because it invokes sheer nothing but because flying is *different from* everything Theaetetus is actually doing. The solution runs through the doctrine of the "greatest kinds" — being, sameness, difference, motion, rest — which blend with one another in determinate ways: Forms are no longer isolated units for contemplation but nodes in a structure, and dialectic becomes the science of their combinations. This is the first workable account of falsehood and negation, the beginning of the analysis of predication, and the moment Platonism turned from vision to logic; Frege's and Russell's problems about negative existentials are its direct descendants.

### The Timaeus: a likely story of the cosmos

The *Timaeus* — for a millennium the only Plato the Latin West could read — offers a cosmology with a built-in epistemological disclaimer: since the sensible world is a changing image of an eternal model, an account of it can be at best a "likely story" (*eikōs mythos*), not fixed knowledge. Within that limit, the account is systematic. A divine craftsman, the **Demiurge**, being good and ungrudging, orders a pre-existing chaos into the best possible world by looking to the eternal Forms as blueprint — creation as rational artistry, not from nothing. Between model and copy Plato inserts a third thing, the **receptacle** (*chōra*): the characterless medium in which qualities come to be, ancestor of every later notion of space and matter. The world receives a soul and counts as a living being; time enters as "a moving image of eternity," generated with the heavens; and the four elements are reduced to geometry — corpuscles shaped as regular solids, built from two kinds of elementary triangle, so that physics bottoms out in mathematics. Read literally or (as the early Academy already debated) as a timeless dependence-relation dressed in narrative, the *Timaeus* fixed the agenda for natural theology and design arguments, and its conviction that the book of nature is written in mathematics resurfaces, acknowledged, in Kepler and Galileo.

### The dialogue form and the Socratic problem

Plato never speaks in the dialogues. Every doctrine above is voiced by a character — usually "Socrates," a figure based on a real man who wrote nothing and whose other portraitists (Xenophon, Aristophanes) drew him differently. The **Socratic problem** — where the historical Socrates ends and Plato begins — is strictly insoluble, though the standard working hypothesis assigns the early dialogues' method-without-doctrine to something like the real Socrates and the constructive metaphysics to Plato. The deeper question is what the form *means*. On the traditional reading, the dialogues are doctrine in costume, and the mouthpiece-of-the-moment speaks for Plato. On anti-dogmatist readings — ancient (the skeptical Academy claimed him) and modern — the form is the message: philosophy is essentially an activity between interlocutors, arguments are to be re-enacted rather than believed on authority, and the *Phaedrus*'s own critique of writing (a text cannot answer questions or choose its readers) explains why Plato refused the treatise. The *Seventh Letter*, if his, adds that on the deepest matters he never wrote and never would. The interpretive consequence is permanent: every statement "Plato held that..." is a reconstruction, and the dialogue form forces on his readers exactly the labor of active judgment his theory of education demands — which may be its point.

## Influence and reception

The **Academy** he founded outlasted him by three centuries and disagreed with him almost immediately: his nephew Speusippus abandoned the Forms for numbers; Aristotle, the greatest product of the school, made the criticism of participation and the Third Man the launching point of his own metaphysics; and from Arcesilaus onward the Academy read Plato as a skeptic and specialized in refutation. The dogmatic Plato returned in Middle Platonism (Forms as thoughts in God's mind) and above all in **Plotinus**, whose Neoplatonism — the One beyond being, Intellect, Soul, and the ascent of the *Symposium* systematized into mysticism — was the reigning philosophy of late antiquity and, for centuries, simply what "Platonism" meant. Through Augustine, who found in "the books of the Platonists" everything in Christianity except the Incarnation, **Christian Platonism** became a load-bearing wall of Western theology: the intelligible world, the soul's kinship with the eternal, evil as privation. The Renaissance recovery of the full Greek text (Ficino's translations), the Cambridge Platonists, and the perennial rebirths of mathematical platonism — Frege and Gödel both accepted the label — kept the metaphysics alive. The pushback is equally canonical: Aristotle on Forms; Nietzsche, for whom Christianity was "Platonism for the people" and Plato the inventor of the life-slandering "true world"; and Popper, who indicted the *Republic* as totalitarianism's blueprint — a charge the scholarship has heavily qualified without ever quite dissolving the illiberal core of philosopher-rule. Whitehead's footnote remark, in context, makes the sober point: Plato staked out the problems; the tradition is the working-through.

## Reading path

Begin with the *Apology*, *Crito*, and *Euthyphro* — short, dramatic, and the best portrait of the Socratic method — then the *Meno* as the hinge into epistemology. The *Symposium* and *Phaedo* give the middle-period metaphysics in its two moods, erotic and austere. Then the *Republic*, the center of the corpus, ideally whole. Only after that attempt the hard late work: *Theaetetus* (knowledge), *Parmenides* (the self-criticism), *Sophist* (non-being), and *Timaeus*. Cooper's *Plato: Complete Works* (Hackett) is the standard English edition. Secondary: Annas's *An Introduction to Plato's Republic*; Kraut's *Cambridge Companion to Plato* for the state of the questions; Vlastos's *Socrates: Ironist and Moral Philosopher* for the Socratic problem.

## Assessment

Editorially: Plato's greatness lies less in the Forms than in the fact that the *Parmenides* and the *Sophist* were written by the man who invented them. The system is the first great instance of the metaphysical impulse; the self-criticism is the first great instance of philosophy's answering discipline; and the dialogue form holds the two together in permanent, deliberate tension. Read as doctrine, Plato is refutable and was refuted first by himself. Read as a demonstration of what it is to hold a theory responsibly — to build it, arm its enemies, and keep thinking — he is not yet superseded.
