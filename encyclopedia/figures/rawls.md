# John Rawls

*1921–2002 · American political philosopher in the social-contract and Kantian tradition; author of* A Theory of Justice *and the re-founder of normative political philosophy in the analytic world*

## Why they matter

For much of the twentieth century Anglophone political philosophy was widely thought to be finished: the positivists had ruled evaluative claims cognitively empty, and what remained was conceptual analysis of words like "authority" and "freedom," or the history of dead systems. *A Theory of Justice* (1971) ended that by example. It set out a full, systematic, argued theory of a just society — principles, a procedure that generates them, a method for testing them, an account of how institutions embodying them could be stable — and it did so in a form that invited replies at every joint. The replies came, and they became the field: libertarian, communitarian, feminist, Marxist, cosmopolitan, and racial-contract critiques are all, in their first formulations, responses to Rawls. The original position and the veil of ignorance are now philosophical common property; the difference principle is the most-discussed distributive principle since utility; reflective equilibrium is the method most moral philosophers admit to using. Rawls's later turn to the problem of stability under deep disagreement — *Political Liberalism* — set a second agenda, on legitimacy and public reason, that shows no sign of closing.

## Life

Rawls was born in Baltimore in 1921, the second of five sons of a successful lawyer and a mother active in the women's suffrage movement. Two of his brothers died in childhood of diseases — diphtheria, then pneumonia — contracted from him, a fact he never discussed publicly and which biographers connect to his lifelong stammer and to the theme, central to his mature work, of undeserved fortune and misfortune. He attended Princeton, where a senior thesis on sin and faith showed a devout Episcopalian considering the priesthood. Then the war. Rawls enlisted as an infantryman in 1943 and served in New Guinea, the Philippines, and the occupation of Japan, where he saw the aftermath of Hiroshima; a sermon by an army chaplain assuring the troops that God aimed their bullets at the Japanese, and the news of the Holocaust, undid his faith. He declined an officer's commission and left the army as a private. The published record of these years is thin — a late essay condemning the atomic bombings, and a short posthumous statement on his loss of religion — but everything in the theory that treats moral arbitrariness with such gravity bears their imprint.

He returned to Princeton for a doctorate (1950), spent a Fulbright year at Oxford, where Hart, Berlin, and Hampshire were remaking political thought, and taught at Cornell and MIT before settling at Harvard in 1962 for the rest of his career. The life was deliberately quiet: he refused most honors and nearly all interviews, gave few public lectures, and treated his students — a remarkable number of whom became the field's leading figures — with a deference that struck them as almost excessive. He married Margaret Fox in 1949; they had four children. A series of strokes from 1995 slowed but did not stop his revising, and the final restatements of his view were assembled with the help of former students. He died at home in Lexington, Massachusetts, in 2002.

## Works

- **The early papers** (1951–1963) — "Outline of a Decision Procedure for Ethics" (the germ of reflective equilibrium), "Two Concepts of Rules" (practices versus particular acts, still cited in the rule-utilitarianism debate), and "Justice as Fairness" (1958), the first statement of the contract idea and the two principles.
- ***A Theory of Justice*** (1971; revised edition 1999) — the system entire: the original position, the two principles, the argument from the contract, the congruence of justice and the good, and the account of a well-ordered society's stability. Six hundred pages, written and rewritten over two decades.
- **"Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory"** (the Dewey Lectures, 1980) — the method's self-understanding, and the coinage that named a metaethical position.
- ***Political Liberalism*** (1993; expanded 1996, 2005) — the reframing: justice as fairness as a *political* conception, overlapping consensus, public reason. The 1997 essay "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited" is the definitive statement of the last of these.
- ***The Law of Peoples*** (1999) — the theory extended, and pointedly not extended, to relations among societies.
- ***Justice as Fairness: A Restatement*** (2001) — the late, compact, most readable version of the whole view, incorporating three decades of replies.
- **The lecture courses** — *Lectures on the History of Moral Philosophy* (2000) and *Lectures on the History of Political Philosophy* (2007): Rawls reading Hume, Kant, Hegel, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, and Mill as his real interlocutors.

## Thought

### The original position and the veil of ignorance

Rawls's opening claim is that justice is the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of theories, and that its primary subject is the **basic structure** — the constitution, the economy, the family, the major institutions that together fix people's life prospects before they have done anything. The question is what principles should govern that structure, and his answer revives a device that Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, and Kant had used and the nineteenth century had discarded: agreement. Principles of justice are those that free and rational persons would choose in an initial situation of equality to govern their common life. The situation is the **original position**, and its defining feature is the **veil of ignorance**: the parties know the general facts of social science and human psychology, but nothing of their own place in society, class, race, sex, natural talents, or conception of the good, and nothing even of their society's stage of development. Since no one can tailor principles to their own advantage, whatever they agree to is fair; hence *justice as fairness*.

The device does two kinds of work. As a modeling tool it converts a question about justice into a question about rational choice under uncertainty — a question with a tractable structure. As a moral idea it enforces impartiality of a specific kind: not the utilitarian's sympathetic spectator, who sums everyone's interests into one, but a plurality of distinct persons each of whom must be able to accept the result. This is the heart of Rawls's case against utilitarianism, the reigning view he set out to displace. Utilitarianism, he argued, extends the principle of rational choice for one person — accept present losses for greater later gains — to society as a whole, and so treats persons as channels through which utility flows rather than as separate lives with their own claims. It does not take seriously the distinction between persons. The original position is constructed so that it must: no party will gamble their basic liberties on the chance of being in the majority whose greater satisfaction would justify the sacrifice.

### The two principles and the difference principle

The parties, Rawls argues, would choose two principles in a strict order. The **first** requires an equal scheme of basic liberties — political liberty, conscience, association, the person, the rule of law — for each, compatible with the same scheme for all. The **second** permits social and economic inequalities only if they satisfy two conditions: they attach to positions open to all under **fair equality of opportunity**, and they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged — the **difference principle**. The first principle has lexical priority over the second, and opportunity over the difference principle: no gain in wealth can purchase a loss of liberty, once a society is affluent enough for liberties to be effectively exercised. The currency in which the least advantaged are measured is neither welfare nor resources in general but **primary goods** — liberties, opportunities, income, wealth, and the social bases of self-respect: the all-purpose means any rational plan of life needs.

The difference principle is the theory's signature, and its argument is the theory's most contested. Rawls's central thought is that the distribution of natural talents, and of the social circumstances that develop them, is arbitrary from a moral point of view — no one deserves their starting position — and that a society which lets the natural lottery determine shares is merely ratifying luck. Meritocracy, on this view, is not the remedy but a refinement of the problem: fair equality of opportunity corrects for social class but leaves the lottery of talent untouched. The difference principle treats the distribution of talents as a **common asset**, permitting the talented to reap gains only on terms that improve the situation of those who lost the draw. The formal argument is that parties behind the veil, unable to assign probabilities to their positions and facing outcomes they could not live with, would reason by **maximin**, choosing the arrangement whose worst position is best. Critics from Harsanyi onward objected that maximin is an irrational rule for choice under uncertainty; Rawls's later restatements lean less on decision theory and more on the idea that principles must be publicly justifiable to all, including the worst off, who could not accept a principle that sacrifices them for aggregate gain. The institutional upshot was, he insisted, not the welfare state but a **property-owning democracy** in which capital and skills are widely dispersed in advance, rather than redistributed after the fact.

### Reflective equilibrium and Kantian constructivism

How could such a theory be justified, when Rawls granted the positivists that there is no moral fact to be discovered and no foundational axiom to be derived from? His answer is **reflective equilibrium**. We begin with **considered judgments** — the moral convictions we hold with confidence under conditions favorable to judgment: that slavery is unjust, that religious persecution is wrong. We formulate principles that would account for them. Where principles and judgments conflict, we revise in whichever direction seems, on reflection, more compelling — sometimes abandoning a judgment the principle exposes as prejudice, sometimes rejecting a principle whose implications we cannot accept — until the two cohere. Justification is thus neither top-down nor bottom-up but mutual: a matter of everything fitting together into one view, and the original position itself is defended this way, as the modeling of premises we already accept about fairness and impartiality. Daniels's widening of the method to include background theories — of persons, of the role of morality in society — is what Rawls actually practices.

The Dewey Lectures of 1980 gave this method a name and a lineage. **Kantian constructivism** holds that principles of justice are not discovered as facts about an independent moral order, as rational intuitionism held, but constructed through a procedure — the original position — that expresses a certain **conception of the person**: citizens as free and equal, possessing two moral powers, a capacity for a sense of justice and a capacity to form, revise, and pursue a conception of the good. The procedure has authority because that conception has authority for us. This was Rawls's reading of Kant's autonomy: not a noumenal self legislating from outside nature, but the idea that persons are bound only by principles they would give themselves under conditions that express their freedom and equality. The reading is deliberately detranscendentalized — Kant with the metaphysics of the two standpoints removed — and it is the origin of a metaethical position that Korsgaard and others would develop far beyond Rawls's own, more modest, ambitions.

### Political Liberalism: reasonable pluralism, overlapping consensus, public reason

*A Theory of Justice* had argued that a well-ordered society would be stable because its members, raised under just institutions, would come to see justice as congruent with their good — an argument that quietly assumed everyone shared something like Kantian liberalism as a comprehensive philosophy of life. By the 1980s Rawls judged the assumption false and the falsity important. Free institutions produce, and will always produce, a diversity of reasonable comprehensive doctrines — religious, philosophical, moral — that cannot be reconciled, not because people are foolish but because of the **burdens of judgment**: evidence is conflicting, concepts are vague, experience differs. This is the **fact of reasonable pluralism**, and a liberalism that rested its own justification on one comprehensive doctrine — Kant's autonomy, or Mill's individuality — would be simply another sectarian faith, maintained, if at all, by the oppressive use of state power.

*Political Liberalism* reconceives justice as fairness as a **political conception**: worked out for the basic structure alone, presented as freestanding from any comprehensive view, and drawn from ideas implicit in the public culture of a democratic society. Stability is then to be secured by an **overlapping consensus**, in which citizens holding different comprehensive doctrines each affirm the political conception from within their own view, for their own reasons — the Kantian because it honors autonomy, the Catholic because it protects conscience, the utilitarian because it promotes welfare — rather than by a mere balance of forces that would shift with the balance. The **liberal principle of legitimacy** follows: political power is legitimate only when exercised in accordance with a constitution whose essentials all citizens, as reasonable, may be expected to endorse. And from legitimacy comes **public reason**: on constitutional essentials and basic justice, citizens and officials should justify their positions in terms all can accept as free and equal, not in terms of religious or philosophical doctrines others reasonably reject — a constraint Rawls later loosened with a *proviso* allowing comprehensive reasons to be voiced so long as properly public reasons are in due course supplied. The contrast with Mill is instructive rather than flattening: Mill's liberalism is comprehensive, defending liberty because it produces a particular kind of human being; Rawls's is political, refusing to say which kind of human being is best, on the ground that a free people will never agree about that.

### The Law of Peoples

Rawls's last book extends the contract to relations among societies and denies the extension nearly everyone expected. The parties to a **second original position** are not individuals but *peoples* — liberal societies, and *decent* non-liberal societies that respect basic human rights and consult their members through some hierarchy — and what they choose are familiar principles of international law: independence, non-intervention, fidelity to treaties, just war, a **duty of assistance** to *burdened* societies unable to sustain decent institutions. What they do not choose is a global difference principle. Rawls's reasons are that a people's wealth depends chiefly on its political culture and institutions rather than on resources, so that redistribution across borders would subsidize bad governance; that the duty of assistance has a *target and a cutoff* — the achievement of decent institutions — beyond which inequalities among peoples are not unjust; and that liberal peoples must tolerate decent hierarchical ones rather than treat their internal illiberalism as a fault to be corrected. The cosmopolitan critics — Beitz, Pogge, and others who had already extended the original position globally in the 1970s — objected that the theory's own logic overruns its border: birthplace is as arbitrary as talent, a global basic structure already shapes life prospects, and making peoples rather than persons the parties abandons the individualism the whole theory was built on. Rawls's defenders (Nagel and Blake most influentially) reply that egalitarian justice is triggered by a specific relation — shared coercive authority exercised in one's name — which no global institution has, and that Rawls was right to see the international case as one of toleration rather than distribution.

## Influence and reception

The measure of Rawls's influence is that the field's map can be drawn as a map of his critics, each marking a boundary of the program. **Nozick**'s *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) is the libertarian reply: justice is historical, not patterned, holdings are just if justly acquired and transferred, and the treatment of talents as a common asset licenses a claim on persons that self-ownership forbids. **Sandel**'s *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) opened the communitarian front: the original position presupposes an "unencumbered self," prior to and independent of all its ends and attachments, whereas real persons are partly constituted by community and history, and a politics built on the abstraction cannot sustain the solidarity the difference principle needs — an objection *Political Liberalism* is in large part designed to answer. **Okin**'s *Justice, Gender, and the Family* (1989) accepted the theory and pressed it on its blind spot: Rawls names the family as part of the basic structure and then exempts it from the principles, though it is the site where citizens' sense of justice is formed and where gendered inequality is reproduced; taken seriously, the veil would hide one's sex and the family would be reformed accordingly. **G. A. Cohen**, from the egalitarian left, argued in *Rescuing Justice and Equality* (2008) that the difference principle's tolerance of incentive-based inequality is a concession to the talented's unwillingness to work for less, which a just *ethos* — not merely a just basic structure — would forbid; a society whose members held Rawls's principles would not need the inequalities the principle permits. **Mills**'s *The Racial Contract* (1997) and the essays on ideal theory charged that a theory beginning from a well-ordered society under full compliance idealizes away the racial domination that has actually structured the societies Rawls wrote for, and produces tools unfit to diagnose it.

Other engagements shaped the view as much as they opposed it. **Hart**'s 1973 critique of the priority of liberty led Rawls to rebuild the first principle around a specified list of basic liberties tied to the two moral powers. **Habermas**'s exchange with Rawls in 1995 set the discourse-theoretic against the contractarian route to legitimacy. **Sen** and **Nussbaum**'s capabilities approach began as an objection to primary goods as a metric. **Dworkin**'s and later luck egalitarianism began as a reading of what the difference principle should have said. **Scanlon**'s contractualism carried the method from institutions to interpersonal morality. And more recent scholarship has turned historical and skeptical: Forrester's *In the Shadow of Justice* (2019) reads the Rawlsian consensus as a contingent product of postwar American liberalism that narrowed the field's questions; political realists (Williams, Geuss) charge the whole program with moralism, treating politics as applied ethics and neglecting power. The reception outside philosophy has been wide — Rawls is cited in constitutional courts, economics, and public health — and the *Theory* is among the most translated works of twentieth-century philosophy.

## Reading path

Begin not with the *Theory* but with ***Justice as Fairness: A Restatement*** (2001): short, clear, and the view as Rawls finally wished it stated. Then *A Theory of Justice* itself, in the revised edition — Part One (chapters 1–3) contains the original position and the argument for the two principles and is what most readers need; Part Three, on stability and the good, is the part Rawls later rethought and the one that repays specialists. Then *Political Liberalism*, ideally the expanded edition with "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited," reading Lectures I, IV, and VI first. *The Law of Peoples* is brief and can be read on its own. Among the critics, Nozick's chapter 7, Sandel's introduction and chapter 1, and Okin's chapter 5 are each a single sitting. Secondary: Freeman's *Rawls* is the standard comprehensive guide; Pogge's *John Rawls: His Life and Theory of Justice* is the best short life; Forrester's *In the Shadow of Justice* is the historical corrective.

## Assessment

Editorially: Rawls's achievement is less any principle than the demonstration that a systematic, rationally contestable theory of justice could be built at all, and his later work is the rare case of a philosopher rethinking a masterpiece's foundations under criticism rather than defending them. Whether the retreat from comprehensive to political liberalism strengthened the view or hollowed it — leaving a theory that can be affirmed by everyone reasonable precisely because it no longer says why — is the question the next generation inherited and has not settled. The difference principle's fate is a fair index of the man: the most humane distributive idea of its century, and one whose author regarded his own good fortune, and others' misfortune, as nothing anyone had earned.
