# Ludwig Wittgenstein

*1889–1951 · Austrian-British philosopher; author of two complete and opposed philosophies, each foundational for a school of the analytic tradition*

## Why they matter

Wittgenstein is the only figure in the modern canon who produced two finished philosophies, repudiated the first in building the second, and saw each become the charter of a movement. The *Tractatus* — the picture theory of the proposition, logic as tautology, the strict boundary between what can be said and what can only be shown — was taken by the Vienna Circle as the founding document of logical empiricism. The *Philosophical Investigations* — meaning as use, language-games, the rule-following considerations, the private language argument — became the charter of ordinary-language philosophy and remains the most cited work of twentieth-century philosophy. Beneath both runs a single, constant conviction: that philosophical problems are not discoveries awaiting solution but confusions generated by misunderstanding the workings of language, so that the aim of philosophy is not theory but clarity — a conception that indicts most of what philosophers do, including most of what has been done with Wittgenstein. No other thinker poses so sharply, from inside the discipline, the question of what the discipline is for.

## Life

Born in Vienna in 1889, the youngest child of Karl Wittgenstein, one of Europe's richest steel magnates, in a household of high musical culture and private catastrophe — three of his four brothers died by suicide. He studied aeronautical engineering at Manchester, was driven by the foundations of mathematics to Frege, who sent him to Russell at Cambridge in 1911; within a year Russell regarded him as his successor. He enlisted in the Austro-Hungarian army in 1914, sought front-line postings, was repeatedly decorated, and finished the *Tractatus* as a prisoner of war at Monte Cassino. Convinced it had solved the problems of philosophy, he gave away his entire inherited fortune — partly, anonymously, to poets including Rilke and Trakl — and left the subject: six unhappy years as a village schoolteacher in lower Austria (ended by his striking of a pupil), a spell as a monastery gardener, and the design, exact to the millimeter, of his sister's Vienna house. Conversations with Ramsey and the economist Sraffa, and the challenge of Brouwer's 1928 Vienna lecture, drew him back; he returned to Cambridge in 1929, submitted the *Tractatus* as his doctoral thesis, and spent two decades dismantling it in lectures and notebooks, succeeding Moore in the chair in 1939. During the Second World War he left the lectern to work as a hospital porter and laboratory assistant. He resigned the professorship in 1947 — he thought academic philosophy a kind of living death and urged his best students out of it — and wrote *On Certainty* in his final eighteen months, dying of cancer in Cambridge in 1951. His last words: tell them I've had a wonderful life. The biography is not ornament. For Wittgenstein philosophy was an ethical demand on the person — a struggle for honesty against the will's resistances as much as the intellect's — and the fortune given away, the wars volunteered for, the chairs resigned are that conception enacted.

## Works

- ***Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*** (1921) — the only book published in his lifetime: seven numbered propositions and their ramifying comments, from "The world is all that is the case" to the injunction to silence.
- ***The Blue and Brown Books*** (dictated 1933–35) — the middle period in transition: language-games introduced as a method, the *Tractatus* under demolition.
- ***Philosophical Investigations*** (posthumous, 1953) — the second philosophy: an album of remarks on meaning, rules, understanding, and sensation, structured as a running argument with the *Tractatus* and with an interlocutor's voice.
- ***Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*** (1956) — the rule-following considerations applied to proof and necessity; the most contested part of the corpus.
- ***On Certainty*** (1969) — the final notebooks: Moore's "I know I have hands" and the hinges on which doubt turns.

## Thought

### The Tractatus: picture theory and the limits of language

The *Tractatus* asks how language can represent the world at all, and answers with the **picture theory**: a proposition is a picture of a possible state of affairs. Names stand for objects; the arrangement of names in the proposition mirrors a possible arrangement of objects; the proposition is true if the arrangement obtains. What proposition and world must share for this to work — **logical form** — cannot itself be pictured, since it is the precondition of picturing. The world, correspondingly, is the totality of facts, not of things: atomic facts are configurations of absolutely simple objects. Here lies the real relation to Russell's logical atomism, which the book is often read as merely refining. Russell's atoms were epistemological — sense-data, the things acquaintance bottoms out in. Wittgenstein's simples are demanded a priori: if sense is to be determinate, analysis must terminate somewhere, so there *must* be simples — and he pointedly declines to give a single example. His atomism is a requirement of logic, not an inventory of experience, which is why the *Tractatus* contains no epistemology at all, and why Wittgenstein thought Russell's admiring introduction had missed the point. On this scaffolding sits the book's account of logic: the logical constants do not represent ("my fundamental idea"); complex propositions are truth-functions of elementary ones; and the propositions of logic are **tautologies**, true under every assignment, saying nothing about the world. Logic's necessity is thereby demystified — not the deepest truths about reality, as Frege and Russell had it, but the by-product of what any sign-system capable of representing must be. The metaphysics of the *Tractatus* is austere and total; its afterlife runs from Carnap's syntax to truth-conditional semantics, which still begins from the thought that to understand a proposition is to know what is the case if it is true.

### Saying, showing, and the ladder

The doctrine Wittgenstein told publishers was the book's real point is the distinction between **saying and showing**. What can be said is exactly what can be pictured: contingent configurations of objects — the propositions of natural science. Everything that gives life and thought their significance falls outside this: logical form shows itself in propositions but cannot be stated; the same holds for ethics, aesthetics, the self as the limit of the world, the meaning of life, God. These are not dismissed as trash — the *Tractatus* calls them, precisely, the mystical, and Wittgenstein wrote that the book's point was ethical, consisting of two parts, the written one and everything he had not written. But the doctrine devours the book itself: the *Tractatus*'s own propositions, being about logical form, are by its lights nonsense, and its penultimate remark instructs the reader to throw away the ladder after climbing it. What that instruction means is now the central dispute in Tractatus scholarship. The traditional reading takes the nonsense to be *illuminating* — gesturing at ineffable truths that cannot be put into words. The "resolute" reading (Diamond, Conant) takes Wittgenstein at his word: nonsense is nonsense, there are no ineffable truths, and the book is a therapeutic exercise designed to cure the reader of the illusion that its sentences meant anything — which would make the *Tractatus* continuous with the later therapy rather than its target. However that dispute resolves, the final proposition — whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent — is at once a logical thesis and an ethical act: the refusal to chatter about what matters most.

### Meaning as use, language-games, family resemblance

The *Investigations* opens with Augustine describing how he learned to speak — words name objects, sentences combine names — and spends its length showing what this picture conceals. Language is not one thing, a system of representation, but a motley of **language-games**: ordering, describing, joking, thanking, cursing, praying — activities woven into the "forms of life" of those who play them. For a large class of cases, the meaning of a word is its **use** in the language: not an object it stands for, not an idea it evokes, but its role in these practices. The builder's assistant who fetches a slab at the call "Slab!" understands the word, and there is nothing more his understanding consists in than mastery of the technique. Against the demand — the *Tractatus*'s own — that a concept have a common essence fixing its every application, Wittgenstein offers **family resemblance**: examine the things called "games" and you find no one thing common to all, but a network of overlapping similarities, like the resemblances among members of a family; the concept works perfectly well without an essence, and the demand for one was the philosopher's imposition, not the language's requirement. The reversal of the *Tractatus* is exact: where the early work legislated a priori what language must be for sense to be determinate, the later work looks at what language is, and finds the determinacy demand itself to be the source of the puzzlement. Use-based, pragmatist, and inferentialist theories of meaning descend from these sections; so does the standing objection to them, pressed by truth-conditional semanticists, that "use" is too coarse a notion to explain compositionality — how finite speakers understand unbounded novel sentences.

### Rule-following and Kripke's skeptic

The deepest stretch of the *Investigations* (roughly §§138–242) asks what it is to follow a rule. A pupil taught to add 2 continues the series correctly to 1000, then writes 1004, 1008 — and insists he is going on in the same way. What fact about his training, or ours, makes him wrong? Every finite set of examples is compatible with deviant continuations; a rule formulated in words is itself just more signs, needing interpretation in turn; a mental image or formula before the mind can be applied variously. Hence the paradox of §201: if every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule on some interpretation, then no course of action is determined by the rule. Wittgenstein's own resolution is that the paradox rests on a false assumption — that grasping a rule is always an *interpretation*. There is a way of following a rule that is not interpretation but practice: acting blindly, as trained, within a custom; agreement in judgments among practitioners is not the abolition of objectivity but its background condition. **Kripke's** 1982 reading made these sections an industry of their own. His Wittgenstein is a skeptic who concedes there is *no fact* in virtue of which I mean addition rather than the deviant function "quaddition" — no fact about my past usage, dispositions (which are finite and include errors), or inner life — and who then offers a "skeptical solution": meaning-attributions are not descriptions of facts but moves licensed by a community's practice of concept-attribution. Whether "Kripkenstein" is Wittgenstein is widely doubted — Baker and Hacker argued the paradox is stated only to be dissolved, McDowell that Wittgenstein rejects the skeptic's opening demand — but Kripke's skeptic set the agenda for three decades of work on the metaphysics of meaning and the thesis that meaning is normative. The same considerations, applied to mathematics in the *Remarks*, yield Wittgenstein's most scandalous claims: that proof does not track pre-existing necessity but forges it, that the mathematician is an inventor, not a discoverer — a position Dummett named full-blooded conventionalism and most philosophers of mathematics have found easier to dismiss than to refute.

### The private language argument

Could there be a language that only its speaker could understand — one whose words refer to his immediate private sensations, the things that (on the Cartesian picture) only he can know? Wittgenstein argues no, and the argument, concentrated around §§243–315, targets the entire modern epistemology of mind at its root. Suppose I resolve to write "S" in my diary whenever a certain sensation recurs, with no outward criterion for its occurrence. What makes a later use of "S" *correct*? I have only my impression that this sensation is the same again; but then whatever is going to seem right to me is right, "and that only means that here we can't talk about 'right.'" A sign with no standard of correct use is not a word, and a ceremony of inner ostension — concentrating on the sensation while saying the sign to oneself — fixes nothing, for it provides no technique of application. The moral is not behaviorism: Wittgenstein does not deny sensations, but denies that their grammar is the grammar of object and designation — "it is not a *something*, but not a *nothing* either." Sensation-language gets its life from natural expression and shared circumstance: "pain" is taught to the child crying over a scraped knee, and the beetle in everyone's private box — the famous image — drops out of the language-game as irrelevant to how the word works. The consequences radiate: the problem of other minds is transformed (my relation to another's pain is not inference from behavior to hidden cause but attitude toward a soul); first-person authority is reconceived as expressive rather than observational; and every empiricism that builds knowledge from private givens loses its foundation. Whether the argument succeeds — whether memory-skepticism about "S" proves more than general skepticism about memory would — remains disputed; that it changed the philosophy of mind is not.

### Philosophy as therapy

Both philosophies issue in a metaphilosophy, and the later one is explicit: philosophical problems have the form "I don't know my way about." They arise when language "goes on holiday" — when forms of words are taken out of the language-games where they work and asked metaphysical questions they were never shaped to answer; we are held captive by pictures (the inner object, the crystalline rule, the name-and-object model) that lie *in* our language and repeat themselves to us inexorably. Philosophy, accordingly, is "a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language." It advances no theses and explains nothing; it *describes* — assembles reminders of how words are actually used, invents intermediate cases and imaginary language-games — until the problem does not get solved but disappears. There is no single method, but methods, like therapies: the aim is "to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle," and the real discovery is the one that gives philosophy peace. Steelmanned, this is not anti-intellectualism but a diagnosis with a mechanism: it predicts where philosophical problems will cluster (wherever surface grammar assimilates unlike uses — "I have a pain" modeled on "I have a pin"), and it explains a datum most metaphilosophies must awkwardly excuse, philosophy's millennia of unresolved disagreement. Its critics answer that the diagnosis is itself a thesis, defended by argument like any other; that some problems (consciousness, mathematical truth) survive every grammatical clarification; and that the therapeutic conception, consistently applied, would end the subject. Wittgenstein accepted the last consequence with equanimity — which is exactly why the discipline that canonized him has never digested this part of him.

### On Certainty: hinge propositions

Moore had answered the skeptic by holding up his hands: here is one hand, here is another, therefore an external world exists — things he claimed to *know* with certainty. Wittgenstein's last notebooks accept Moore's certainties while rejecting his account of them. "I know I have hands," said in ordinary circumstances, is not a piece of knowledge at all: knowledge belongs where doubt, checking, and evidence have a place, and nothing is more certain than these propositions by which any checking would be done. They are **hinges**: propositions that stand fast so that inquiry can turn — "the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn." The earth has existed for many years; there are physical objects; my name is what I believe it to be — these form the inherited world-picture, the scaffolding of our language-games, held not by evidence but by everything we do; they belong to the riverbed of thought rather than the river. The skeptic's mistake is not asserting a falsehood but simulating a doubt: doubt that doubted everything would not be doubt, for it would have dissolved the very practices — of asserting, testing, meaning one's words — that give "doubt" its sense. The position is neither foundationalism (hinges are not self-evident premises; they are not propositions we reason *from* so much as the form of our reasoning) nor coherentism, and its classification remains contested. "Hinge epistemology" is now a research program — Wright's entitlements, Pritchard's non-epistemic hinge commitments, Moyal-Sharrock's non-propositional certainties — and the notebooks, written by a dying man and never revised, are the most influential unfinished book in modern epistemology.

## Influence and reception

The first reception was a productive misreading. The **Vienna Circle** read the *Tractatus* line by line and took from it verificationism and the elimination of metaphysics — discarding as embarrassment the saying/showing doctrine and the mysticism that Wittgenstein considered the point. Carnap's logical syntax and the empiricist criterion of meaning are Tractarian instruments turned to purposes their author disowned; Wittgenstein eventually refused to meet Circle members other than Schlick and Waismann. The second wave he seeded in person: through pupils and executors — **Anscombe** (whose *Intention* founded modern action theory on Wittgensteinian ground), Malcolm, Rhees, von Wright — and through Ryle's *Concept of Mind* and the Oxford ordinary-language school, the *Investigations* dominated Anglophone philosophy into the 1960s. The pushback was proportionate. **Russell**, who had sponsored the early work, declared the later philosophy an abdication of serious thinking that had made philosophy "at best, a slight help to lexicographers"; Popper treated the therapeutic conception as a betrayal of philosophy's real problems (their one meeting, the poker incident, is the discipline's favorite anecdote); Gellner's *Words and Things* attacked the whole school as a complacent sociological formation. The deeper eclipse came not from polemic but from practice: Quinean naturalism, Chomskyan linguistics, and the systematic semantics and metaphysics of Kripke, Montague, and Lewis simply resumed the theory-building Wittgenstein had declared misconceived, and by the 1980s he was cited everywhere and followed almost nowhere. The current revival has three strands: the resolute reading's rewriting of the *Tractatus*; hinge epistemology's colonization of *On Certainty*; and the use of the rule-following and private-language material by philosophers of mind and meaning (McDowell, Wright, Brandom's inferentialism) who accept the arguments while declining the therapeutic frame. Beyond the analytic core his mark runs through anthropology and social theory (Winch, Geertz), theology, and aesthetics. He remains the discipline's standing self-accusation: a thinker who held that philosophy as professionally practiced was a disease of the intellect, canonized by the profession he diagnosed — and neither party to that irony has yet explained it away.

## Reading path

Do not start with the *Tractatus*. Start with the ***Blue Book***: dictated in plain English, it introduces the later method with the least apparatus. Then the ***Philosophical Investigations*** (Anscombe's translation, revised by Hacker and Schulte), read slowly: §§1–133 on meaning and philosophy, §§138–242 on rules, §§243–315 on private language carry the core; it is an album, and rereading is the intended mode. Then the ***Tractatus*** (Pears–McGuinness translation), ideally alongside Russell's "Philosophy of Logical Atomism" for contrast, and ***On Certainty*** last — it is short and self-standing. Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* is essential and should be read as Kripke advertises it: the argument that struck him, not exegesis. Secondary: Monk's biography *The Duty of Genius*, the rare life of a philosopher that is also an interpretation; Hacker and Baker's commentaries for the traditional reading; Crary and Read's *The New Wittgenstein* for the resolute one; McGinn's *Routledge Guidebook* as the accessible companion to the *Investigations*.

## Assessment

Editorially: the fashionable question — early or late Wittgenstein? — is less instructive than the continuity both books enforce: that the limits of sense are the real subject of philosophy, and that most philosophical assertion transgresses them. The rule-following considerations and the private language argument seem to this encyclopedia the strongest sustained arguments in twentieth-century philosophy of mind and language, and largely unanswered rather than answered. The therapeutic metaphilosophy is another matter: it is defended by arguments, which makes it a thesis in the genre it condemns, and the discipline's decision to mine Wittgenstein for doctrines while shelving his conception of what doctrine is worth may be self-serving — but it may also be the correct verdict on the one part of his work where the diagnosis outran the evidence. He would have regarded this article, with its tidy sections and steelmanned "positions," as a symptom. That the symptom is the only way to transmit him is the joke he built into his legacy.
