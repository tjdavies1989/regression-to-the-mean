# Dharmakīrti

*fl. c. 6th–7th century CE · Indian Buddhist philosopher of the pramāṇa school; epistemology, logic, and metaphysics at classical India's highest pitch of rigor*

## Why they matter

Dharmakīrti is the Buddhist tradition's most rigorous philosopher and, by a defensible margin, the philosopher's philosopher of classical India: the thinker every subsequent school — Buddhist, Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, Jain — had to answer, whether to build on him or to bury him. Inheriting from Dignāga a young science of knowledge-sources (pramāṇa), he rebuilt it into a complete system in which epistemology, logic, semantics, and metaphysics interlock: exactly two ways of knowing, matched to exactly two kinds of object; a criterion of reality (causal efficacy) that generates a metaphysics of momentary particulars; and a theory of concepts (apoha, "exclusion") that explains generality without admitting a single universal into the world. For five centuries his system set the agenda of Indian philosophy, and after Buddhism's disappearance from India it became the backbone of Tibetan scholastic education, where it is still taught, debated, and memorized. No other Indian philosopher's technical machinery has had so long a working life.

## Life

Almost nothing about Dharmakīrti's life is secure, including his century: scholars place his activity somewhere between about 550 and 660 CE, the traditional dating favoring the seventh century. The Tibetan hagiographies, written a millennium later, make him a South Indian brahmin who converted, traveled north to the great monastic university of Nālandā, and studied the pramāṇa system under Īśvarasena, a pupil of Dignāga's — a lineage claim consistent with his works, which repeatedly correct Īśvarasena's positions by name. The one nearly first-personal note is famous: verses framing the *Pramāṇavārttika* complain that no contemporary is equipped to understand it, and that the work will age within its author "as water in the ocean." The complaint proved half right — there is little evidence he was widely read in his lifetime — and then spectacularly wrong.

## Works

Tradition counts **seven treatises on pramāṇa**, the core surviving partly in Sanskrit and completely in Tibetan translation:

- ***Pramāṇavārttika*** (*Commentary on the Compendium of Valid Cognition*) — nominally a commentary on Dignāga's *Pramāṇasamuccaya*, actually the system's masterwork, in four chapters: inference-for-oneself (with Dharmakīrti's own prose auto-commentary, his most argumentatively dense writing), the establishment of the Buddha as an authority, perception, and inference-for-others.
- ***Pramāṇaviniścaya*** (*Ascertainment of Valid Cognition*) — the mature restatement, much of whose Sanskrit was recovered only recently from manuscripts preserved in Tibet.
- ***Nyāyabindu*** (*Drop of Logic*) — a compact manual of the whole epistemology and logic; the standard entry point ever since.
- ***Hetubindu*** (*Drop of Reasons*) and ***Sambandhaparīkṣā*** (*Examination of Relations*) — monographs on the theory of evidence and on relations, the latter arguing that relations are conceptual constructions, not additional realities.
- ***Vādanyāya*** — the rules of debate, with a theory of what loses one an argument.
- ***Santānāntarasiddhi*** (*Establishment of Other Mind-Streams*) — a defense of other minds available even to an idealist, from the inference of agency behind observed behavior.

## Thought

### Two pramāṇas: the architecture of knowledge

Every classical Indian school opened its system by declaring its pramāṇas — the instruments that make cognition knowledge. Where Nyāya counted four (adding testimony and analogy) and Mīmāṃsā up to six, Dharmakīrti, following Dignāga, admits exactly **two: perception and inference**, and grounds the count in ontology: there are only two kinds of object, the unique particular and the constructed universal, and each requires its own mode of access. A pramāṇa, on his defining formula, is cognition that is **nondeceptive** (avisaṃvādin) — it reliably delivers the practical results it promises; knowledge is vindicated by successful engagement with things, not by correspondence contemplated from nowhere. Testimony, on this austere accounting, is not a third source but inference from a speaker's credibility — a demotion with large consequences, since it makes scripture itself answerable to reason: even the Buddha's word counts as knowledge only where it concerns matters beyond ordinary access and survives the test of coherence with what perception and inference establish. The *Pramāṇavārttika*'s second chapter argues at length that the Buddha is an authority — but earns the title by demonstrated reliability about suffering and its cure, not by revelation. The framework is thus simultaneously an epistemology and a religious epistemology: a Buddhism that stakes its claims on argument.

### Particular and universal: the two-tiered real

The system's metaphysical hinge is the divide between **svalakṣaṇa** — the bare particular, unique, momentary, ineffable — and **sāmānyalakṣaṇa**, the "universal-characterized" object of thought and language. Only particulars are ultimately real, and Dharmakīrti supplies the criterion: **to be real is to be causally efficacious** (arthakriyā — the capacity to produce effects, paradigmatically the effects one seeks: fire is real because it cooks and burns). Universals fail the test: cowhood never gave milk. What thought and language traffic in — kinds, properties, enduring objects — are conceptual constructions, superimposed on the flux of particulars and useful precisely because they blur differences that practice can ignore. Perception, defined as cognition **free of conceptual construction** and nonerroneous (the second clause is Dharmakīrti's tightening of Dignāga's definition), grasps the particular in its unrepeatable singularity; inference and language handle the constructed general. The result is a two-tier account of truth reminiscent of, but not derived from, the Buddhist two-truths doctrine: a conventional world of stable, nameable things, fully adequate for practice, floating on an ultimate reality of point-instants that no description fits. The Western analogy — a causal or "Eleatic" criterion of existence wielded on behalf of nominalism — illuminates only if one adds what has no Western twin: the claim that conceptualization as such falsifies, that thought's generality is exactly what reality lacks.

### Apoha: concepts by exclusion

If there are no universals, what does "cow" mean, and why does it apply to some particulars and not others? Dharmakīrti's answer develops Dignāga's **apoha** ("exclusion") theory into a full nominalist semantics: the word "cow" does not name a shared essence; it *excludes* — its content is the negation *not non-cow*. Generality is manufactured by thought's ignoring of differences: particulars utterly distinct in themselves are grouped because each is excluded from the class of things that fail to serve certain ends. The Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā realists pressed the obvious circularity — to exclude the non-cows one must already know which things are cows — and Dharmakīrti's reply is the theory's distinctive move: the grouping is anchored not in a prior grasp of cowhood but in **causal facts**. Certain particulars, and not others, are disposed to produce the same effects — milk, the satisfaction of the same interests, and, crucially, the same recurring judgment in conditioned perceivers — and sameness of effect requires no shared essence in the causes, any more than fever-reducing herbs of unrelated species need a common "fever-quelling universal." Classification is thus real work done by minds tracking causal regularities under the pressure of interests, and error is possible because the construction answers to how particulars actually perform. Built from double negation and causation, apoha is a nominalism with resources its Western cousins (resemblance and predicate nominalism) lack — and burdens they escape, since everything now rests on unconceptualized causal difference doing the sorting.

### Inference and the natural bond

Dharmakīrti's logic asks a question his predecessors had left soft: what *entitles* an inference — why does smoke on the hill license fire on the hill? Dignāga had answered with a formal test (the "three characteristics" of a good reason, essentially a scheme of copresence and coabsence), and Īśvarasena, pressed by the Naiyāyika Uddyotakara's counterexamples, had retreated to mere non-observation of counterinstances. Dharmakīrti saw that no survey of instances, however clean, yields necessity, and grounded inference instead in a **natural connection** (svabhāvapratibandha) between the evidence and what it proves — a connection of exactly two kinds. Either the reason is **causally related** to the property inferred (smoke is fire's effect; effects do not occur without their causes), or it is **essentially related** (being a śiṃśapā tree just is a way of being a tree, so the first entails the second by identity of nature). To these he adds a third form of legitimate reason, **non-perception**: the warranted judgment that there is no pot on the table, grounded in the failure to perceive what would, if present, necessarily be perceived. The typology is a substantive thesis: all genuine evidential force reduces to causation, conceptual containment, or licensed absence, and induction unsecured by one of these is superstition. Read against the Western problem of induction the position is striking — Dharmakīrti concedes Hume's point about enumeration and answers it with metaphysics, buying the necessity of inference at the price of a world articulated by real causal natures.

### Momentariness: existence as flash

The Buddhist doctrine that all conditioned things are impermanent becomes, in Dharmakīrti's hands, the radical thesis of **momentariness** (kṣaṇikatva) — whatever exists lasts exactly one moment — established by his most celebrated argument, the inference **from existence itself**. To exist is to be causally efficacious; so consider anything allegedly enduring unchanged through time. At each moment it either produces its effect or does not. If its intact nature suffices to produce the effect, it must produce it at every moment it exists — the tree burns the instant it exists, seeds yield sprouts in the granary. If it needs auxiliary conditions, then when they arrive either they alter it — in which case the "assisted" thing is a numerically new entity and permanence is surrendered — or they leave it unaltered, in which case they contribute nothing and the effect should have occurred without them. A strictly unchanging thing therefore either does everything at once or nothing ever; staggered efficacy, which is what we observe, entails a new entity at every moment. A companion argument removes the last support of endurance: destruction needs no cause — perishing is intrinsic to whatever arises, since a thing that needed something else to make it cease would, left alone, be permanent. Stable objects are conceptual constructions over causal series of momentary particulars, as a "flame" is a series of ignitions. Nyāya's rejoinder — that recognition ("this is the same jar I saw") is perceptual evidence of endurance — Dharmakīrti dismisses as conceptual construction misread as perception; the thousand-year debate that followed turned on exactly that line.

### Self-awareness and the sliding scale

Every cognition, Dharmakīrti holds, cognizes itself in cognizing its object: **svasaṃvedana**, reflexive awareness, is one of perception's four varieties (with sense perception, mental perception, and the yogic perception that makes liberating insight an empirical possibility). Awareness is self-luminous, like a lamp that reveals itself in revealing the room; if a cognition required a second cognition to be known, a regress would follow, and the felt immediacy with which experience is present to itself would be inexplicable. This doctrine is the pivot on which the system's deepest instability — or deepest strategy — turns. Much of the time Dharmakīrti argues as an external realist of broadly Sautrāntika stripe: perception is caused by external momentary particulars, which it represents through their imprinted aspect (ākāra). But at key junctures he ascends to **Yogācāra idealism**, most sharply in the argument from necessary co-apprehension: blue and the awareness of blue are never given apart, and what can never be found apart cannot be shown to be two; the object may be nothing beyond the aspect within awareness, its externality an inference we never needed. Interpreters since the medieval commentators have disputed which is the real Dharmakīrti; the influential modern reading (Dreyfus, Dunne, McClintock) sees instead a deliberate **sliding scale of analysis** — arguments pitched at the level of ontological commitment the opponent grants, external realism as a way station, reflexive awareness alone surviving at the highest level. On that reading the system's ladder-like structure is not equivocation but pedagogy in the Buddhist grain: provisional frameworks discarded as analysis deepens.

## Influence and reception

Within Buddhism, Dharmakīrti simply became the curriculum. A commentarial line — Devendrabuddhi and Śākyabuddhi consolidating, Dharmottara revising the theory of nondeceptiveness, Prajñākaragupta reading the system at its most idealist — carried the works forward; Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla fused his epistemology with Madhyamaka; and the last great Indian Buddhists, Jñānaśrīmitra and Ratnakīrti, spent their genius refining apoha and momentariness against ever-sharper realist attack. The attack is the other half of his legacy: Dharmakīrti's running battle with **Nyāya** — answering Uddyotakara, answered in turn by Vācaspati Miśra, Jayanta, and finally Udayana, whose defense of the self and demolition of momentariness closed the argument only because Buddhism itself vanished from India — is one of philosophy's great sustained rivalries, comparable in duration and discipline to nothing in the West before the medieval schools. Mīmāṃsā's Kumārila, apoha's fiercest critic, belongs in the same story; the realists' best arguments were forged against Dharmakīrti, and his school's against them.

When Buddhism died in India (c. 12th–13th century), the system emigrated. In **Tibet** it became the backbone of scholastic education: Sakya Paṇḍita's *Treasury of Reasoning* fixed the interpretive tradition; the Gelug curriculum built its degree course, and the daily practice of formal courtyard debate, on Dharmakīrtian epistemology; monks still memorize the *Pramāṇavārttika*. (East Asia, which received Dignāga through Xuanzang but almost nothing of Dharmakīrti, developed no comparable tradition — a reminder of how contingent transmission is.) Western scholarship began in earnest with Stcherbatsky's *Buddhist Logic* (1930–32), which dressed Dharmakīrti as a neo-Kantian; the Vienna school (Frauwallner, Steinkellner) replaced portraiture with philology, and the last two generations (Matilal, Dreyfus, Tillemans, Dunne, and successors) have brought him into direct conversation with analytic epistemology and philosophy of language — where apoha now figures as a serious entry in the theory of concepts, not an exotic anticipation of one.

## Reading path

Direct entry is hard: the *Pramāṇavārttika* presupposes Dignāga and argues in compressed verse. Start instead with the ***Nyāyabindu***, the system's own primer, alongside Dharmottara's commentary (Stcherbatsky's old translation is serviceable; newer partial translations are more accurate). For orientation, Tillemans's Stanford Encyclopedia article on Dharmakīrti is the best short map. Then the two indispensable monographs: Dunne's *Foundations of Dharmakīrti's Philosophy* (the system read on the sliding scale, with translations from the *Pramāṇavārttika*'s first chapter) and Dreyfus's *Recognizing Reality* (the system through its Tibetan interpreters). Matilal's *Perception* sets the Buddhist–Nyāya rivalry in a frame analytic readers will recognize; the Siderits–Tillemans–Chakrabarti collection *Apoha* brings the exclusion theory into contemporary philosophy of concepts. For the religious stakes of the second chapter, Franco's and Eltschinger's studies of the proof of the Buddha's authority.

## Assessment

Editorially: Dharmakīrti is the strongest available evidence that "rigor" names no single tradition's property. His system has the virtue Frege's has — commitments wired together tightly enough to be refutable, so that its failures teach — and the additional interest that its center of gravity is inverted relative to most Western epistemology: causal efficacy, not representation, defines both reality and knowledge. The sliding scale still divides interpreters because it is genuinely double-edged — either the subtlest dialectical architecture in Indian philosophy or a system that never decides what exists. That both readings survive the texts is itself a measure of how much argument he compressed into them.
