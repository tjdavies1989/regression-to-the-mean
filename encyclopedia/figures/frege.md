# Gottlob Frege

*1848–1925 · German logician and philosopher of mathematics; founder of modern logic and fountainhead of the analytic tradition*

## Why they matter

Frege's importance can be dated to a single book: the *Begriffsschrift* of 1879, which replaced a logic essentially unchanged since Aristotle with the quantificational logic in use everywhere today. He built the instrument for a purpose — to prove that arithmetic is nothing but logic — and that program forced from him the distinctions (sense and reference, concept and object, thought versus idea) that became the founding equipment of the philosophy of language, a field that did not exist before he needed it. The program collapsed, wrecked by a paradox Russell found in its foundations; the collapse proved as fertile as the program, setting the agenda for set theory, type theory, and the philosophy of mathematics for a half-century. Analytic philosophy's two defining habits — treating logical form as the deep structure beneath grammar, and approaching thought through language rather than psychology — are both, directly, his.

## Life

Born in 1848 in Wismar, on the Baltic coast, Frege studied at Jena and Göttingen, took his doctorate in 1873, and spent his whole career at the University of Jena, promoted slowly and read little. The external life was nearly eventless: decades of lecturing to tiny audiences; a marriage ended by his wife's death in 1904; an adopted son, Alfred. The internal life was organized around one project and one catastrophe — Russell's letter of June 1902, arriving as *Grundgesetze* II was in press, which Frege answered with famous candor, acknowledging at once that the foundation of his life's work had given way. He never rebuilt the system, came to suspect that arithmetic might rest on geometry instead, and retired in 1918. A diary from 1924, published only in 1994, revealed the political opinions considered below. He died in Bad Kleinen in 1925, still almost unknown; his papers, deposited at Münster, were largely destroyed in the bombing of 1945.

## Works

- ***Begriffsschrift*** (1879) — the "concept-script": the first complete presentation of quantificational logic, in an axiomatic system.
- ***The Foundations of Arithmetic*** (1884) — the philosophical statement of logicism, and by common consent one of the best-written books in philosophy: the rivals demolished, numbers analyzed as extensions of concepts.
- **The three great essays** (1891–92) — "Function and Concept," "On Sense and Reference," "On Concept and Object": the semantic doctrines, worked out as the mature system's scaffolding.
- ***Basic Laws of Arithmetic*** (*Grundgesetze*, vol. I 1893, vol. II 1903) — the formal execution of logicism, resting on the fatal Basic Law V; volume II closes with the appendix confessing Russell's paradox.
- ***Logical Investigations*** (1918–23) — late essays, above all "The Thought": thoughts as timeless, mind-independent entities, and anti-psychologism at its most uncompromising.

## Thought

### The Begriffsschrift and the invention of quantificational logic

Before Frege, formal logic meant the syllogistic plus a propositional supplement, and statements of multiple generality — "for every x there is a y such that..." — had no home in it. Frege's solution was to abandon the subject–predicate analysis inherited from grammar for the mathematician's distinction between **function and argument**: "Cato killed Cato" can be analyzed as a function of its first name-position, its second, or both, and which analysis is right depends on the inference being drawn, not the sentence's surface. Add a sign for generality — the quantifier — binding variables, and multiple generality becomes routine: "everyone loves someone" and "someone is loved by everyone" differ simply in the scope of two quantifiers. With this apparatus Frege gave the first axiomatic presentation of what is now first- and higher-order logic, defined the ancestral of a relation (the key to defining the natural numbers in purely logical terms), and separated a proposition's content from its being asserted, marking assertion with his judgment-stroke. The *Begriffsschrift* also inaugurated the modern ideal of a formal system: inference gapless, every assumption explicit, nothing intuitive smuggled in unnoticed. Almost nobody read it; its two-dimensional notation died; everything else about it became the discipline of logic.

### Logicism: the Grundlagen and Grundgesetze

The logic was built to answer a Kantian question. Kant held that arithmetic, like geometry, is synthetic a priori, grounded in pure intuition. Frege thought geometry might be intuitive but arithmetic could not be: its laws govern everything thinkable, not merely everything spatiotemporal, and a foundation that narrow insults its universality. His thesis — **logicism** — is that arithmetic is analytic: derivable from general logical laws plus definitions. The *Grundlagen* demolishes the alternatives (Mill's pebble-arithmetic, formalism's ink-marks, psychologism's mental images), then argues that a statement of number is an assertion about a *concept*: "there are eight planets" says of the concept *planet* that eight objects fall under it. Numbers are defined by abstraction — the number of Fs is the extension of the concept "equinumerous with F," where equinumerosity, one-one correspondence, is definable in pure logic — and from this Frege defines zero, successor, and, via the ancestral, the finite numbers, and derives arithmetic's basic laws. The principle now called **Hume's Principle** — the number of Fs equals the number of Gs just in case the Fs and Gs correspond one-one — does most of the mathematical work, a fact whose significance emerged only a century later. The *Grundgesetze* was the formally gapless execution, resting on basic laws including the fateful Law V. The prize, had it succeeded: arithmetical knowledge explained without Kantian intuition, empiricist induction, or psychology — certainty inherited from logic itself.

### The collapse: Russell's letter and Basic Law V

Basic Law V asserts that two concepts have the same extension just in case the same objects fall under them — a harmless-sounding bookkeeping principle that functions as naive comprehension: every concept gets an extension, an object. In June 1902 Russell wrote to Frege pointing out that the class of all classes that are not members of themselves yields a contradiction: it is a member of itself if and only if it is not. Frege saw immediately that this was no local repair job. His reply, and the appendix to *Grundgesetze* II, are monuments of intellectual honesty: he states the paradox more exactly than Russell had, locates the fault in Law V, and observes that it afflicts every extant attempt to ground arithmetic on classes. His patched Law V was itself later shown inconsistent, and Frege concluded that the extensions strategy — and with it, since he saw no other route to logical objects, logicism — was hopeless. Two codas matter. The paradox launched the century's foundational programs: Russell's type theory and Zermelo's axiomatized set theory are rival principled answers to the question Law V answered carelessly — which concepts determine collections. And the neo-logicism of Wright and Hale showed in the 1980s that Frege's own derivation of arithmetic needs only Hume's Principle, which is consistent; whether that principle counts as logic, or as an implicit definition of number, is the live question logicism's descendants now dispute.

### Sense and reference

"The morning star is the evening star" was a discovery of astronomy; "the morning star is the morning star" is news to no one. Yet if a name's meaning were exhausted by the object it stands for, the two sentences would say the same thing. Frege's solution distinguishes the **reference** (*Bedeutung*) of an expression — the object designated — from its **sense** (*Sinn*) — the mode of presentation, the way the object is given. "Morning star" and "evening star" name one planet under two senses; hence the identity is informative. The doctrine generalizes into a full semantics. Sentences have both: their sense is the **thought** (*Gedanke*) expressed; their reference, Frege argued, is a truth-value — the True or the False — since reference is what survives exchange of co-referring parts, and truth-value is exactly what such exchanges preserve. The theory earns its keep on the hard cases. In belief reports, substitution of co-referring names fails; Frege's diagnosis is that in such **indirect contexts** words refer to their customary senses — belief relates a thinker to a thought, not to objects. Empty names ("Odysseus") have sense without reference, which is why fiction is intelligible but neither true nor false. And sense is no private idea: it is objective and shareable — what makes communication and science possible, as mental images, varying from head to head, could never be.

### Concept and object

Frege's ontology divides reality exhaustively by logical role. **Objects** are what singular terms stand for: complete, "saturated," self-standing. **Concepts** are what predicates stand for: functions from objects to truth-values, essentially incomplete — "( ) is a horse" carries an empty place only an object can fill, and this unsaturatedness is what binds a sentence into a unity rather than a list. The distinction is absolute, and it underwrites both the logic (quantifiers are second-level concepts, saying of a first-level concept that it is instantiated — why existence is not a predicate of objects, and why the ontological argument fails) and the philosophy of arithmetic (number statements are about concepts). It also generates Frege's most notorious puzzle, which he faced rather than hid: "the concept *horse* is not a concept" seems true on his own theory, since "the concept *horse*," a singular term, must stand for an object. Frege conceded an "awkwardness of language" — the distinction is real but strictly unstatable, showable only in use. Critics from Kerry onward have read the paradox as evidence that the saturated/unsaturated division cannot be drawn as absolutely as Frege needs; sympathizers, including the early Wittgenstein, drew instead the moral that logical category-distinctions can only be shown, not said — a thought with a long afterlife.

### The context principle

The *Grundlagen* announces three methodological rules, and the second — never ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a sentence — became the seed of a revolution. Its immediate work is anti-psychologistic: if word-meaning were a free-standing mental item, "seven" would mean an image, and no image of seven exists; but if the question is what "seven" contributes to the truth-conditions of sentences containing it, the demand for a picture lapses. Its deeper work licenses definition by abstraction: to confer sense on number-terms it suffices to fix the truth-conditions of the identity-statements they occur in — Hume's Principle again. Dummett argued that this principle is the founding act of the "linguistic turn": the moment a question about the nature of abstract objects was transformed, deliberately and with argument, into a question about the functioning of expressions in sentences. Whether the principle survives in Frege's mature, compositional theory of sense, and whether it can bear the ontological weight neo-logicists place on it — can fixing truth-conditions *make it the case* that numbers exist? — are both contested; but the priority of the sentence over the word became common ground for traditions otherwise at war, from Wittgenstein to Quine's and Davidson's holisms.

### Anti-psychologism

The polemical constant of Frege's career is the separation of logic from psychology. Psychologism — the view, dominant in his day, that logical laws are generalizations about how humans think — confuses three things he held apart: the *act* of thinking (psychological, private, datable), the *thought* thought (objective, shareable, timeless), and the *truth* of the thought (independent of anyone's grasping it). If logic described actual thinking, its laws would be contingent on human constitution — differently wired beings would have a different logic, and no one could err. But logic is normative: it states not how we do think but how we must think to reach truth, and its authority does not wait on a census; the Pythagorean theorem was true before anyone proved it and would be true if everyone denied it. This drove Frege to a striking ontology: thoughts and senses occupy a "third realm" — neither physical things nor private ideas — timeless, existing whether grasped or not. The costs are plain (how does a mind grasp a third-realm entity? Frege admits the relation is sui generis), but the influence was enormous: Husserl's turn from psychologism after Frege's harsh 1894 review made anti-psychologism the shared premise of *both* the analytic and phenomenological traditions, and the normativity of logic remains a live problem posed in Frege's terms.

## Influence and reception

Frege's influence ran through a handful of readers who mattered. **Russell** absorbed the logic and the logicism, generalized the program to all of mathematics in *Principia Mathematica*, and pushed back on the semantics: the theory of descriptions (1905) was built to do without sense, handling informativeness and empty names with quantificational structure alone, and the Frege–Russell alternatives still organize the theory of reference. **Wittgenstein**, sent by Frege to Russell, wrote the *Tractatus* in constant dialogue with both; the *Investigations* argues with Frege's picture of sense as much as with Augustine's of naming. **Carnap** sat in Frege's nearly empty Jena lecture room and carried the ideal of formal rigor into the Vienna Circle, where sense and reference resurfaced as intension and extension. The wider profession discovered Frege late and largely through others: Church defended a Fregean semantics in America, and **Dummett**'s *Frege: Philosophy of Language* (1973) made him the contested origin-point of analytic philosophy and the linguistic turn — a reading Sluga and later scholars, restoring Frege's Kantian and nineteenth-century mathematical context, pushed back against as anachronistic. The strongest doctrinal pushback came from the anti-descriptivist revolution: Kripke's and Putnam's arguments that names and kind-terms refer without mediation by anything like Fregean sense — though neo-Fregeans (Evans, McDowell) replied that sense, construed as object-dependent, survives the attack.

Reception now includes a darker document. Frege's 1924 diary, published in 1994, contains virulent antisemitism — including approval of the idea of expelling Jews from Germany — alongside hostility to democracy and Catholics and admiration for far-right nationalist figures of early Weimar. Dummett, who had given years to Frege and decades to fighting racism in Britain, recorded his shock in print and did not extenuate. The debate since divides over context and connection: some historians stress that the diary dates from the embittered final year of an isolated man amid Weimar's crises, though correspondence suggests the attitudes were not wholly new; others ask whether anything in the philosophy is implicated. The near-consensus is that the doctrines — quantification theory, the semantics, anti-psychologism — neither derive from nor support the politics, which makes Frege the sharpest available test case for separating the validity of a body of work from the character of its author. The diary forecloses hagiography; it cannot touch the mathematics.

## Reading path

Start with the ***Foundations of Arithmetic*** (Austin's translation): short, lucid, self-contained, requiring no logical notation — the introduction and §§55–91 carry the core. Then the essays of 1891–92 in order — "Function and Concept," "On Sense and Reference," "On Concept and Object" (all in Beaney's *The Frege Reader*): "On Sense and Reference" is the most assigned paper in philosophy of language, but it presupposes the first essay's function–argument apparatus. Then "The Thought" for anti-psychologism and the third realm. The *Begriffsschrift* and *Grundgesetze* are for readers with logical training; the *Grundgesetze* appendix on Russell's paradox rewards anyone. Secondary: Kenny's *Frege* as the accessible introduction, Dummett's *Frege: Philosophy of Language* as the monumental (and tendentious) classic, Wright's *Frege's Conception of Numbers as Objects* for the neo-logicist revival.

## Assessment

Editorially: Frege is the best argument in the modern canon for judging a philosopher by problems opened rather than doctrines secured. Nearly every headline thesis failed or remains besieged — logicism collapsed, the third realm has few tenants, sense is under permanent siege — yet the *Grundgesetze*'s failure taught more than most systems' successes, because it was rigorous enough to fail definitively. That rigor is his deepest legacy; the diary is a permanent reminder that it coexisted with grave moral failure, and that the work must be prized apart from the man to be prized at all.
