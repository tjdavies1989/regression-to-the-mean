# Aesthetics

In 1917 Marcel Duchamp submitted a urinal to an exhibition — and *Fountain* now sits in the canon. The scandal is philosophical: the urinal in the gallery is perceptually indistinguishable from the one in the warehouse, so whatever makes it art is not visible in it. Danto drew the moral that arthood depends on a surrounding "artworld" of theory and history; Dickie hardened this into the institutional theory, on which arthood is a conferred status. Functionalists object that institutions can be wrong — art earns its title by affording aesthetic experience. Historical definitions tie new art to the intended regard of past art; Weitz, following Wittgenstein, argued that "art" is an open concept no definition can close. The question is hard because every proposed boundary is answered by artists who make crossing it the point of the work.

A second family of problems concerns judgment. Calling a building beautiful claims more than liking it: we argue, cite features, feel that dissenters miss something — yet nothing measurable settles the matter. Hume located the standard of taste in the verdicts of practiced, unprejudiced critics; Kant held that judgments of taste rest on felt pleasure yet rightfully demand universal assent — the antinomy his third Critique labors to resolve. Their heirs divide into realists, for whom aesthetic qualities are features responses track, and sentimentalists, who build the standard from refined response. Nearby puzzles have their own literatures: why we weep for Anna Karenina while knowing she never existed (the paradox of fiction), why we seek out tragedy and horror, and whether a work's moral corruption — the elegance of propaganda — is ever an aesthetic defect (autonomists: never; ethicists: sometimes). A symphony survives its score and every performance, so it seems a type — but types are abstract, and abstract objects are not supposed to be created. Interpretation asks whose meaning a work bears: the author's intention, none (the "intentional fallacy"), or what an informed audience would hypothesize.

The field articulates into a core — the definition of art, aesthetic experience and judgment, beauty — ringed by the ontology of artworks, interpretation, and the questions of emotion and morality; each major art form sustains a philosophy of its own, and newer work extends aesthetics past art to nature and everyday life. Its boundaries: the nature of emotion is homed in mind, fictional entities in metaphysics, meaning and metaphor in language; and where aesthetic value meets moral value, the line to ethics is itself contested.

## Bridges

- To `metaphysics` and `language`: the ontology of artworks imports the debate over types and abstract objects; interpretation and depiction share machinery with theories of meaning.
- To `mind` and `epistemology`: aesthetic experience and the fiction paradox run on theories of emotion and perception; whether taste can be got from testimony is a live question.
- To `ethics`: ethical criticism of art, propaganda, and censorship cross into moral and political value.
- To the registers: figures run from Plato and Aristotle through Hume, Kant, Hegel, and Danto; German idealism and critical theory carry the traditions register.
