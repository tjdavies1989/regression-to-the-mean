# Depiction

A few strokes of ink on a flat page and you see a bull. Nothing on the page is a bull, or even bull-shaped in three dimensions; yet the recognition is immediate, unlearned in any obvious sense, and available to a child who could not read a word of any description of the same animal. The problem of depiction is to say what this relation is — in virtue of what does a marked surface represent its subject, and why does grasping a picture feel like *seeing* rather than *decoding*?

The natural first answer is resemblance: the picture depicts the bull because it looks like one. Goodman's *Languages of Art* (1968) is the classic demolition. Resemblance has the wrong logical shape — it is reflexive and symmetric, while depiction is neither: everything resembles itself, and the bull does not depict its portrait. Worse, a painting resembles every other painting far more than it resembles any bull. Goodman concluded that depiction is a species of denotation, distinguished from description not by any natural tie to its subject but by formal features of the symbol system — syntactic and semantic density, relative repleteness — so that pictorial realism is nothing but habituation to the locally standard system. The strength of the position is its discipline: it explains stylistic variety, and it forces every rival to say precisely what resemblance is supposed to do. Its notorious cost is that pictures come out as conventional as words, when the plainest datum in the field is that they are not: no one learns Flemish painting the way one learns Flemish.

The post-Goodman theories divide over where to relocate the perceptual core. Wollheim locates it in a distinctive experience, *seeing-in*: an ability, prior to depiction, to see things in marked surfaces — a face in a stained wall — which pictures harness and standards of correctness (the artist's intention) discipline. Its signature is *twofoldness*: in looking at a picture we are simultaneously aware of the surface and of what is seen in it, which is why brushwork can inflect what is depicted. Walton assimilates pictures to his general theory of representation as make-believe: a picture is a prop that mandates imaginings, and its distinctively pictorial character is that our actual seeing of the canvas is itself part of the game — we imagine, of our seeing of the surface, that it is a seeing of the bull. Recognition theories (Schier, developed by Lopes) start from the datum Goodman suppressed: pictures are *naturally generative* — once you can interpret one picture in a system, you can interpret indefinitely many more, provided only that you can recognize their subjects. Depiction works by recruiting the same recognitional capacities exercised on the objects themselves, which explains without mystery why pictorial competence is cheap and description is not. Perspective and realism cut across all these: whether linear perspective is optical truth or European habit, and what makes one style more realistic than another, remain live questions inherited from Gombrich and Goodman.

Photography sharpens everything. Walton argued that photographs are *transparent*: because the photograph counterfactually depends on the scene through purely mechanical channels, unmediated by anyone's beliefs, we literally see our dead relatives through their photographs, as we see through mirrors and telescopes. Critics (Currie, Carroll) object that seeing requires egocentric spatial information — seeing something locates it relative to you, which a snapshot does not — so photographs give us something evidentially special but not sight. Under the whole field lies the deep contrast between depiction and description, the picture and the sentence: whether it marks a difference in symbol systems, in the perceptual capacities recruited, or in kinds of content — analog against digital, iconic against discursive — is where depiction hands its problem to the philosophy of language and of perception.
