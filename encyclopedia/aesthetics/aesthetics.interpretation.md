# Interpretation and Intention

A poet is asked in an interview what her poem meant, and her answer contradicts the reading every careful critic has settled on. Who is right? The question has bite because both verdicts feel mandatory: the poem is *hers* — surely she knows what she was doing — and yet if her stated meaning cannot be found in the words, insisting on it looks like changing the subject from the poem to her biography. The problem of interpretation is to say what fixes a work's meaning: the artist's intention, the public text alone, or something built between them.

The modern debate opens with Wimsatt and Beardsley's "The Intentional Fallacy" (1946): the author's intention is "neither available nor desirable" as a standard, because a work is a public object in a public language, and an intention succeeds only insofar as it is realized in the work — where it is realized, appeal to it is redundant; where it is not, appeal to it is special pleading. The strength of this **anti-intentionalism** is its fidelity to critical practice: critics argue from the words on the page, and no archive of avowals can rescue a poem that fails to mean what its author hoped.

**Actual intentionalism** answered that texts are not driftwood. In Carroll's conversation model, engaging with art is relevantly like conversation: just as we care what our interlocutor meant, not merely what her sentences could mean, we owe the artist an interest in what she was doing. Its strongest cards are irony, allusion, and reference — features a text cannot carry on syntax and semantics alone. Moderate versions let intentions fix meaning only within what the text can support, blunting the "special pleading" charge.

**Hypothetical intentionalism** (Levinson) is the acknowledged via media: a work means what an ideal audience — fully informed about the artist's oeuvre and public context, but barred from private avowals — would most reasonably hypothesize the artist intended. This preserves the conversational stance while explaining why the interview answer does not simply win: authors get authority over the evidence, not a veto over the verdict. **Value-maximizing** accounts (S. Davies, Goldman) go a step further from the author: among interpretations the work admits, prefer the one that makes it best — the practice of interpretation aims at rewarding works, not decoding senders. **Constructivists** (Margolis, Krausz; Fish's interpretive communities) deny there is a fully determinate object waiting to be decoded at all: interpretation partly constitutes its object. The continental parallel is Barthes's "The Death of the Author" (1967) and Foucault's author-function — the birth of the reader as the death of the author — reached from theories of writing and discourse rather than from analysis of meaning, with philosophical hermeneutics (Gadamer's fusion of horizons) offering a historically dialogical alternative to both camps.

Cutting across all of this is **critical pluralism**: can incompatible interpretations — the psychoanalytic and the Marxist *Hamlet* — both be acceptable? Monists (Beardsley; Hirsch, who split determinate *meaning* from changing *significance*) say at most one is correct; pluralists say interpretive practice visibly tolerates more. The pluralist then owes an answer to a sharp question: if both are acceptable but incompatible, are they interpretations *of the same work*, or does each construct its own object — at which point pluralism shades into constructivism and the ontology of the work is back on the table.
