# Art and Emotion

Charles grips his seat as the green slime oozes toward the camera. His pulse races; he may shriek. Yet he never phones the police, never flees the cinema, and would calmly deny that any slime exists. Radford made the trouble explicit: we seem committed to three claims that cannot all be true. We are genuinely moved by fictions; genuine emotion requires believing that its object exists; and we do not believe the slime, or Emma Bovary, exists. Radford's own verdict — that our tears for fictions are real but irrational, a soft spot in human rationality — has served mostly as the position everyone else defines themselves against.

Walton denies the first claim. Charles feels *quasi-fear*: real sensations and arousal, but generated inside a game of make-believe in which it is fictionally true, not actually true, that he fears the slime. The view's strength is its systematicity — it belongs to a full theory of representation as prop-governed make-believe, and it explains why Charles stays in his seat: quasi-fear motivates nothing outside the game. Thought theories deny the second claim instead: vividly entertaining a content — merely holding before the mind the thought of a child dying — can arouse emotion without belief, as anyone who has shuddered at an imagined fall from a cliff edge already knows. Their strength is economy: no new mental kind, just the observation that belief was never necessary for feeling. Illusion theories deny the third claim — absorbed spectators half-believe, their disbelief "willingly suspended" — and though crude versions founder on Charles's undisturbed calm about the actual world, the position has been rehabilitated through Gendler's *alief*: an automatic, belief-discordant response that also explains why we hesitate to step onto a glass skywalk we know is safe.

A second paradox: we pay to weep. Tragedy, melodrama, and horror deliberately arouse feelings we spend ordinary life avoiding, and audiences return for more. Hume's answer is conversion — the eloquence and formal beauty of the telling dominate the painful passion and convert its very energy into the pleasure of the whole. Carroll's answer for horror is compensation: the monster is a categorical violation, and the fascination of the unclassifiable pays for the disgust it necessarily arouses. Rich-experience views reject the hedonic bookkeeping in both: we do not seek painful art because pain is secretly converted or outweighed, but because we value experiences for depth, self-knowledge, and felt contact with what matters, not for pleasure alone — sad music is wanted *as* sad.

Emotion enters art from the maker's side too. For Tolstoy, art just is the transmission of feeling — the artist infects the audience with an emotion once lived, and sincerity is the measure of success. For Collingwood, expression is not transmission but articulation: the artist begins with an inchoate perturbation and, in expressing it, discovers what it is; art proper is this exploration, and arousing feelings to order is mere craft, the technique of the advertiser. Collingwood's heirs have detached expressiveness from any actual episode in the artist — a work can be expressive of a sadness nobody felt — turning expression into a property of the work's appearance and handing philosophy of music its central puzzle.

Narrative art adds engagement with persons. Whether we *empathize* with Anna — simulating her situation from the inside — or *sympathize* from outside, feeling for rather than with, shapes accounts of suspense, identification, and how fiction educates feeling. All of these debates run on an underlying theory of what emotion is: judgment theories make the paradox of fiction sharp, while perceptual and appraisal theories largely dissolve it. That theory is homed in mind.
