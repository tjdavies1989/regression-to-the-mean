# Philosophy of Music

The slow movement of the *Eroica* is a funeral march, and any listener hears it as sorrowful. But it is only organized sound. It has no vocabulary, no reference, no propositions; nothing in it means "grief" the way the word does. Nor is the orchestra grieving, and the listener need not be. Sadness seems to be *in* the music — heard there, not merely caused by it — while the music, unlike a face or a story, has no one whose sadness it could be. This is the central puzzle of the field: instrumental music is the paradigm of meaning without semantics, expressive to nearly everyone and expressive of nothing anyone can specify.

The oldest answer, arousal theory, says the music is sad because it makes listeners sad. Its strength is honesty about the phenomenon — music demonstrably moves us, sometimes to tears. Its embarrassment is that we can hear a passage as anguished while enjoying it calmly, and that "moving" and "sad" cross-classify: a sad piece may leave us cold, an exuberant one may move us to weep. Eduard Hanslick's formalism drew the radical conclusion: the content of music is "tonally moving forms," and emotion talk is loose metaphor for purely musical beauty. Formalism's strength is discipline — it takes seriously that nothing in a score picks out an emotion — but it must explain away too much of how competent listeners actually describe what they hear. Resemblance or contour theories, developed by Peter Kivy and Stephen Davies, split the difference: music is expressive of sadness because its dynamic shape — drooping lines, slow tempi, dark timbre — resembles the contour of sad human comportment, as a St. Bernard's face looks sad without feeling anything. Kivy's later "enhanced formalism" folds this back into Hanslick: expressive properties are heard features of the form itself. Persona theories, in Jerrold Levinson and Jenefer Robinson, object that resemblance is too inert to explain why we hear music as *expressing* rather than merely matching: we imagine, they argue, an indefinite agent — a musical persona — undergoing the emotion the music traces, which restores an owner for the feeling at the price of imputing imaginative work some listeners disown.

The expressiveness debate opens outward. Musical understanding: following a symphony seems to require no theory — Kivy insisted the untutored listener who can hum the theme back understands it — yet something separates hearing music from hearing noise, and what that is, if not technical knowledge, is disputed. Profundity: we call the late Beethoven quartets profound, but profundity is normally profundity *about* something, and instrumental music is about nothing; either our praise is confused or aboutness is not required for depth. Behind both stands the nineteenth-century quarrel between absolute music, valued as pure form, and program music, which borrows content from titles and texts. The ontology of musical works — what a symphony is such that it survives its score and all its performances, how improvisation and studio recordings strain the work-concept — is homed at `ontology-of-art` and only pointed to here; music is that debate's hardest and most discussed case.
