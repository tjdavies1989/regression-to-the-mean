# Philosophy of Film

A camera is a machine. Point it at a street and it records the street, indifferent as a photocopier — so how could its output be art? The earliest philosophy of film is defensive: Münsterberg and Arnheim answered the "mere recording" objection by cataloguing everything the screen does that the eye does not — close-ups, cuts, the flatness and framing that let film *transform* rather than duplicate. The defense hardened into a doctrine of medium specificity: each art should exploit what its medium alone can do, so film should maximize montage and manipulation and minimize theatrical staging. The doctrine organized decades of theory before collapsing under Carroll's objections — nothing about a medium's distinctive capacities entails that its works are better for exploiting them, and "the medium" of film is anyway several media at once.

The deepest divide inverts the defensive posture. For Bazin, the camera's mechanical indifference is not film's embarrassment but its glory: the photographic image is ontologically bound to what caused it, and film's vocation is realism — the long take and deep focus that preserve the world's ambiguity, against the formalist tradition (Eisenstein's montage) that builds meaning by cutting. Bazin's intuition returns in analytic dress as Walton's transparency thesis: photographs are perceptual aids, so in watching *casablanca* we literally see, indirectly, the actors of 1942 — a claim whose critics reply that seeing requires the counterfactual and egocentric ties that photographs sever. Cavell's *The World Viewed* belongs here too: film is "a succession of automatic world projections," a medium that satisfies the skeptic's wish to view the world unseen, and whose philosophical interest lies in that strange relief.

Two further problems are film's own. First, authorship: a film emerges from hundreds of hands, yet auteurism — imported from the *Cahiers du cinéma* critics by Sarris — treats the director as its author and reads oeuvres for a signature. Its strength is critical fruitfulness; its weakness is the production record, which supports multiple-authorship views (Livingston, Sellors, Gaut) or the skeptical conclusion that most films have no author at all. Second, spectatorship: what are viewers doing? Talk of "identification" with characters fragments, under analysis, into distinct imaginative alignments; and the imagined-seeing debate asks whether we imagine seeing the fictional events — which threatens to make us invisible observers inside the fiction, present at murders and deathbeds — or merely see images that prescribe imaginings, with Walton, Currie, Gaut, and Wilson dividing over how much imagining-from-inside the experience requires.

The frontier case is interactive. Videogames put moving images under the player's control, adding agency to spectatorship; whether they are art (the institutional battle replayed a century after film's), how interactivity changes appreciation, and whether the work is the code, the performance, or the space of play are now the liveliest questions descending from film's old ones.

## Children

- `realism` — **Photographic Realism and Transparency** — whether films let us literally see the world through them, and what the camera's causal bond to its objects does to film's art status. *(standard)*
- `authorship` — **Film Authorship** — who, if anyone, authors a collaboratively made film: auteurism, multiple-authorship views, and no-author skepticism. *(standard)*
- `spectatorship` — **Spectatorship and Imagined Seeing** — what viewers imagine and feel: identification, point of view, and the imagined-seeing debate. *(standard)*
- `narration` — **Cinematic Narration** — whether every fiction film has an implicit narrator who shows us its events. *(satellite)*
- `documentary` — **Nonfiction Film** — what distinguishes documentary from fiction film, and whether the camera can assert. *(satellite)*
- `videogames` — **Videogames and Interactive Art** — whether interactive moving images are art and what player agency adds to appreciation. *(standard)*
