# The Ontology of Artworks

If the Louvre burns, the *Mona Lisa* is gone; if every score and recording of the *Eroica* burns, the symphony is arguably intact so long as someone can still perform it. The two works seem to be different *kinds* of thing. A painting behaves like an ordinary physical object — one canvas, at one place, damageable, ownable. A symphony, a novel, an etching, a film behaves like something repeatable: the *Eroica* was performed last night in two cities at once, and neither performance was the work itself, since both could be flawed while the work is not. The ontology of artworks asks what kind of entity a work is, such that these facts come out true — and every answer purchases some of the facts at the price of others.

The natural machinery is the type/token distinction: repeatable works are types, their performances, copies, and prints tokens. Its strength is that it takes the data at face value — the multiplicity of tokens, the one work they share, the work's survival of any token's destruction. Its cost is metaphysical: types are abstract objects, and abstracta are standardly eternal and uncreatable, whereas Beethoven seems to have *composed* the *Eroica* in 1803, not discovered it. Platonists such as Dodd accept the revision — composition is creative *discovery*, selection of a pre-existing sound structure. Against this stands the intuition Levinson built into his theory of **indicated structures**: works are historically embedded — a sound structure *as indicated by* this composer, in this musico-historical context — so that the same structure indicated by another hand at another time would be a different work. Levinson keeps creation and keeps the individuation of works fine-grained (two sonically identical works can differ in properties like originality); the cost is a novel category, an abstractum that comes into being. Nominalists and fictionalists bite other bullets: works as classes of copies, or as convenient fictions.

Goodman's **autographic/allographic** distinction cuts the terrain differently: a work is autographic when even its exactest duplication does not count as genuine (painting — history of production matters), allographic when anything complying with its notation counts (music, literature). The distinction organizes the **forgery puzzle**: a perfect fake of a Vermeer, indistinguishable to any eye, is still not a Vermeer — and, many hold, aesthetically inferior. Why should provenance, which the eye cannot see, matter aesthetically? Formalists say it cannot, and that our recoil from forgery is moral or economic; Goodman replies that knowing the difference changes trained perception; Dutton and Walton argue that achievement and category are partly what we appreciate — a fake is a different achievement, so a different (and lesser) work.

Works-for-performance raise their own layer: the work underdetermines its performances, so what makes a performance a performance *of* it — score compliance, intention, causal chain? — and what does authenticity in performance demand (the historically-informed-performance debate)? Restoration generates parallel paradoxes for singular works: cleaning a darkened canvas, or rebuilding a damaged fresco, either restores the work or replaces it with a new object wearing its name — the ship of Theseus, hanging in a museum.

What is strongest in each camp is also what the field agrees on: any adequate ontology must save the practices — creation, repeatability, forgery, restoration — and the live methodological question (pressed by David Davies) is whether ontology should defer to those practices or correct them.
