# Aesthetic Experience and Judgment

Say the adagio is beautiful and you have not merely reported your pleasure — you have claimed something from everyone else. Yet you can point to nothing that obliges them. There is no measurement to cite, no rule from which "beautiful" follows; a person unmoved by the passage has not miscalculated. The problem of this node is that aesthetic judgment behaves like neither of its neighbors: it is not a preference (we argue about it, and disagreement stings) and not an ordinary factual claim (nothing settles it). What kind of judgment is this, and what kind of experience underwrites it?

Kant's *Critique of the Power of Judgment* is the core text because it refuses to dissolve the strangeness. The judgment of taste, he holds, is grounded in felt pleasure — mine, now, before this object — yet claims *subjective universality*: I speak "with a universal voice," demanding everyone's agreement while wielding no concept that could compel it. The pleasure must be *disinterested*, indifferent to the object's existence, use, or moral worth; and it arises from the *free play* of imagination and understanding — the faculties of cognition engaged, harmonized, but issuing in no determinate cognition. The position's strength is precision of fit: it explains at once why we argue about beauty (the claim is normative) and why argument never terminates in proof (there is no rule). Its cost is a transcendental machinery — the shared faculties that license the demand — that few now accept at face value.

Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste" offers the empiricist alternative: beauty is sentiment, but sentiments can be ranked, and the joint verdict of *true judges* — practiced, delicate, unprejudiced, comparative — is the standard. Its strength is realism about critical practice; its notorious weakness is circularity: the true judges are identified by judging well, and judging well by tracking what true judges say. Whether the circle is vicious or virtuously self-correcting remains a live question.

Twentieth-century *aesthetic attitude* theories (Bullough's psychical distance, Stolnitz's disinterested attention) relocated the aesthetic from judgment to a special mode of regard, available before any object whatever. Dickie's "The Myth of the Aesthetic Attitude" (1964) argued there is no such mental act — only ordinary attention, variously motivated — a demolition widely credited with killing the theory, though experience-based accounts persist in chastened forms. Sibley's "Aesthetic Concepts" (1959) redrew the map: terms like *graceful*, *garish*, *balanced* are not condition-governed — no set of nonaesthetic features entails their application, which requires taste — yet aesthetic character *supervenes* on nonaesthetic features: no two objects alike in every nonaesthetic respect can differ aesthetically. How both can hold is the node's sharpest technical puzzle. The *testimony* puzzle generalizes it epistemically: I can learn from you that the film is Hungarian but seemingly not that it is beautiful — judging on another's say-so looks defective in a way ordinary deference does not, though optimists deny the asymmetry is deep. Behind all of this sits a deflationary threat: perhaps "aesthetic experience" names no single kind — Beardsley's unified accounts against Dickie's skepticism again — and the node's unity is an artifact of a word.
