# Illusionism and Eliminativism about Phenomenality

Illusionism is the thesis that phenomenal consciousness does not exist and that its apparent existence is an artifact of introspection. Frankish's 2016 statement, which fixed the name and the program, distinguishes two grades. *Weak* illusionism concedes that experiences have phenomenal properties but holds that introspection misrepresents some of their features — presenting as intrinsic, ineffable, or simple what is relational, describable, and complex; Pereboom's qualitative inaccuracy hypothesis belongs here. *Strong* illusionism denies the properties outright. Brain states have only *quasi-phenomenal* properties — physical properties that introspection detects and mischaracterizes as phenomenal — and the sense that there is "something it is like" to be in them is a misrepresentation with no phenomenal remainder. Rey defended a version in the 1980s; Humphrey's consciousness-as-magic-show and Graziano's attention-schema theory, on which the brain models its own attention as an ineffable inner glow, are the scientific cousins.

The strong program descends from Dennett. "Quining Qualia" (1988) fixed the target as whatever is simultaneously ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly apprehended, then argued by intuition pump — the coffee tasters Chase and Sanborn, who cannot tell whether their taste or their judgment has changed — that nothing satisfies all four conditions, so the concept has no referent. *Heterophenomenology* is the accompanying method: treat a subject's reports of experience as data about what the subject *believes* to be the case, construct the "heterophenomenological world" they describe as one would a novel's, and explain why the beliefs are held without assuming that world exists. Realists object that the neutrality is spurious — bracketing the reports' truth already discounts first-person data; Dennett replies that it is the stance any third-person science must take toward testimony.

The theory's chief virtue is its ledger. It keeps physicalism without opaque a posteriori identities; it needs no phenomenal concepts with special powers; it avoids epiphenomenalism, since the states that cause reports are the only states there are; and it converts the hard problem into an easy one — explain the mechanism by which introspection produces phenomenal judgments. That last is the *illusion problem*, and it is not optional. Illusionism makes the illusion its explanandum, so a version that cannot say how introspection misfires this thoroughly has explained nothing. The first difficulty is that the seeming must itself be non-phenomenal: if it phenomenally *seems* that things seem some way, the view collapses. Frankish's answer is that the seeming is nothing but the disposition to judge and report — an introspective representation whose content is false, not a further quale. Kammerer sharpens the second difficulty into the *illusion meta-problem*: explain not only why we judge that experience is phenomenal, but why illusionism strikes nearly everyone as obviously false. His proposal: introspection represents its objects as self-intimating, so whatever it delivers arrives labeled undeniable; the resistance is predicted rather than damning.

The objections take that certainty as data. Strawson, in a 2018 essay, called the denial of consciousness the silliest claim ever made, on the ground that experience is the one thing one cannot be wrong about: the appearance–reality distinction every illusion exploits has no purchase where appearance is the reality (Searle's version). Chalmers recasts this as a Moorean argument — people feel pain; if illusionism is true, no one feels pain; so illusionism is false — and argues that no illusionist premise is more credible than the datum it must overturn. A second line, pressed by Balog and by Nida-Rümelin, holds that the illusionist's "seeming" is unintelligible unless it is itself an experience. The illusionist reply is that a Moorean datum whose certainty the theory independently explains is not evidence against the theory; that confabulation and change-blindness research show the faculty behind the certainty to be unreliable; and that the realist buys the datum at the cost of epiphenomenal properties or a science-proof gap.

The debate has converged on Chalmers's meta-problem. Illusionism can be defined as the view that solving the meta-problem solves the hard problem, and its strongest argument is a debunking one: if a complete physical explanation of our phenomenal judgments never mentions phenomenal properties, those judgments would be as they are whether or not the properties existed, so they do not track them. Realists reply that phenomenal beliefs may be partly constituted by the experiences they are about — the acquaintance response — so the explanation cannot be complete while omitting them. The two sides agree on the research program and disagree about what its success would show; that is where the dispute is now conducted.
