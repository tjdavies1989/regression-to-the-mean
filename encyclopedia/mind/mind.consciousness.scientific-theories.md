# Scientific Theories of Consciousness

A scientific theory of consciousness names the property that separates conscious from unconscious processing and says how to test for it. What divides the candidates is one question: is consciousness identical to cognitive access, or can it overflow what a subject can report?

**Global workspace theory** answers that it is access. Baars pictured specialist processors competing for a limited-capacity workspace whose contents are broadcast to all; Dehaene and Naccache's global neuronal workspace gave it an anatomy — long-range fronto-parietal neurons — and a signature, "ignition," a late, nonlinear, self-sustaining amplification separating seen from masked stimuli at matched input. Its best argument is that it explains the psychology of consciousness wholesale: all-or-none masked perception, the attentional blink, the serial bottleneck of thought. Its cost is that a critic can grant every mechanism of availability and ask why availability should feel like anything.

**Integrated information theory** begins from the other end. Tononi starts with axioms — properties self-evident of any experience: it exists intrinsically, is structured, specific, unified, definite — and derives postulates a substrate must satisfy: cause–effect power over itself, composed, informative, irreducible to independent parts, and maximal against overlapping rivals. Φ measures the irreducibility, and consciousness *is* the maximally irreducible cause–effect structure a system specifies. Its strengths are principled: it explains why the cerebellum, with more neurons than cortex, contributes nothing, why anesthesia abolishes experience without abolishing activity (Massimini's perturbational complexity index is its clinical form), and predicts a posterior "hot zone." Its costs are equally principled. Aaronson showed an inert XOR grid has Φ exceeding a brain's; Tononi accepted the consequence. The unfolding argument (Doerig, Schurger, Hess, Herzog 2019) presses harder: any recurrent network can be unfolded into a feedforward one with identical input–output behavior and zero Φ, so no report-based experiment can distinguish IIT's verdicts from their negation — the theory is unfalsifiable or must renounce inference from reports. Defenders reply that the argument applies to any theory tying consciousness to substrate rather than function, which is the point.

The 2023 controversy made these stakes public. A preregistered adversarial collaboration between IIT and the workspace theory, tested by neutral laboratories, reported that conscious content was decodable and sustained in posterior cortex, as IIT predicted, but the predicted posterior synchrony was absent; workspace ignition appeared at stimulus onset but not, as required, at offset. Each lost a prediction. When press coverage read the result as IIT's vindication, 124 researchers signed an open letter calling it "pseudoscience": its commitments — consciousness in grids, organoids, inactive systems — are untestable and its public standing unearned. Respondents, many with no allegiance to IIT, replied that untested is not untestable, that the theory had just submitted to a preregistered test, and that the label harms the field more than its target. What a theory may claim before it can be tested remains open.

**Higher-order theories** enter the laboratory through Lau. Metacontrast masking can match discrimination while awareness differs, and the difference tracks dorsolateral prefrontal cortex; his perceptual reality monitoring theory makes the higher-order state a discriminator tagging first-order representations as world rather than noise or imagery. The prefrontal evidence is contested — Boly and colleagues read it as reflecting report and task, not experience — and no-report paradigms are the attempt to adjudicate. **Recurrent processing theory** (Lamme) takes the overflow side: the feedforward sweep is unconscious, local recurrent feedback within sensory cortex suffices for phenomenal consciousness, and global broadcast adds only access. **Predictive-processing accounts** (Hohwy, Clark, Seth's interoceptive "beast machine") describe perception as controlled hallucination, conscious content as the brain's best current hypothesis, and selfhood as bodily prediction; Seth and Hohwy concede the framework can house a theory of consciousness rather than being one.

What these theories can settle is where, when, and in which systems consciousness occurs: cortical locus, time course, awareness in unresponsive patients, increasingly in animals and machines. What they leave where it was is the gap: each is a theory of experience's correlate, and none says why the correlate is accompanied by anything. Seth's "real problem" reframes the aim as explaining, predicting, and controlling phenomenological properties, betting the hard problem will dissolve as vitalism did. The **measurement problem** (Bayne, Seth, Michel) is the field's internal version of the same difficulty: measures are validated against report, but whether report is a criterion of consciousness or merely evidence of access is what the theories dispute, so no measure can be validated without a theory and no theory tested without a measure. Bayne's proposal — treat consciousness as a natural kind and triangulate tests across patients, infants, animals, and machines — is the current escape.
