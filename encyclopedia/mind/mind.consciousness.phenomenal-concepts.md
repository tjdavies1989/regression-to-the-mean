# The Phenomenal Concept Strategy

The strategy is a bet that the explanatory gap is a fact about our concepts rather than about the world. Every anti-physicalist argument, from Mary to zombies, runs through an epistemic premise — no physical description entails what pain is like — and infers a metaphysical conclusion. The physicalist who accepts the premise owes an account of why it holds if pain simply is a brain state. The phenomenal concept strategist supplies one: concepts of experience formed from the inside are cognitively isolated from every physical and functional concept, so no amount of the latter yields the former, though both pick out one property. Two ways of thinking, one thing thought about; the gap becomes a prediction of physicalism rather than an objection to it.

Loar gave the strategy its canonical form. Phenomenal concepts are *recognitional*: type-demonstratives, "that kind of state," acquired by attending to instances and deployed by reidentifying them, with no descriptive content connecting them to anything neural. Loar's decisive move is to reject what he calls the semantic premise — that when two conceptually independent concepts co-refer, at least one must reach its referent through a contingent mode of presentation, which would be a further property and reopen the dualism. Recognitional concepts, he argues, can refer *directly* to a physical property, presenting it neither as physical nor via anything else. Conceptual duality then carries no ontological weight.

Papineau's quotational account makes the isolation structural rather than stipulated. A phenomenal concept has the form "the experience: ___," with a token or imaginative replica of the experience itself filling the blank; to think of pain phenomenally is partly to undergo or rehearse it. This explains the persistent *feeling* of distinctness — the antipathetic fallacy, in Papineau's phrase: since a neural concept involves no pain and a phenomenal concept does, the identity thought never feels like an identity. Balog's constitutional version sharpens the same idea and turns it into a weapon: a zombie's phenomenal concepts would be empty, so the zombie argument cannot even be stated by the zombie who is supposed to model it, which she takes to show that the argument smuggles in the conclusion.

The strategy's leading critique is Chalmers's master argument. Let P be the complete physical truth and C the thesis that we possess phenomenal concepts with the isolating features the strategist describes. Either P&¬C is conceivable or it is not. If it is, then C is itself not physically explicable — the special features of the concepts are one more unexplained residue, and the gap has been moved, not closed. If it is not conceivable, then C is a thoroughly physical-functional matter, one a zombie could share; but a zombie does not share our epistemic situation, so C cannot explain it. Thick concepts explain but are unexplained; thin concepts are explained but explain nothing. The strategist may choose a horn, not escape both.

Two replies contest the second horn. Carruthers and Veillet argue that Chalmers characterizes our epistemic situation in phenomenal terms — the situation of *having experience and finding it opaque* — which a zombie by stipulation lacks; described functionally, in terms of judgments, inferential isolation, and the incapacity to derive one class of concepts from another, the situation is exactly what the zombie shares, and it is all the physicalist ever undertook to explain. Díaz-León presses the point into a charge of circularity: the demand that C explain a non-deflated epistemic situation presupposes that there is a non-physical fact to be in a situation about, so the master argument is the conceivability argument over again, with the same premise doing the same work. Chalmers grants that the deflationary reading is available and insists that it concedes the datum instead of explaining it.

The deeper suspicion, voiced by Levine and pressed by Stoljar, is that even a fully successful account of *why* phenomenal concepts are isolated leaves the original question standing: why this neural property should be one that any recognitional concept could present as feeling like something. On that view the strategy restates the gap in the vocabulary of concepts. Two further lines threaten it from inside. Tye, recanting, and Ball argue that there are no special phenomenal concepts — Mary in her room already possesses the concept *red* deferentially, as she possesses *water* without chemistry — so the strategy's explanandum is misdescribed. Goff argues from the other side that phenomenal concepts are *transparent*, revealing the essential nature of what they denote, in which case their isolation from physical concepts is evidence of distinct properties after all.
