# Philosophy of Mind

Philosophy of mind asks how anything comes to think, feel, and experience — and whether whatever does so could be, without remainder, a physical thing. A neuroscientist can trace the path from retina to cortex, every step physical; yet nothing in the tracing says why there is something it is like to see red rather than the same discriminations running in the dark. This is Chalmers's hard problem, made vivid by Jackson's Mary: knowing every physical fact about color vision from her black-and-white room, she seems to learn something on first seeing a tomato. Physicalists reply that the apparent gap is a difference between concepts, not facts — or, for illusionists, an introspective illusion. Property dualists take it at face value; panpsychists and Russellian monists close it from below, locating experience in the intrinsic nature physics leaves unspecified.

A second puzzle is aboutness: beliefs and hopes are *of* things, including things that do not exist. Brentano made this intentionality the mark of the mental; the modern question is how a lump of matter gets it: causal and teleological theories ground content in what states track or were selected to indicate; critics press that tracking cannot underwrite *mis*representation — a frog's snap at a BB is an error only if the state already had a content to get wrong.

Framing both is the mind–body problem itself. Descartes's interactionism founders on how the immaterial could push matter; the identity theory, equating pain with a brain state, on multiple realizability — an octopus or a machine might hurt without our neurons. Functionalism, the current default, identifies mental states by their causal roles rather than their material — at the price of Searle's Chinese Room and the worry that roles leave out what it is like to have a mind. Minds also act on the world. What separates action from mere movement — Davidson's answer: causation by reasons — and whether mental causes can do real work in a physically closed world (Kim's exclusion argument) are the problems of agency.

The field articulates accordingly: consciousness, intentionality, and the metaphysics of mind are the core; perception is homed here; action faces outward to free will and ethics; emotion, self-knowledge, other minds, machine minds, and the foundations of cognitive science complete the ring. Free will and personal identity are homed in metaphysics, meaning in language, the empirical study of cognition in science — this division keeps what concerns the nature of the mental as such.

## Bridges

- To `metaphysics`: the mind–body problem is a metaphysical dispute; free will and personal identity are homed there but argued on this division's evidence.
- To `epistemology`: perception is homed here while its justificatory yield is treated there; the joint question is what experience can give belief.
- To `language` and `science`: theories of mental content share machinery with theories of linguistic meaning, and the foundations of cognitive science face both divisions at once.
- To `ethics` and the registers: action theory underwrites responsibility, machine minds press on moral status, and the problem-space runs from Descartes and Brentano through Ryle, Putnam, Davidson, and Chalmers.
