# Other Minds

You have met exactly one mind from the inside. Everyone else presents you with a surface — faces, words, winces, tears — and the conviction that something lies behind the surface is one you have never once verified and could not begin to. The problem of other minds is that this conviction, the most unshakable we have, seems to rest on the weakest evidence imaginable.

The classical response, given its canonical form by Mill, is the argument from analogy: in my own case, injury is followed by pain and pain by crying out; other bodies are like mine and cry out when injured; so probably they feel pain too. The objection is immediate and severe: this is an induction from a single case. A generalization confirmed once — and *necessarily* only once, since my own mind is the only one I can check — is barely a generalization at all. Worse, the conclusion is uncheckable in principle, so the inference can never be audited the way ordinary analogies can.

Wittgenstein redrew the problem rather than solving it. If the skeptic's picture were right — each of us naming private inner episodes and guessing at everyone else's — then, by the private-language argument, the words "pain" and "belief" could never have acquired meaning in the first place: a sign whose correct use only its owner could check has no correct use. On the criterial view that follows, pain-behavior is not fallible *evidence* for a hidden state but part of what fixes the concept of pain; the skeptic is not cautious but confused. The position's strength is that it explains why radical doubt about other minds feels idle; its cost is saying precisely how criteria can be conceptually tied to mind without collapsing into behaviorism.

The contemporary literature has largely traded the skeptical question for a descriptive one: not *whether* we know other minds but *how* we manage it so effortlessly. Theory-theory holds that we deploy a tacit folk psychology — a body of law-like generalizations linking situations, states, and behavior — and its best evidence is developmental: children's staged mastery of false belief looks like theory revision. Simulation theory replies that we need no theory because we have a working mind to run offline: we put ourselves in the other's place and read our own output; mirror-neuron findings gave the view an empirical second wind. Phenomenological and interactionist accounts (Scheler's descendants: Gallagher, Zahavi) reject the shared assumption that minds are hidden at all — in the live encounter we *perceive* anger in the face and intention in the reaching hand, and mindreading is the special case, not the rule.

The problem's serious modern form is distributional. Granting that other humans have minds, where does mind run out — great apes, octopuses, bees, fish larvae? Here Nagel's question of what it is like meets the methodological one of which behavioral and neural markers can carry evidential weight across radically different architectures, with animal welfare hanging on the answer. And its newest form is artificial: systems that pass every conversational criterion while sharing neither our biology nor our history force the question of whether criteria, analogy, or perception ever reached minds at all — or only creatures relevantly like us.
