# The Self and Self-Knowledge

The founding datum is a failure. Hume went looking for his self and reported only traffic: "some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred" — never the something that *has* them. Yet nothing seems better known than oneself. Every experience arrives already stamped as mine; no one wakes wondering *whose* headache this is. The problem of the self is to say what, if anything, answers to that stamp; the problem of self-knowledge is to explain why my access to my own mind seems so unlike my access to yours — direct, authoritative, not based on evidence — and how far that seeming survives scrutiny.

On the first question, two conceptions divide the field short of full metaphysics (persistence over time is homed at `metaphysics.personal-identity`). The *minimal* self of the phenomenologists (Zahavi) is nothing over and above the first-personal givenness of experience — the for-me-ness Hume's search could not find precisely because it is the searchlight, not an object in the beam. The *narrative* self (Ricoeur, Dennett's "center of narrative gravity," Schechtman) is a construction: the protagonist of the self-story by which a life's actions and commitments hang together. Each is strongest where the other is silent — the minimal self explains why even the amnesiac's experience is still *someone's*; the narrative self explains identity crises, which no bare subject could suffer. Galen Strawson presses that narrativity is one temperament universalized: some lives are lived episodically, and none the worse.

Whatever the self is, thought about it is special. Perry's shopper trails sugar around the supermarket, believing *someone* is making a mess; everything changes when he realizes *I am the one*. No objective description seems to capture that de se realization — a perfect map still needs the arrow saying *you are here*. The semantics of such self-locating attitudes is homed at `language.attitudes`; here the moral is that the first person is not one more name among others.

Self-knowledge divides its theorists by where they locate the authority. Inner-sense models (Locke, Armstrong) posit a quasi-perceptual scanner directed at one's own states — mechanistically sober, and comfortable with error, but authority becomes a merely reliable instrument's. Rationalist or agentialist accounts (Moran) relocate it: I know what I believe not by observing myself but by *making up my mind* — authority through commitment, the difference between reporting an inner object and being answerable for a stance. Evans's transparency method is the working procedure: one answers "do I believe p?" by answering "p?" — attending to the world, not the mind. Expressivists (in Wittgenstein's wake, Bar-On) hold that avowals express states rather than describe them, which is why "I am in pain" is odd to challenge. Each account fits some states beautifully and others badly: transparency works for belief and falters for wishes, moods, and standing prejudice.

The limits are empirical as well as philosophical. Confabulation studies (Nisbett and Wilson; split-brain patients fluently justifying choices made for undisclosed reasons) suggest much "introspection" is theorizing about oneself; Schwitzgebel argues introspection is untrustworthy even about current experience; Carruthers concludes we know our own minds by the same interpretive faculty we turn on others. And beneath everything sits the no-self option: Hume's bundle, and the Buddhist analysis as its worked-out rival — the person as a designation upon causally linked aggregates, with two millennia of debate (reflexive-awareness theorists against their critics) over whether even minimal for-me-ness survives the reduction.
