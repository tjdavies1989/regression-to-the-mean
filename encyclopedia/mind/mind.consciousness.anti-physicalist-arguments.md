# The Knowledge and Conceivability Arguments

The modern arguments descend from Kripke. If "pain" and "C-fiber firing" are rigid designators, their identity, if true, is necessary; yet it seems contingent. For "water is H₂O" the seeming is explained away: someone could be in an epistemic situation qualitatively just like ours while the watery stuff was XYZ, so what we imagine is not water failing to be H₂O but something else. Kripke's point is that this manoeuvre is unavailable for pain, because pain is picked out by how it feels, and to be in an epistemic situation qualitatively like being in pain *is* to be in pain. The appearance of contingency cannot be reassigned to a stand-in, so it must be taken at face value, and the identity fails. Every later argument presses this asymmetry.

Jackson's Mary (1982, 1986) presses it epistemically. Confined to a black-and-white room, she learns every physical fact about color vision; released, she learns what red looks like. Formalized: she knew all the physical facts; she learned a fact; so some fact is not physical. Jackson's later recantation (1998, 2003) belongs to the argument's literature. Two pressures moved him: his original paper had made qualia epiphenomenal, and an epiphenomenal quality could not be the cause of anyone's knowledge of it, including Mary's; and representationalism about experience persuaded him that the sense of a left-over "feel" is an artifact of how experience represents, so that Mary's gain is real but not a gain in facts.

Chalmers's zombie argument (1996, 2010) gives the conceivability step its most careful form. Let P be the complete physical truth and Q a phenomenal truth. P&¬Q is ideally *primarily* conceivable — no amount of a priori reflection reveals a contradiction in it; ideal primary conceivability entails primary possibility; and because phenomenal concepts have coinciding primary and secondary intensions, primary possibility here transfers to the secondary, metaphysical possibility that physicalism denies. The Kripkean cases are absorbed, not evaded: "water ≠ H₂O" is primarily but not secondarily possible, and nothing comparable explains the zombie case away. The single exit Chalmers leaves is Russellian monism — if P is silent about the intrinsic nature of the physical, a zombie world may differ from ours in that nature rather than in laws — which he treats as the physicalist's least bad option.

Levine's explanatory gap (1983, 2001) is deliberately weaker in conclusion. Water's macro-behaviour is deducible from its microstructure once "water" is functionally characterized, so the identity explains; pain has no functional characterization that qualia satisfy, so "pain is C-fiber firing" leaves a why-question the water case does not. Levine refuses the step to dualism — he is a materialist who holds the gap to be an unsolved problem for materialism — and his version owes least to modal epistemology.

The replies attack different joints. Lewis and Nemirow's ability hypothesis grants that Mary gains something and denies it is information: she acquires abilities to remember, imagine, and recognize red. Lewis's supporting argument is the strongest in the debate. Information eliminates possibilities; Mary had already eliminated every possibility inconsistent with the physical facts; so unless there were non-physical possibilities left to eliminate, she gained none — and assuming there were is assuming the conclusion. The objection is that Mary can now wonder whether *this* is what others see, which looks propositional. Conee's acquaintance hypothesis offers a third category: knowledge by acquaintance is maximally direct contact with a quality, neither factual nor practical, which explains why no book could deliver it and why an imagining-impaired Mary would still acquire it.

Loar's type-B physicalism keeps everything the arguments say about concepts and denies it about properties. Phenomenal concepts are recognitional, referring to physical properties directly rather than through a contingent mode of presentation; so their a priori independence from physical concepts shows nothing about the properties. Zombies are conceivable and impossible. The metaphysical cost is to affirm "strong necessities" — necessary truths opaque even to ideal reflection and unexplained by two-dimensional structure — which Chalmers argues occur nowhere else and type-B must posit for this case alone. Whether phenomenal concepts can explain the appearance of a gap without themselves reopening it is prosecuted at the sibling `phenomenal-concepts`.

The dependence on `metaphysics.modality.modal-epistemology` is thus exact: Kripke's argument, the zombie argument, and Lewis's reconstruction of Mary each turn on a licence to read possibility off conceivability, and type-B physicalism is precisely the denial of that licence, priced as strong necessity. Levine's gap survives the licence's revocation; that is both its safety and its inability to conclude anything about what exists.
