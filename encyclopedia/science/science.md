# Philosophy of Science

Science is the institution we trust most and understand least. Its predictions land spacecraft on comets; its theories are revised, sometimes discarded wholesale, within generations. Philosophy of science asks how both facts can be true at once — what makes scientific reasoning good, and what its success entitles us to believe about the world.

Start with the reasoning. Every scientific inference runs from finite observations to claims that outstrip them: universal laws, future predictions, unobservable mechanisms. Hume's problem of induction says no such inference can be certified without circularity — any argument that the future will resemble the past already assumes it. Popper's response was to deny that science inducts at all: theories are bold conjectures, and evidence can only refute, never confirm. Most of the field instead tried to say how confirmation works, and found it hard: Hempel's ravens paradox makes a white shoe confirm "all ravens are black," and Goodman's "grue" shows the same evidence confirming incompatible hypotheses unless some predicates are privileged. Bayesian confirmation theory, now the leading framework, models evidence as probability raised, at the price of disputes over the priors.

Then the metaphysics of success. Scientific realists argue that the reliability of mature theories would be a miracle unless they were approximately true — electrons exist, roughly as physics describes them. Antirealists answer with the pessimistic induction: the graveyard of once-successful theories (phlogiston, caloric, the ether) suggests ours will join them. Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism splits the difference: accept theories as right about observables while withholding belief about the rest. Underdetermination sharpens the standoff: if incompatible theories can fit all possible evidence, evidence cannot decide between them.

Science does not just predict; it explains — but what is explanation? Hempel's covering-law model (to explain is to derive from laws) faces stubborn counterexamples: a flagpole's shadow is derivable from its height and vice versa, but only one derivation explains. Causal, unificationist, and pragmatic accounts compete to say what it missed. The laws themselves divide Humeans, for whom laws just summarize what happens, from necessitarians, for whom laws govern. And Kuhn's historical challenge cuts across all of this: if science moves by revolutions between incommensurable paradigms, theory choice may owe as much to community dynamics as to evidence.

The field articulates twice over: general philosophy of science (confirmation, explanation, laws, realism, models, theory change) and the philosophies of the special sciences, where physics, biology, and the social sciences each generate problems no general account settles. Its borders: confirmation shades into epistemology's formal wing; laws and causation are shared with metaphysics; the cognitive sciences are homed in philosophy of mind.

## Bridges

- To `epistemology`: the problem of induction is jointly claimed, and confirmation theory shares its Bayesian machinery with formal epistemology.
- To `metaphysics`: laws, causation, and natural kinds are homed there but load-bearing here; the realism debate is metaphysics conducted on scientific evidence.
- To `logic`: the structure of theories, probability, and the formal apparatus of confirmation.
- To the registers: Hume, Mill, Popper, and Kuhn among `figures`; the Vienna Circle in `traditions` is the field's modern point of origin.
