# Scientific Realism and Antirealism

No one has ever seen an electron. Yet physics describes its mass, charge, and spin to a dozen decimal places, and devices built on those descriptions work. The realism debate asks what such success entitles us to believe: is a good theory a true (or approximately true) description of a hidden reality, or only a reliable instrument for prediction? The question is not idle skepticism — both sides accept the science — but a dispute about what accepting a theory *means*.

Two arguments drive the field. The no-miracles argument, made canonical by Putnam, holds that realism is the only philosophy that does not make the success of science a miracle: if mature theories were not latching onto the world, their novel predictions — Fresnel's wave optics predicting a bright spot at the center of a disk's shadow — would be staggering coincidences. Against it stands Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction: the history of science is a graveyard of theories that were successful by any standard and are now judged not even approximately true — phlogiston chemistry, caloric heat, the optical ether. Induction over that record suggests our best current theories will join them. Each engine is strongest exactly where the other is weak: no-miracles owns the present, the meta-induction owns the past. Stanford's problem of unconceived alternatives sharpens the pessimism: scientists repeatedly failed to imagine the rivals that later displaced their theories, so our confidence that none exists now is worth little.

Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism is the debate's pivotal antirealism. Theories are read literally — electron-talk is genuinely about electrons, not code for meter readings — but acceptance requires believing only that a theory is *empirically adequate*: right about all observable things, where "observable" means detectable by unaided human senses. The moons of Jupiter are observable; microbes and quarks are not. The view's strength is disciplined modesty: it never believes more than the evidence forces. Its standing objection is the line itself — why should the accidents of human physiology mark an epistemic boundary, when the microscope-to-telescope continuum shows no natural joint?

The most active current positions try to keep what is right in both engines. Structural realism, revived by Worrall, notices that what survives theory change is mathematical structure: Fresnel's equations passed intact into Maxwell's theory while the ether they were "about" vanished. Believe the structure, not the natures — the retention answers the meta-induction while preserving the no-miracles insight. Ladyman and French push the epistemic version to an ontic one: there are no natures behind the structure; structure is what there is. Selective (or deployment) realism — Kitcher, Psillos — instead reads the historical record more finely: credit accrues only to the *working posits* that actually generated a theory's novel successes, and the dead theories' falsehoods turn out to be idle parts. Hacking's entity realism cuts across the theory-centered debate entirely: when experimenters use electrons as tools to investigate something else — "if you can spray them, they are real" — manipulation, not theoretical success, grounds belief in entities, even amid agnosticism about the theories describing them. The field's drift is accordingly from wholesale verdicts on science toward retail arguments over particular posits and episodes.

## Children

- `no-miracles` — **The No-Miracles Argument** — the success-to-truth inference, the role of novel prediction, and the base-rate and circularity objections. *(standard)*
- `pessimistic-induction` — **The Pessimistic Meta-Induction** — Laudan's historical challenge, realist rebuttals, and Stanford's unconceived alternatives as its contemporary sharpening. *(standard)*
- `underdetermination` — **Underdetermination of Theory by Evidence** — Duhem–Quine holism, empirical equivalence, and whether rivals fitting all possible data are more than shadows. *(standard)*
- `constructive-empiricism` — **Constructive Empiricism** — van Fraassen's empirical adequacy, the observable/unobservable line, and epistemic voluntarism. *(standard)*
- `structural-realism` — **Structural Realism** — Worrall's epistemic and Ladyman–French's ontic versions, and the Newman objection. *(anchor)*
- `selective-realism` — **Selective and Deployment Realism** — working versus idle posits, and the case-by-case reading of theory change. *(standard)*
- `entity-realism` — **Entity Realism** — Hacking and Cartwright on manipulation as the ground of belief in entities without theories. *(satellite)*

## Bridges

- To `metaphysics`: the debate is metaphysics conducted on scientific evidence, and ontic structural realism is a metaphysical thesis outright.
- To `epistemology`: the no-miracles argument stands or falls with inference to the best explanation, and van Fraassen's voluntarism is an epistemology of belief and acceptance.
- To `language`: whether "electron" in 1900 and 2026 refers to the same thing turns on theories of reference — causal theories are the selective realist's chief tool against the meta-induction.
- To `science.theory-change`: the historical record that Kuhn made central is the meta-induction's evidence base; what survives revolutions decides which realism is tenable.
