# Values in Science

A regulator must decide whether a chemical causes cancer. The evidence is suggestive but incomplete; demanding more certainty risks poisoning people, demanding less risks banning a harmless product. Where should the threshold sit — and is fixing it a scientific judgment or a moral one? The problem of this node is whether science can, and should, be free of value judgments: not in choosing what to study or how to apply results, where everyone concedes values operate, but in the core business of weighing evidence and accepting conclusions.

The value-free ideal deserves its fair statement, because it is not naive. On this view, social and moral values legitimately govern the *external* phases of science — problem selection, ethical constraints on method, decisions about application — but must be excluded from the *internal* phase of evidential assessment, precisely because science's public authority depends on its conclusions not being anyone's preferences in disguise. A science that let wishes weigh evidence would be Lysenkoism; the firewall protects both science's reliability and the public's ability to trust it.

The inductive risk argument attacks the firewall at its strongest point. Rudner argued that accepting or rejecting a hypothesis requires deciding whether the evidence is *sufficient*, and since no evidence is ever logically sufficient, that decision must weigh the consequences of being wrong — consequences that are often moral. Douglas extended the argument inward: choices about statistical significance, how to characterize ambiguous data (is this slide a tumor?), and which errors to risk pervade methodology itself, so value judgments enter the core, not just the borders. The best reply, due to Jeffrey, is that scientists need not accept hypotheses at all — they should report probabilities and leave acceptance to decision-makers. Douglas's counter: the value-laden choices arise upstream of any final probability report, in the making of the evidence itself.

Underdetermination opens a second doorway. If evidence never fixes theory choice by itself, something fills the gap, and Longino's contextual empiricism argues that background assumptions — often carrying social content — do the filling, ineliminably. Her constructive move relocates objectivity from individual neutrality to social organization: a community is objective to the degree that it sustains criticism from diverse perspectives with real uptake. Feminist philosophy of science supplies the case studies that give the view its bite — androcentric assumptions in primatology and human evolution that went unnoticed until the community's composition changed — and its point is not that values corrupted the science but that *different* values exposed the corruption.

A standard containment strategy distinguishes epistemic values (predictive accuracy, scope, consistency, fruitfulness — Kuhn's list) from non-epistemic ones, permitting the former everywhere and the latter nowhere internal. The distinction is real but unstable: simplicity and fruitfulness are contested guides to truth, the classification of a given consideration shifts with context, and critics argue the boundary itself is drawn differently by differently situated inquirers.

The debate has an institutional face. Science advising forces the question of whether experts should report findings neutrally or weigh policy consequences, and public trust in science arguably depends on managing value influence transparently rather than denying it. Meanwhile industry funding gives the problem teeth: the well-documented funding effect — sponsored trials favoring sponsors — and the deliberate manufacture of doubt show that values already operate in actual science, and the live question is which arrangements distinguish legitimate influence from corruption.
