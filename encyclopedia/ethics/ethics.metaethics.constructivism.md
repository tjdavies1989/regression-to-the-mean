# Constructivism

Constructivism is the attempt to have objectivity without discovery. Moral truths are neither found in the world nor projected onto it; they are what would be endorsed from a privileged practical standpoint, and their authority derives from the standpoint's being one no reasoner can escape. The view thus promises the realist's mind-independence in one sense — no individual's attitudes make a judgment true — while conceding the anti-realist's point that nothing in the inventory of the world answers to moral predicates. Everything turns on how the standpoint is characterized and how much it can be shown to contain.

**Kantian constructivism.** Rawls's Dewey Lectures (1980) introduced the term for a method: principles of justice are whatever free and equal persons would agree to under fair conditions, and the conditions model a conception of the person rather than track a prior moral order. Rawls later retreated to a *political* constructivism confined to the public culture of a liberal democracy, leaving it to others to make the method a metaethics. Korsgaard did. *The Sources of Normativity* (1996) locates obligation in reflective endorsement: a reflective creature cannot act without taking some description of itself — a *practical identity* — as the ground of its reasons, and since every particular identity is contingent, the one that cannot be shed is one's identity as a reflective agent as such, which commits one to valuing humanity. The argument's strength is its route from the mere structure of agency to a substantive result; its standing difficulty is the regress objection (Cohen, Nagel, in the same volume): if every particular identity is optional, nothing stops the reflective agent from treating humanity as optional too, unless valuing it is already forced — in which case the constructive machinery adds nothing to a realism about that value.

**Humean constructivism.** Street's version (2008, 2010) keeps the standpoint and abandons the Kantian claim that it contains anything determinate. Normative truth is a function of what withstands scrutiny from within an agent's own evaluative attitudes; the standpoint is *practical* but its contents are given, not derived. An ideally coherent Caligula, whose valuations survive full reflection, is not making a mistake. Street's positive case is that this is the only position left standing once her debunking argument has undermined realism and once the Kantian derivation of substantive value from bare agency has failed. Its cost is relativism at the foundation, cushioned by the empirical claim that actual human evaluative starting points overlap enough for shared morality to be robustly constructible.

**Constitutivism.** The Kantian side has been reformulated as the thesis that normative standards are constitutive of action: to act at all is to aim at self-constitution (Korsgaard 2009), self-understanding (Velleman), or power (Katsafanas), and standards internal to that aim bind any agent without appeal to external facts. Enoch's *shmagency* objection (2006) is the principal reply: constitutive aims deliver at best hypothetical authority, since one can ask why be an agent rather than a *shmagent* — something that does everything an agent does minus the constitutive aim. Answers divide between denying that the question can be coherently asked, since asking is already acting (Ferrero's inescapability reply), and conceding that constitutive standards bind only those who care about them while arguing that everyone in fact does (Katsafanas, Smith). Enoch's counter is that inescapability shows only that one cannot leave the game, not that its rules matter.

**Metaethics or normative theory?** A recurring question is whether constructivism is a rival to realism and expressivism at all. Its metaethical form must say what makes a judgment true — endorsement from the standpoint — and then answer whether the standpoint's authority is itself constructed or given. If given, the view collapses into realism about the standpoint (Hussain and Shah's charge against Korsgaard; Enoch's against Rawls); if constructed, the regress reopens. Street's reply distinguishes *restricted* constructivism, which takes some normative truths as inputs and is indeed a first-order theory, from *thoroughgoing* constructivism, which constructs everything from the bare attitude of valuing and is therefore a genuine metaethics. Whether the latter can be stated without a Frege–Geach-style problem of its own — what does it mean to say an attitude *withstands* scrutiny, if scrutiny's standards are themselves attitudes? — is where the debate presently stands.
