# Value Theory

Before asking what we ought to do, there is a prior question: what is worth having at all? Money is good for what it buys, health for what it lets us do — but the chain of "good for the sake of" must end somewhere, in things good in themselves. Value theory asks what those things are and whether they form one kind or many.

The oldest battleground is well-being: what makes a life go well *for the one living it*. Hedonism gives the cleanest answer — pleasure, and only pleasure — and its strength is that everything else on any rival list seems to matter partly because of how it feels. Its crisis is the experience machine, already met in this division's root: if a perfectly simulated life of bliss is worth refusing, we value contact with reality itself, not just its felt quality. Desire-satisfaction theories take the hint: a life goes well when the person gets what she actually wants, which explains why well-being differs across people and why paternalism grates. But actual desires can be ignorant, manipulated, or pathological, so the theory idealizes — what you *would* want if fully informed and rational — and each idealization drains the theory's original appeal, until the "you" whose desires count is no longer recognizably you. Objective-list theories bite the other bullet: knowledge, achievement, friendship, and autonomy benefit a person whether or not she wants them. Their strength is fidelity to what we actually mourn in a wasted life; their standing charge is elitism — who wrote the list, and why does it look like a philosophy professor's weekend? A further question shadows all three: does well-being exhaust value? If the last sentient creature painlessly destroyed a beautiful world on its way out, would nothing bad have happened? Those who hesitate suspect there is impersonal value — goodness *simpliciter*, not goodness for anyone.

Moore made that impersonal notion central: intrinsic value depends only on a thing's intrinsic nature, tested by asking whether it would be good existing in absolute isolation. His doctrine of organic unities adds that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of its parts — pleasure at another's suffering may be worse than the suffering alone. The fitting-attitudes tradition analyzes value the other way around: to be good is to be a fitting object of favor — desirable is what it is *fitting* to desire. Scanlon's buck-passing version says goodness is not itself a reason-giving property; it is the formal fact that a thing's other properties give reasons to favor it. The analysis's strength is economy — one normative primitive, reasons, instead of two. Its notorious obstacle is the wrong kind of reasons: if a demon will torture you unless you admire him, you have compelling reason to admire, yet the demon is not admirable. Distinguishing object-given from state-given reasons without circularity remains the live task.

Finally, structure. Monists hold that all value reduces to one thing and hard choices are merely hard sums; pluralists, following Berlin and Stocker, hold that liberty and equality, or friendship and achievement, are irreducibly distinct — which threatens rational choice between them. Chang's response is that incomparability is rarer than it looks: two careers may be neither better, worse, nor equal, yet still *on a par* — comparable under a fourth relation that keeps choice rational without a common scale.
