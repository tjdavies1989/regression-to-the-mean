# Future People and Population Ethics

Nearly everyone affected by today's choices does not yet exist. Energy policy, resource depletion, genetic selection, the running of any existential risk — these will shape billions of lives, none of which can vote, complain, or consent. Common sense says we owe those lives something. The field's founding discovery, Parfit's **non-identity problem**, is that the obvious way of cashing this out collapses. Large-scale policies alter who meets whom and when children are conceived, so they change *which people* come to exist. Choose depletion over conservation and the people alive in two centuries will have harder lives than the conservation-people would have had — but they are not the conservation-people. Given lives still worth living, no one is worse off than they would otherwise have been; there is no victim. Yet the verdict that depletion is therefore permissible is one almost no one can swallow. Something is wrong, and nobody is wronged: that is the depth of the problem, and every position below is a way of paying for an answer.

The impersonal route says outcomes can be worse without being worse *for* anyone, and asks which populations are better than which — the question of **axiology**. The total view (more well-being is better, whoever has it) handles non-identity cleanly but yields the **repugnant conclusion**: for any world of ten billion excellent lives, some vastly larger world of lives barely worth living is better. The average view escapes it but implies that adding good lives can make things worse and prefers tiny elite populations. Critical-level views (Blackorby, Bossert, Donaldson) count only well-being above a threshold, and incur the sadistic conclusion — adding a few miserable lives can beat adding many modestly good ones. Variable-value views (Hurka, Ng) let extra lives matter less as numbers grow, and inherit relatives of both failures. **Arrhenius's impossibility theorems** turn this pattern into a result: no axiology satisfies a short list of individually compelling adequacy conditions. Every option pays; the live debate is over which price is least ruinous — including Parfit's own late hope for, and doubt about, "Theory X."

**Person-affecting views** refuse the impersonal turn: an act is wrong only if it is bad for someone. Their strength is fidelity to morality's complaint-based structure, and to the **procreation asymmetry** — the firm intuition that we are obligated not to create a miserable child but not obligated to create a happy one. Their weakness is that non-identity was built to defeat them, and consistent versions drift toward indifference about the future or toward Benatar's anti-natalism. **Discounting** is the economists' entry: climate models discount future well-being at compound rates, so the choice between Stern's near-zero and Nordhaus's market-derived rate does more work than any explicit moral premise — while philosophers since Ramsey have called pure time preference ethically indefensible. **Longtermism** (Bostrom, Ord, Greaves and MacAskill) draws the applied moral: if future people count fully and the possible future is vast, reducing extinction risk dominates most other causes — an argument whose strength is bare arithmetic plus impartiality, and whose critics answer with cluelessness, fanaticism, and person-affecting resistance. Climate ethics is where all of this stops being hypothetical: an intergenerational, non-identity-riddled, discount-sensitive problem argued jointly with `applied-ethics` and `politics`.

## Children

- `non-identity` — **The Non-Identity Problem** — no victim, yet a wrong: Parfit's argument, the biting-the-bullet, rights-based, and impersonal responses. *(standard)*
- `axiology` — **Population Axiology** — total, average, critical-level, and variable-value views; the repugnant conclusion; Arrhenius's impossibility theorems. *(standard)*
- `person-affecting` — **Person-Affecting Views and the Asymmetry** — morality as complaint: the procreation asymmetry, Narveson's slogan, anti-natalism. *(standard)*
- `discounting` — **Discounting the Future** — pure time preference from Ramsey to Stern–Nordhaus, and whether distance in time can matter morally. *(satellite)*
- `longtermism` — **Longtermism and Existential Risk** — the vast-future argument, its expected-value engine, and the cluelessness and fanaticism objections. *(standard)*
