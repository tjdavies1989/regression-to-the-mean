# Moral Relativism

The word covers three claims that are routinely run together and stand or fall separately. *Descriptive* relativism is the anthropological thesis that moral codes differ across societies in ways that survive correction for differing beliefs about non-moral facts; it is empirical and contested, since much apparent variation — in the treatment of the aged, say — dissolves once beliefs about the afterlife or the economy are factored in. *Metaethical* relativism holds that moral judgments are true or false only relative to a framework — a culture, a set of standards, an agreement — and that no framework is uniquely correct. *Normative* relativism prescribes: since frameworks are all there is, one ought not to judge or interfere with practices another framework sanctions. The distinctions matter because the step from the first claim to the second is a substantive argument, not a truism, and the step from the second to the third is, as will be seen, invalid.

The best-worked metaethical relativism is Gilbert Harman's (1975). Harman does not claim that "wrong" means "wrong by my society's lights." He distinguishes *inner judgments* — "you ought not to have done that," which presuppose that the agent shares reasons the speaker can appeal to — from external evaluations, and argues that inner judgments are true only relative to a motivating agreement among those who share it. Hitler is not, in the inner sense, someone who ought not to have done what he did, because no agreement between him and us gives the judgment purchase; he is simply evil, an external verdict. The view is offered as the best explanation of a linguistic pattern, and its semantic machinery belongs to `moral-semantics`. What it gives this node is a relativism that is naturalistic — moral facts are facts about agreements — and that turns the argument from disagreement into an inference to the best explanation rather than a slogan.

David Wong's *pluralistic relativism* (*Natural Moralities*, 2006) is the other developed position. Morality, on Wong's account, is a human invention answering to universal needs: regulating conflict, sustaining cooperation, ordering the individual's own ends. Those functions constrain what can count as a morality — a code of pure cruelty is not a rival morality but a failure at being one — but they underdetermine it. Within the constraints several adequate moralities exist, differing in how they weigh community against autonomy or relationship against right; the Confucian and the liberal are not garbled versions of each other. Wong's relativism is therefore bounded: not anything goes, but more than one thing does. Its strength is that it explains both the universals the anthropologists find and the residual variation the universals leave over.

The argument from disagreement is relativism's standing evidence, and its weight is the live question. Mackie's "argument from relativity" reasons that moral variation is better explained by differing ways of life than by differing perceptions of an objective moral order — but Mackie drew error theory from it, not relativism, which shows the argument underdetermines its conclusion. Realists counter that disagreement is compatible with objectivity if some parties are mistaken, that much of it is factual disagreement in disguise, and that what matters is convergence under idealized conditions (Brink, Smith). Relativists reply with cases where fully informed, rational parties still diverge — the Moral Twin Earth structure, or the *fundamental* disagreements Doris and Plakias press from the empirical literature — and argue that the persistence of such cases is exactly what a framework-relative account predicts and a realist must explain away. The recent literature (Enoch, Rowland) reframes the issue as an instance of peer disagreement: whether moral disagreement counts as evidence against objectivity depends on what rival explanations are available and on how moral error rates compare with those in domains where nobody turns relativist.

Two objections recur. The charge of *self-refutation* runs: if all moral claims are relative, so is the claim that we ought to respect others' frameworks, and the statement of relativism itself either enjoys a non-relative status the theory denies or has no authority over those whose framework rejects it. The relativist's reply separates the metaethical thesis — a second-order claim about truth conditions, not itself a moral claim — from any normative rider; only the rider is threatened. The *paradox of tolerance* is the rider's own trouble: normative relativism enjoins tolerance as a universal duty, and a universal duty is what relativism says there cannot be. Harman and Wong both deny the inference from metaethical to normative relativism; Wong instead derives a limited value of accommodation from the functional need to live alongside those one cannot convert. The upshot is that the relativism worth arguing about has no political payoff: it changes what moral disagreement *is* without telling either side what to do about it.
