# Deontology

A surgeon could harvest one healthy patient's organs to save five. Almost no one thinks she may. Deontology is the family of theories that takes this verdict at face value: some acts are forbidden whatever they would bring about, because morality consists first of duties and constraints binding on the agent, not of goals to be promoted. The pull of the view is that it matches the phenomenology of obligation — a promise binds *me*, here, to *you*, and the fact that breaking it would let three other promises be kept elsewhere feels beside the point. Its burden is to explain why.

The Kantian core answers: because rational agency itself legislates. Act only on maxims you could will as universal law; treat humanity, in yourself or another, never merely as a means but always also as an end. The universalizability test grounds constraint in consistency rather than in a table of goods; the humanity formula explains what is wrong with the surgeon's cut — the patient is used, his agency bypassed — without mentioning outcomes at all. Korsgaard reads Kant constructively: obligation issues from the practical identity an agent cannot coherently repudiate, so morality's authority is built by reflection rather than found. O'Neill's reading centers what maxims all could act on together, making Kantian ethics a discipline of principles for finite, plural agents. Ross's moderate classic drops the single supreme principle: we face several *prima facie* duties — fidelity, non-maleficence, gratitude, justice — none absolute, conflicts settled by judgment. Its strength is fidelity to moral experience; its cost is that it explains the duties' authority by intuition alone.

The modern literature sharpens the structure. Constraints are *agent-relative*: my duty not to kill is not a duty to minimize killings. Hence the paradox of deontology, pressed by Scheffler: if violating a constraint is so terrible, why may I not violate it once to prevent five violations by others? Nagel locates the answer in the deliberative standpoint — in aiming at a victim's harm, I would be guided by evil, not merely foreseeing it — while Scheffler himself defends agent-centered options (permission not to maximize the good) as better motivated than constraints. That distinction between intending and foreseeing harm is the doctrine of double effect, and the trolley literature founded by Foot and elaborated by Thomson is its laboratory: diverting the trolley and pushing the man off the footbridge yield identical outcomes, so whatever separates them — intention, using-as-means, the redirection of an existing threat — is the shape of the constraint itself. Two systematic engines carry the view today. Scanlon's contractualism makes an act wrong if any principle permitting it could be reasonably rejected — deriving constraints from what we owe to each other, one justifiable-to-each person at a time, rather than from aggregated good. Nozick casts constraints as side-constraints on action, rights that fence each person off from use for others' benefit; the inviolability of persons, not the badness of violations, does the work.

What is strongest across the family is a shared thought: persons have a status — inviolability, a claim to justification — that no sum of benefits can override. What remains contested is whether that status can be articulated without either collapsing into rule-consequentialism or resting finally on the intuitions it was meant to vindicate.
