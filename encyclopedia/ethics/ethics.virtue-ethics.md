# Virtue Ethics

Ask what you should *do* and modern moral theory hands you a principle. Ask what kind of person you should *be* — whom you would trust with your child, whom you hope to resemble at seventy — and the principles fall oddly silent. Virtue ethics takes the second question as primary: character, not act-evaluation, is ethics' basic subject, and the concepts that carry real weight are thick ones — courageous, honest, callous — not the thin pair right and wrong. The revival has a founding document: Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958), which argued that the juridical vocabulary of obligation is the orphaned survival of a divine-law framework no longer believed, and that ethics should fall silent until it recovers an adequate philosophy of psychology — of intention, wanting, flourishing. The field that grew from that indictment went back to Aristotle: virtues as stable dispositions of feeling and choice, acquired by habituation, unified in *eudaimonia*, a whole life lived well.

Its dominant contemporary form is neo-Aristotelian naturalism. Foot's *Natural Goodness* argues that moral evaluation shares its logical grammar with the evaluation of living things as such: as an oak has deep roots or a wolf hunts with the pack, a human being reasons practically well or badly, and vice is a natural defect. Hursthouse develops the framework's account of right action: an action is right if it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances. Critics press the guidance objection — this is circular or empty for the non-virtuous deliberator who most needs help — and the naturalists answer that the objection assumes ethics owes us a decision procedure, which no serious theory delivers; the *v-rules* (be honest, be just) guide as well as any principle, and what settles hard cases is *phronesis*, practical wisdom, the master virtue that perceives what matters in the particular situation and without which each virtue miscarries. The strongest thing in the view is its moral psychology: it alone explains why the continent person, who does the right thing against inclination, stands below the virtuous one, who does it wholeheartedly. Slote's agent-based alternative cuts the naturalist anchor entirely: rightness derives directly from admirable motives — care, benevolence — not from any prior account of flourishing, at the price of making an act's status hostage to its agent's inner life.

The sharpest external challenge is empirical. Doris and Harman marshal situationist social psychology — mood effects, the Milgram results, the dime in the phone booth — to argue that broad, stable character traits are largely illusory; conduct tracks situational variables the agent never notices. Responses: the experiments test one-off behavior, not the cross-situational consistency of *practical reasoning* that virtue theory posits; virtue was always held to be rare, an achievement rather than a default; and the data show individual differences the situationist reading suppresses. The debate permanently raised the field's empirical stakes.

Virtue ethics alone in the normative triad has a fully independent non-Western counterpart. The Confucian tradition — Confucius, Mencius, Xunzi — built a complete ethics of character around *ren* (humaneness), ritual propriety as habituation, moral exemplars, and the Mencius–Xunzi dispute over whether human nature is good: no anticipation of Aristotle but a parallel discovery, homed in the Chinese tradition on its own terms and now a full partner in the contemporary literature.

- `practical-wisdom` — **Practical Wisdom** — phronesis as the master virtue: moral perception, the unity of the virtues, and skill models of ethical expertise. *(standard)*

- `the-virtues` — **The Individual Virtues** — courage, honesty, justice, humility: the working analyses of particular traits and their conflicts. *(standard)*
