# Moral Realism

Moral realism conjoins three claims: moral sentences are truth-apt, some are true, and what makes them true is independent of anyone's attitudes toward them. The parent article set the view against its rivals; this entry maps the realists' quarrel with one another, which turns on one question — are moral facts natural facts? — and on the two challenges every answer must survive.

**Non-naturalism.** Moore's open question argument founds the position: for any natural property N offered as the analysis of goodness, a competent speaker can ask "this is N, but is it good?" without confusion, which a correct analysis should not permit. The argument was long thought to beg the question against synthetic identities — "this is H₂O, but is it water?" was once open too — and its heirs have reshaped it rather than abandoned it. Enoch's *robust realism* argues from deliberative indispensability: one cannot deliberate without treating some considerations as reasons that hold whatever one's attitudes, and what is indispensable to a rationally non-optional project earns ontological commitment on the terms science's posits do. Parfit keeps irreducibly normative truths but denies them ontological weight: they exist in a "non-ontological" sense, as numbers do, so the queerness objection has nothing to strike. Scanlon's *reasons fundamentalism* deflates furthest: the reason relation is primitive, claims about it are settled by the normative domain's own standards, and the demand for external validation misunderstands what a domain is. The three share a diagnosis of naturalism — it changes the subject — and disagree over how much metaphysics a realist must buy.

**Naturalist realism.** Cornell realism (Boyd, Sturgeon, Brink) holds that moral properties are natural in the way biological or economic properties are: higher-order, multiply realized, causally efficacious, and picked out by terms whose reference is fixed causally rather than by analytic definition — which is exactly why Moore's question stays open while showing nothing. Sturgeon's argument from moral explanation is the school's flagship: Hitler's depravity explains his deeds as well as any psychological redescription does, and a property that carries explanatory weight is real. Railton's reductive naturalism defines an agent's good as what a fully informed idealization of her would want her actual self to want, and rightness as what would be approved from a social standpoint impartial across such goods; the definitions are reforming, not lexical, and earn their keep by explanatory power. Jackson's analytic functionalism recovers the definition Moore denied: collect the platitudes of mature folk morality, Ramsify them, and moral terms denote whatever descriptive properties fill the roles the network specifies — a priori tied to the descriptive, yet synonymous with no single natural predicate.

**The supervenience challenge.** All parties grant that the moral supervenes on the natural: no two situations alike in every natural respect differ morally. Blackburn asks the non-naturalist to explain why. If moral properties are distinct from natural ones, the ban on mixed worlds is a brute necessity linking separate domains, unexplained and unexplainable — whereas expressivism explains it through the function of evaluation, which must be consistent to guide action. Replies: identity theorists have no debt, since identity entails supervenience; Shafer-Landau proposes that natural properties constitute moral ones without being identical to them; Enoch and Scanlon answer that normative-on-descriptive supervenience is brute in the way logical and mathematical necessities are, and no worse off; Rosen contends the thesis holds with only normative, not metaphysical, necessity, and so demands less than Blackburn assumes.

**Moral Twin Earth.** Horgan and Timmons turn Putnam's thought experiment against Cornell realism. Suppose "good" on Earth causally tracks the consequentialist property and on Twin Earth the deontological one. Causal semantics says the two populations mean different things and do not disagree; intuition says they disagree about morality. So moral reference is not fixed by a natural kind, and the naturalist owes another semantics. Replies concede the datum and relocate it — Copp fixes reference by a term's normative role, Brink treats the disagreement as marking shared conceptual role rather than shared kind — or deny that the intuition survives a fully described case (Dowell).

**Debunking.** Street's dilemma — either selection tracked moral truth, or our beliefs match it by coincidence — is adjudicated at moral epistemology. Here it registers a division: the third-factor replies (Enoch, Wielenberg) suit non-naturalism, while naturalists answer that moral facts, being natural, can enter the selective explanation itself — the same claim that exposes them to Twin Earth.
