# Ethics

Some things ought to be done and some ought not, some lives go well and some badly, some people are owed things by others — or so all of us talk, all the time, including the philosophers who officially doubt it. Ethics is the division that asks whether any of that talk answers to anything, what it would answer to, and what, concretely, follows for how to live. It articulates into three tiers whose independence is itself a finding: metaethics (the status of the whole enterprise), normative theory (the rival systematizations of right and good), and applied ethics (the practical fields, each now a literature of its own) — ringed by value theory, moral psychology, moral epistemology, and responsibility, and bordered by politics (which took justice), metaphysics (which took free will), and mind (which took motivation's mechanics).

**Metaethics** holds the division's foundations and its deepest unease. The realism map runs from Moore's open-question argument through contemporary non-naturalism (Parfit, Scanlon) and the naturalist realisms; Mackie's error theory takes morality's claims at face value and declares them uniformly false — the argument from queerness pricing what realism would cost; the expressivist arc from Ayer to Blackburn and Gibbard reconstructs moral talk as attitude rather than assertion, with the Frege–Geach problem as its fifty-year tax; constructivism locates moral truth in what agents or procedures would endorse; and Street's evolutionary debunking argument — our moral dispositions were selected for fitness, not truth-tracking — presses every realist to explain why the correlation with moral fact is no coincidence. Underneath: where could normative authority come from at all?

**Value theory** asks what is good in itself: hedonism against Nozick's experience machine, desire-satisfaction and its idealization spiral, objective lists and the charge of elitism; Moore's intrinsic value and organic unities; the fitting-attitudes analysis and its wrong-kind-of-reasons problem; incommensurability and parity; whether value is one kind or many. **The normative triad** carries the classical contest. Consequentialism — rightness as best outcome, profoundest in Sidgwick — absorbs its objections (demandingness, integrity, the separateness of persons, Taurek's numbers) through an industry of refinements (rule, indirect, sophisticated, satisficing, scalar) whose very success raises the consequentializing worry that the theory has become unfalsifiable by absorption. Deontology holds that some things may not be done whatever the consequences: the Kantian core (universalizability, humanity as end), Ross's prima facie duties, the paradox of deontology (why not minimize violations of the very constraints?), double effect and the trolley laboratory, and Scanlon's contractualism as the modern engine. Virtue ethics, revived by Anscombe's indictment of law-without-a-lawgiver morality, makes character and practical wisdom primary — neo-Aristotelian naturalism, the guidance objection, the situationist challenge from psychology — and finds in the Confucian tradition an independent, fully developed counterpart treated on its own terms.

**Moral psychology** reruns reason against sentiment with data: motivational internalism and the Humean theory, dual-process evidence and Berker's challenge to its normative significance, the moral emotions and empathy's critics, psychopathy as test case. **Moral epistemology** asks how any of it is known: intuitions and reflective equilibrium against the debunkers, the puzzle of moral testimony (deference that seems defective), moral expertise and disagreement. **Moral responsibility** — split by editorial decision from `metaphysics.free-will`, which owns the freedom question — treats blame, praise, and desert as normative statuses: the Strawsonian program, quality of will, attributability against accountability, the ethics of blame, and what survives if the skeptics are right. **Applied ethics** is the hub where the division meets the world — bioethics, AI ethics, environmental and animal ethics, war — with its own methodological question (principlism, casuistry, theory-application) and its newest client at `mind.artificial-minds`. **Future people** holds the field's hardest open problem: Parfit's non-identity argument and the population axiologies, every one of which pays an impossibility theorem's price.

The figures register anchors: `aristotle`, `hume`, and `kant` supply the triad's three roots; `mill` its liberal conscience; `nietzsche` the standing genealogical attack; `parfit` (stubbed, queued) its modern re-founder.
