# Error Theory and Fictionalism

Error theory sides with the realist about what moral talk means and with the anti-realist about what the world contains. Moral judgments are beliefs purporting to describe objective, categorically binding facts; there are no such facts; so moral judgments are uniformly untrue. Its literature splits into the arguments for the verdict and the question of what a convinced error theorist should do the following morning.

**Mackie's arguments.** *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977) supplies the classical case. The *argument from relativity* observes that moral codes vary across societies in ways that track ways of life rather than perceptions of a shared moral reality; the best explanation is that people approve of what they participate in, not that some of them see better. Mackie grants its modesty: scientists disagree too. The *argument from queerness* carries the weight and has two limbs. Metaphysically, objective values would have to be intrinsically prescriptive: states of the world that of themselves give any agent reason to act whatever her desires, with to-be-pursuedness built in — "utterly different from anything else in the universe." Epistemologically, knowing them would require a faculty unlike any ordinary way of knowing, since no perception or inference carries from how things are to what must be done. Moral concepts are not incoherent but empty: the discourse presupposes something that does not exist, as witch-talk once did.

**Joyce's evolutionary route.** Joyce (*The Myth of Morality*, 2001; *The Evolution of Morality*, 2006) reaches the verdict by genealogy. The capacity to experience certain demands as inescapable and authoritative enhanced cooperation in ancestral groups, and would have done so whether or not anything answered to it. Since the best explanation of our moral beliefs nowhere mentions moral facts, the beliefs are unjustified; since what they assert — practical clout independent of any institution or desire — is what nothing could supply, they are false. This is the debunking argument of `ethics.moral-epistemology` carried past skepticism to error.

**Olson's version.** Olson (*Moral Error Theory*, 2014) prunes Mackie's several queerness arguments to the one that survives. Supervenience, motivation, and epistemic access all have realist answers; what does not is the queerness of *irreducible normativity* — favouring relations that count in favour of acting apart from any desire, aim, or convention. Institutional reasons (chess, etiquette) are unproblematic because their normativity reduces to the institution's standards; error theory targets only the irreducible remainder.

**Companions in guilt.** The standing reply, sharpened by Cuneo (*The Normative Web*, 2007), is that epistemic facts are exactly as queer. That one *ought* to believe what the evidence supports is a categorical, irreducibly normative claim; if such claims cannot be true, there is no reason to believe error theory — or anything — and the view saws off its own branch. Error theorists answer three ways. Deny the parity: epistemic reasons reduce to truth-conduciveness or the aims of inquiry in a way moral reasons do not. Accept the parity and generalize: Streumer's *Unbelievable Errors* (2017) defends an error theory of all normative judgment and argues that we cannot believe it — which is why it seems false. Or separate truth from reasons: Olson holds that error theory can be true though there is no irreducible reason to believe it, since an argument's validity requires no normative facts.

**The morning after.** Grant the verdict; what follows for practice? Four options structure the literature. *Abolitionism* (Hinckfuss, Garner) argues that moral talk does harm — it fossilizes disputes and licenses cruelty — and should go the way of astrology. *Fictionalism* (Joyce) keeps the discourse as a useful pretence: moral thinking is a precommitment device against weakness of will, and one can be immersed in it as a spectator is immersed in a play while retaining the standing knowledge that it is false. Its problem is stability — whether one can occupy the fiction whenever motivation is needed and step out whenever theory demands. Kalderon's *hermeneutic* fictionalism differs in holding that moral discourse was never assertoric to begin with. *Conservationism* (Olson) advises keeping the false beliefs themselves in everyday contexts, since beliefs motivate better than pretences and the error theorist's second-order knowledge is seldom before her mind. *Substitutionism* (Lutz, 2014) recommends replacing moral claims with claims about something real — what one's considered desires or a specified standard requires — that does most of morality's coordinating work without the false presupposition.
