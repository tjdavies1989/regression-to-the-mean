# Peer Disagreement in Philosophy

Expert dissensus in philosophy is not an impression; it has been measured. The PhilPapers surveys (Bourget and Chalmers, 2009 and 2020) polled professional philosophers on the discipline's central questions and found near-even splits on most of them — normative ethics divided three ways, free will and personal identity contested, no position on most questions commanding even a bare majority — with strikingly little movement between the two surveys. The respondents were trained in the same methods, at the same institutions, on the same public arguments. If anyone is anyone's epistemic peer, philosophers are each other's. The machinery built at `epistemology.disagreement` therefore applies, reflexively, to the discipline that built it — and its most popular family of views yields an alarming output. Conciliationism says that discovering a peer who disagrees requires substantial belief revision; run on the survey data, it appears to require suspending judgment on nearly every contested philosophical question, which is nearly every philosophical question. Goldberg concludes that systematic peer disagreement leaves philosophical assertion outrunning its warrant and recommends attitudes weaker than belief — regarding a view as defensible, speculating, hoping; Barnett similarly retreats to mere inclination; Kornblith accepts the skeptical verdict outright. The position's strength is its refusal of special pleading: it applies the independence principle to our own guild exactly as we apply it to disagreeing jurors, forecasters, and theologians.

The standard rejoinder is that the argument eats itself. Its skeptical conclusion is a philosophical thesis; conciliationism is itself contested among epistemologist peers (the surveys confirm it); so conciliationism demands diminished confidence in conciliationism. What follows from the self-undermining is disputed. Elga exempts fundamental epistemic principles from their own scope, at the cost of looking ad hoc; steadfast theorists read the instability as a reductio; Christensen suggests that a self-limiting epistemic principle may simply be our condition, not a refutation. What no one has shown is that the argument's collapsing licenses renewed confidence in the first-order views it targeted.

A second escape denies the setup. Perhaps genuine peerhood is rare in philosophy — intuitions function as private evidence, so disputants rarely share an evidential base. Or perhaps, more corrosively, philosophical training is not truth-tracking expertise at all: reliable expertise develops where practice receives prompt, unambiguous feedback (chess, meteorology), and philosophy's verdicts receive none. That deflates the evidential force of philosophers' disagreement — but at the price of deflating the force of their agreement too, a cost the expertise defense debated at `meta.methods` must also pay.

What the dissensus is evidence *of* remains open. It may indicate hardness — the surviving questions are the hardest survivors, the incubator reading of `meta.progress` — or method failure, the restrictionist moral. Chalmers's verbal-dispute diagnosis offers a partial dissolution: some persistent disputes are merely verbal, and his method of elimination (ban the contested term, see whether disagreement survives) is a filter for them. Applied honestly, though, the filter passes most of the survey questions; the diagnosis trims the scandal without dissolving it.
