# Progress in Philosophy

A physics undergraduate knows more physics than Newton did; it is not obvious that any living philosopher knows more philosophy — in the sense of settled answers — than Aristotle. The problems of free will, skepticism, and the good life are older than most sciences, and the PhilPapers surveys confirm that professional philosophers remain split on nearly all of them, often close to evenly. This is the scandal framing: millennia of work by many of history's best minds, and no consensus to show for it. David Chalmers gives the framing its sharpest modern form: there has not been large collective convergence on the big questions of philosophy, and since convergence on big questions is roughly what progress looks like in science, the pointed question is *why isn't there more?*

Something is wrong with the framing, and something survives its correction. What is wrong is a selection effect: philosophy is the incubator that loses every child it raises. Questions that become empirically or formally tractable — motion, the heavens, the mind, inference, meaning — secede as physics, psychology, logic, linguistics, and their solutions are booked to the new discipline's account, never the old one's. Judge philosophy only by the residue that has not yet left, and it is guaranteed to look barren; the "big questions" are big partly *because* they are the hardest survivors. What is right in the framing is that even after this correction, persistent expert disagreement under shared methods still wants explaining, and the explanations on offer (the questions are uniquely hard; the method is defective; the questions are misconceived) are themselves philosophical positions with no consensus among them.

The substantive debate concerns the currency in which progress should be counted. If the currency is **convergence to the truth on the big questions**, pessimism follows almost analytically. Pessimists press an induction run on philosophy itself: every past philosophical theory has been rejected by later philosophers, so current theories will be too — belief in any of them is unwarranted. Colin McGinn adds a diagnosis: the problems are closed to minds like ours, as calculus is to a dog. Optimists answer on several fronts. One is to change the currency: philosophy accumulates **distinctions** (a theory of knowledge must now survive Gettier; no earlier era could even state that constraint), **refuted options** (verificationism and unreconstructed logical behaviorism are genuinely off the table), and **spun-off sciences** — on which measure the pessimistic induction inverts into an optimistic one, since the same survivorship bias that makes philosophy look sterile proves it repeatedly fertile. Daniel Stoljar's "reasonable optimism" argues instead that once problems are individuated properly — specific, formulable questions rather than perennial topic-labels — philosophy has a respectable record of actually solving them. A further family of views, developed by Finnur Dellsén and others, holds that philosophical progress consists in **understanding** rather than settled knowledge: command of the space of positions, their costs, and their entailments can deepen without any position winning. Its strength is fit with practice — this is visibly what philosophers produce; its standing worry is cheapness, since it threatens to count every well-mapped stalemate as an achievement.

The comparison with science cuts both ways. The contrast "science converges, philosophy doesn't" is drawn against an idealized science: science's own history contains discarded ontologies, Kuhnian losses, and stretches of stalemate — the century-long standoff over the interpretation of quantum mechanics is philosophy-style disagreement inside physics. But the disanalogy resists full dissolution: in the sciences such episodes are exceptional and usually terminated; in philosophy they are the normal condition.
