# Faith and Reason

Billions of people believe in God, and almost none of them believe on the strength of the cosmological argument. If the arguments of natural theology settle nothing — and after three centuries of refinement they have convinced few who did not already believe — then either nearly all religious belief is irrational, or rationality does not require arguments. Faith and reason is the epistemology of that dilemma: not whether God exists, but whether anyone is entitled to believe it, and what the believing even is.

The challenge is **evidentialism**. Locke held that a rational believer proportions assent to evidence; Clifford sharpened it into a moral charge — "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" — with his shipowner, who lulled his doubts about a leaky vessel into sincere conviction and drowned his passengers. On this standard, religious belief without propositional evidence is not just mistaken but negligent. The position's strength is that it is nobody's invention: it is how we already police belief about everything else.

**Reformed epistemology** replies by attacking the standard rather than meeting it. Plantinga observes that evidentialism cannot satisfy itself — belief in other minds, the past, the external world all lack the required arguments yet are plainly rational, because they are *properly basic*: formed directly by our faculties, not inferred. Why not belief in God, produced by a *sensus divinitatis* working as designed? In the mature version, a belief has warrant when produced by cognitive faculties functioning properly in a congenial environment according to a truth-aimed design plan — so if theism is true, theistic belief is probably warranted, and any argument that it is irrational must first argue that it is false. The **Great Pumpkin objection** presses the obvious worry: could not any sect declare its creed properly basic? Plantinga's answer — proper basicality is grounded in actual belief-forming conditions, not claimable by fiat — spawned the *son-of-Great-Pumpkin* exchanges over whether the reply lets every internally coherent community certify itself.

**Religious experience** offers a middle road: not basicality but perception. Alston argues that the Christian mystical practice of forming beliefs about God from experience is a *doxastic practice* on all fours with sense perception — neither can be validated without circularity, both are innocent until proven guilty. Swinburne's principle of credulity generalizes: how things seem is prima facie evidence for how things are, absent defeaters. The skeptic's lever is the defeaters: experiences in rival traditions deliver incompatible gods.

**Pragmatic arguments** change the currency. Pascal's wager argues that belief is the dominant bet — finite stake, infinite payoff — and founders, or not, on the many-gods objection: the decision matrix omits rival deities, including ones who reward disbelief. James's will to believe is more careful: where an option is live, forced, and momentous, and evidence cannot decide, the heart may lawfully choose — a direct rebuttal of Clifford rather than an evasion.

Beneath all of this lies the question of **what faith is**. If faith is belief that God exists, the evidentialist challenge bites hard; if it is trust in a person, or — as Howard-Snyder, Kvanvig, and others argue — a resilient commitment compatible with doubt and even with acceptance without belief, the epistemology changes shape entirely. Meanwhile **cognitive science of religion** supplies a new debunking pressure: if theistic belief issues from hypersensitive agency detection and similar mechanisms selected for survival rather than truth, does explaining the belief explain it away — or, as Plantinga's design-plan theodicy of cognition would have it, merely describe the instrument?
