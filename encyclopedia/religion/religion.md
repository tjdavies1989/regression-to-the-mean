# Philosophy of Religion

Philosophy of religion asks whether there is a God, what such a being could be like, and what it is reasonable to believe about either question. The division is hard because its central object, if real, is unlike anything else philosophy handles: not one more item in the world but the alleged reason there is a world at all. The philosopher's tools were calibrated on ordinary things, and each question turns partly on whether they still work at this altitude.

Start with existence. The cosmological argument presses a question that never goes away: why is there something rather than nothing? Contingent things call for explanation, and a regress of contingent explainers explains nothing; so there must be a necessary being. The critic replies that the universe may be a brute fact. The design tradition argues from the appearance of order — now most forcefully from the fine-tuning of physical constants — and its opponents answer with selection effects, multiverses, and Hume's point that the analogy between cosmos and artifact is thin. The ontological argument tries to close the question from the armchair, deriving God's existence from the concept of a maximally great being; nearly everyone suspects a trick, and the enduring disagreement is over where it lies.

Against all this stands the problem of evil, the same engine running in reverse. If God is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good, why do children die of leukemia? The logical version claims outright contradiction; the evidential version, harder to dislodge, claims that the quantity and apparent pointlessness of suffering is strong evidence against theism. Theodicies answer that evil is the price of free will or the condition of soul-making; skeptical theists answer that we cannot judge what reasons God could have — a defense whose cost may be general skepticism about divine purposes.

A second cluster concerns belief itself. Must faith answer to evidence? Evidentialists, following Clifford, say believing without sufficient evidence is always wrong; Reformed epistemologists reply that belief in God can be properly basic, like trust in memory; Pascal and James argue that practical stakes can move belief where evidence is silent. Religious experience, Hume's case that testimony can almost never establish a miracle, and the plurality of incompatible faiths all feed this debate.

The field articulates accordingly: natural theology and its critics; the problem of evil; the divine attributes; religious epistemology; religious language; diversity; miracles; survival of death; and non-theistic traditions — Buddhist, Daoist, and others — which test how far the division's theistic framing generalizes. Its boundaries run along metaphysics (necessary being, creation), epistemology (evidence, testimony), mind (souls, persons), and ethics (divine command theory is homed there and bridges here).

## Children

- `existence-of-god` — **Arguments for the Existence of God** — the cosmological, ontological, design, and fine-tuning arguments and their critics. *(anchor)*
- `evil` — **The Problem of Evil** — whether the world's suffering is compatible with, or evidence against, a perfect God. *(anchor)*
- `attributes` — **Divine Attributes** — the coherence of omnipotence, omniscience, eternity, and perfect goodness, singly and jointly. *(standard)*
- `faith-reason` — **Faith and Reason** — the epistemology of religious belief: evidentialism, Reformed epistemology, pragmatic arguments, religious experience. *(anchor)*
- `language` — **Religious Language** — whether talk of God is literal, analogical, or non-cognitive; bridges language. *(satellite)*
- `diversity` — **Religious Diversity and Pluralism** — what the plurality of incompatible faiths implies for the rationality and truth of any one. *(standard)*
- `miracles` — **Miracles** — what a miracle would be and whether testimony could ever establish one. *(satellite)*
- `afterlife` — **Death, Souls, and Afterlife** — whether persons could survive death, and what they would have to be if so; bridges mind. *(satellite)*
- `non-theistic` — **Non-Theistic Religious Philosophy** — the philosophical content of Buddhist, Daoist, and other traditions without a creator God; bridges traditions. *(standard)*

## Bridges

- To `metaphysics`: necessary being, creation, eternity, and providence are metaphysical theses; the theistic arguments run on modality and causation.
- To `epistemology`: the faith-and-reason debate is applied epistemology — evidence, testimony, disagreement, proper basicality.
- To `mind` and `language`: survival of death turns on personal identity and dualism; religious language tests theories of meaning.
- To `ethics` and the registers: divine command theory bridges from ethics; the division's history runs through figures (Anselm, Aquinas, Avicenna, Hume) and its non-Western breadth through the traditions register.
