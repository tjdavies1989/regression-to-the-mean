# Miracles

Every theistic tradition rests part of its case on reports of the extraordinary: a sea parted, a prophet ascended, a man raised from the dead. The philosophical problem is double. Metaphysically, what would a miracle even be, in a world that natural laws are supposed to govern? Epistemologically, could any evidence — above all testimony, which is all most believers have — ever make it reasonable to believe one occurred? The second question is where the field's weight sits, because a single well-established miracle would be the most direct evidence theism could ask for, and the skeptic knows it.

On the first question, **Hume's definition** sets the terms: a miracle is a violation of a law of nature by the volition of the Deity. The standing objection is that this may be incoherent — if laws simply describe what happens, a "violated law" is just a falsified description, and the miracle dissolves into a counterexample. **Swinburne's** repair defines the miracle as a *non-repeatable counterinstance*: the law remains the best account of what nature does when left to itself, and the exception is singular, not law-refuting. **Leibniz** refuses the picture entirely: God does not interrupt his own order; miracles exceed the natures of created things but conform to the universal plan, decreed with everything else from the beginning — a view congenial to classical theism but hard-pressed to say what distinguishes miracle from providence. At the other pole, **coincidence accounts** (Holland's child saved on the railway tracks by an unrelated mechanical failure) require no broken law at all: a miracle is an extraordinary, religiously significant convergence. Its strength is fidelity to religious practice, where significance rather than physics does the work; its cost is making miracles relative to an interpreting eye.

On the second question, **Hume's argument** deserves analysis, not recitation. Its structure is a balance of proofs: the wise man proportions belief to evidence; testimony earns its force from experience of its reliability; but a law of nature is backed by uniform experience — the strongest proof experience affords. A miracle report therefore always faces the maximum possible counterweight, and testimony suffices only if its falsehood would be *more* miraculous than the event it reports. Part II adds the empirical dirt: credulity, the love of wonder, the origin of miracle reports in "ignorant and barbarous" ages, and the mutual cancellation of rival religions' miracles. The perennial charge is question-begging — the "uniform" experience against the miracle is uniform only if the disputed reports are discounted in advance.

**Earman's** *Hume's Abject Failure* presses the deeper charge: read as an a priori ceiling, the argument proves too much, licensing the rejection of testimony to *any* sufficiently rare event — scientific anomalies included. The **Bayesian reconstructions** make the point precise: the posterior turns on the prior probability of the miracle and the likelihood ratio of the testimony, and a tiny prior can in principle be overcome by testimony whose probability of being false is smaller still. The in-principle question thereby dissolves into base rates and witness reliability. Multiple-witness cases sharpen it further — Babbage and Condorcet showed that *independent* witnesses multiply likelihood ratios, driving the probability of collective error down exponentially — and the McGrews' cumulative Bayesian case for the resurrection is the frontier example. But independence is exactly where Hume's Part II returns: enthusiasm, contagion, and the selective survival of reports are how correlated error mimics independent confirmation. What survives of Hume is not a proof but a discipline: extraordinary claims face extraordinary base rates, and the argument now runs on numbers.
