# Non-Theistic Religious Philosophy

Most of the humans who have ever lived a religious life have not lived it around a creator God. Yet this division's toolkit — arguments for God's existence, the problem of evil, the divine attributes — presupposes one. This node asks what philosophy of religion looks like when the presupposition is dropped: what the great non-theistic traditions actually claim about the ultimate, argued on their own terms rather than as deviant answers to theism's questionnaire; and, sharpening that worry, whether the category "religion" itself is fit to classify them. The traditions register's warning governs everything here: Madhyamaka is not sloppy paraconsistency, and the Dao is not God under an alias.

**Buddhist non-theism** is the strongest case, because it is not an absence but a position. The tradition developed explicit refutations of a creator — Vasubandhu's dilemma (a complete cause would produce its effect all at once; a changing creator is one more conditioned thing), extended by Dharmakīrti and perfected in Ratnakīrti's *Refutation of Īśvara* — atheism argued with full rigor *inside* a soteriology. And what stands where theism puts its ground of being is emptiness: not a deeper substance but the absence of intrinsic nature in anything, a doctrine whose content the `buddhist-philosophy` article carries. Religion, here, is a diagnosis of suffering and a path — no worship of a maker required.

The **Dao** is a different structure again: an impersonal source that generates without making, "the mother of the ten thousand things" that acts by not acting and cannot be named. **Advaita Vedānta's nirguna Brahman** — the ultimate without qualities, with the personal Lord (saguna Brahman, Īśvara) demoted to a provisional appearance — shows the theism question fought *within* a single tradition: Rāmānuja and Madhva answered Śaṅkara that the highest truth about Brahman is personal, making Vedānta the best archive of the personal-versus-impersonal ultimate as a live debate rather than a comparativist's contrast. **Confucian tian** completes the spread: Heaven as normative order rather than lawgiver, and — Fingarette's reading — the sacred located in ritual itself, reverence without revelation, transcendence in the ceremonial texture of ordinary life.

These traditions converge with theism's own **via negativa** — *neti neti*, Dionysian unsaying, the unnameable Dao — and the convergence is real but must not be flattened: the negations do different work. Dionysius protects a creator's transcendence; Advaita denies qualities of the one thing that is fully real; emptiness denies intrinsic nature of everything, itself included. Same grammar, three metaphysics.

The frame-question radicalizes the node. **W. C. Smith, Asad, and Masuzawa** argue that "religion" as a genus — private belief, creed, membership — is a modern Western export, generalized from Protestant Christianity and carried outward by colonial administration; Josephson's study of Meiji Japan shows the category being *imposed*, with law deciding what would count as religion, superstition, or secular ethics. If so, asking whether Confucianism is "really a religion" mistakes an artifact of classification for a discovery. The standing reply: family resemblance without essence is how most categories work, and the genealogy of a concept does not show it carves nothing. At the opposite limit stands **Dworkin's religion without God**: a religious attitude — the conviction that value is objective and the universe sublime — detachable from every theology, shared by Einstein and Spinoza's heirs. Its strength is exposing that theism was never the category's center; its cost is a category so wide it may no longer exclude anything.
