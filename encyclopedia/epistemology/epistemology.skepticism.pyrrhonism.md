# Pyrrhonian Skepticism

Ask a dogmatist why she believes anything, and Agrippa's Five Modes, as Sextus reports them, supply the questions. Philosophers *disagree* about it, and no umpire is agreed. It appears thus only *relative* to a perceiver and a condition. Her ground is either another ground, and so on without end (*regress*), or simply posited (*hypothesis*), or supported by what it was meant to support (*reciprocity*). Barnes's *The Toils of Scepticism* (1990) showed that these are not five arguments but one net: disagreement and relativity summon the demand for a ground, and the last three modes close every exit. The net catches reasoning as such, which is why none of the Cartesian responses treated by this node's siblings reaches it — safety, dogmatism, and shifting standards all *offer grounds*, and grounds are what the modes eat. The theory of what a ground could be is homed at `epistemology.justification`; here the modes are treated as the Pyrrhonist used them, as a machine for producing *equipollence*, the condition in which opposed considerations weigh the same.

Equipollence is where Sextus locates the whole practice. The skeptic is not one who denies that anything is known — that is the "negative dogmatism" he attributes to the Academy — but one with an *ability* to set appearances and arguments against each other so that, weighing equal, they induce *epochē*, suspension. Tranquility follows, he says, as a shadow follows a body, and the famous parable is of the painter Apelles, who achieved the foam on a horse's mouth by flinging his sponge at the picture in despair. The skeptic's own phrases — "no more this than that," "everything is undetermined" — are not assertions but reports of how he is affected, and they apply to themselves: like purgatives, they expel themselves along with the humors. Whether a set of sentences can consistently do this is the first modern question about the text.

The second is older, and the Stoics posed it to the Academy before Sextus inherited it: *apraxia*, inaction. Action requires assent; whoever suspends all assent will neither eat nor step out of the path of a cart. Sextus's reply is that the skeptic follows the *appearances* without opinion, along four guides — nature, which makes him perceive and think; feeling, which makes him hungry; the laws and customs of his country; and instruction in crafts. He lives, and undogmatically. The reply generates the interpretive quarrel that has dominated the last half-century, organized around a distinction Galen drew between *rustic* and *urbane* skeptics. Frede's "The Sceptic's Beliefs" (1979) holds that the Pyrrhonist has beliefs in a perfectly good sense — the passive acquiescence in how things strike him — and withholds only *dogmata*, beliefs held on the authority of reason and theory; on this reading the skeptic differs from his neighbor not in what he takes to be so but in his refusal to underwrite it. Burnyeat's "Can the Sceptic Live His Scepticism?" (1980) answers that appearances, for Sextus, are explicitly not assessed for truth, that belief is precisely assessment for truth, and that a creature who managed to suppress it would not be living an unopinionated human life but something else; the skeptic's *ataraxia* is bought by an insulation of appearance from truth that a human being cannot maintain. Barnes's "The Beliefs of a Pyrrhonist" (1982) declined to choose: Sextus's scope varies with his target, rustic when the mode of disagreement runs against everyday judgments, urbane when the *Outlines* limit suspension to what is "unclear" and investigated by science; the text underdetermines the man. The three papers, gathered in *The Original Sceptics* (1997), remain the debate's frame.

Fogelin's *Pyrrhonian Reflections on Knowledge and Justification* (1994) revived the position rather than the text. His claim is double: the Agrippan modes remain unanswered by foundationalism and coherentism alike, and contemporary theories of justification refute themselves by trying to answer them; yet ordinary knowledge claims, assessed by the standards in force when they are made, are mostly true. The neo-Pyrrhonist holds both and declines to build a theory of their reconciliation, which would be one more dogma. Williams's reply is that the modes presuppose a "Prior Grounding" requirement — that a belief is justified only if the believer has already grounded it — which is itself a foundationalist assumption the Pyrrhonist smuggles in; Lammenranta counters that disagreement alone, without any such requirement, regenerates the problematic.

Comparison with Indian and Buddhist thought is standard and hazardous. Pyrrho traveled with Alexander to India, and Diogenes Laertius records that he met the gymnosophists; Beckwith's *Greek Buddha* (2015) and Kuzminski make the debt substantive, while Bett's *Pyrrho, His Antecedents, and His Successors* (2000) reads the Aristocles passage as attributing to Pyrrho a *metaphysical* claim that things are indeterminate — not the epistemic suspension of Sextus, and not Buddhist either. Nāgārjuna's "I have no thesis" and Sextus's self-expelling phrases share a form, and Garfield has argued that both are non-dogmatic in the same sense; but emptiness is a soteriological teaching within a tradition that keeps its four truths, and tranquility for Sextus is a byproduct one stops seeking. The closest structural kin is Jayarāśi's *Tattvopaplavasiṃha*, which dismantles every pramāṇa in turn — toward not *ataraxia* but the enjoyment of a life freed from doctrine. Zhuangzi's problem, as `figures.zhuangzi` argues, is the criterion rather than the regress. The traditions converge on suspension and diverge on what suspension is for; the register's rule is to let them.
