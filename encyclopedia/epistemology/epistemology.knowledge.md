# The Analysis of Knowledge

Everyone can tell the difference between knowing something and merely being right about it. A juror who convicts on planted evidence that happens to point at the guilty man was right; he did not know. The analysis of knowledge asks what the difference consists in — and whether it can be spelled out in more basic terms at all.

The classical answer held for centuries by default: knowledge is justified true belief. Truth rules out error, belief makes it yours, justification rules out lucky guessing. In 1963 Edmund Gettier's three-page paper broke the analysis with two cases of a single design: a subject justifiably believes a falsehood, competently infers something from it, and the inferred belief turns out true for reasons unconnected to the evidence. Smith justifiably believes his colleague will get the job and has ten coins in his pocket; he infers that the man who gets the job has ten coins; then Smith himself gets the job — and, unnoticed, has ten coins. All three conditions hold. Nobody thinks Smith knew.

The repair industry that followed is a study in method. Defeasibility theorists added a fourth condition: no true proposition may exist which, if learned, would destroy the justification. Causal theorists (the early Goldman) required that the fact known cause the belief — good for perception, hopeless for mathematics. Nozick's sensitivity condition — if it were false, you would not believe it — handles Gettier cases elegantly but at a famous cost: sensitivity fails for "I am not a brain in a vat," so you can know you have hands without knowing what plainly follows; closure of knowledge under known entailment is abandoned. Safety, its modal cousin (Sosa, Williamson), inverts the conditional — in nearby worlds where you believe it, it is true — keeping closure while courting its own counterexamples. Each fix bred new cases; forty years converged on no analysis.

Zagzebski diagnosed why. Any analysis of the form *true belief + independent condition* invites Gettier cases by recipe: since the extra condition is independent of truth, imagine it satisfied while the belief is false, then add an accident making the belief true anyway. The luck returns by construction. The moral divides the field. Virtue epistemologists conclude the extra condition must not be independent of truth: knowledge is belief whose truth is *because of* the believer's competence — an achievement, not a conjunction. Williamson concludes something more radical: the analysis fails because knowledge is unanalyzable — a mental state in its own right, as basic as belief, indeed the state belief aspires to. His knowledge-first program inverts the whole project, taking knowledge as the primitive that explains evidence (your evidence just is what you know), assertion, and action, rather than the compound to be explained.

Two further pressures reshape the question. Contextualists argue that "knows" is context-sensitive like "flat": the standards for truly ascribing it shift with the conversation, which is why skeptical arguments compel in the seminar and seem idle at the airport. Pragmatic encroachment goes further: whether you know can depend on what is at stake for you, since knowledge is what licenses acting. Behind everything stands the oldest question, from Plato's *Meno*: why is knowledge better than mere true belief, when a true belief about the road to Larissa gets you there just as well? An analysis that cannot say what knowledge adds — stability, creditability, fitness to guide action — has anatomized something not worth the name.
