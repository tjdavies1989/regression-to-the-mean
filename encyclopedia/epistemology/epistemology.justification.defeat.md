# Defeaters and Defeasibility

Justification is defeasible: a belief held well at noon can be held badly at one o'clock though its original grounds are untouched. The widget looks red, so you believe it is red; then you learn the room is lit by red light. Nothing has happened to the look. Defeat is the theory of what has happened to the belief. Its two ancestors are the defeasibility analysis of knowledge — Lehrer and Paxson's undefeated justified true belief, and Klein's long struggle to exclude *misleading* defeaters such as Tom Grabit's lying mother — and Harman's dogmatism paradox: if I know *p*, I know any counter-evidence is misleading, so why not ignore it? Harman's answer, that acquiring evidence can destroy knowledge, is the thesis this node examines.

**Pollock's taxonomy** is the field's grammar. A *rebutting* defeater is a reason to believe not-*p*: the expert tells you the bird is not a robin. An *undercutting* defeater attacks not *p* but the link between ground and belief: the red light gives no reason to think the widget is not red, only that its looking red no longer indicates that it is. Pollock's strongest argument for the distinction is that undercutters have no rebutting paraphrase — the red light lowers your confidence in *p* without being evidence against *p* — and he built a nonmonotonic architecture (OSCAR) in which arguments are defeated, reinstated, and ranked as ultimately undefeated. *Defeater-defeaters* fall out naturally: learn that the red light is switched off and your original reason revives. The chief challenge is reductive: Kotzen and others model undercutting probabilistically, as evidence that screens off the ground's support, so that a single Bayesian relation replaces Pollock's two; the reply is that screening-off needs the very structure — reasons as distinct from their probabilistic shadows — that the reduction wanted to dispense with.

**Propositional versus doxastic defeat.** A defeater may be something the subject believes, or something she has justification to believe but does not. Bergmann argues that only believed defeaters defeat, and that they defeat even when unjustified: the subject who irrationally takes her faculties to be broken is, in fact, no longer justified in trusting them, because justification cannot survive a believed verdict against itself. Opponents reply that this rewards paranoia and licenses the reverse — an irrational belief that a genuine defeater is misleading would reinstate justification — and that a purely doxastic account cannot explain why *ignoring* evidence in one's possession is an epistemic failing. Plantinga's distinction between defeaters relative to one's noetic structure and defeaters relative to the facts marks the same fault line.

**Normative defeat** pushes further out: Lackey (on testimony) and Goldberg argue that evidence a subject *should have had* defeats, though she does not possess it — the physician who never read the chart, the researcher who skipped the journal. The argument is from practice: we hold such believers to be unjustified, not merely blameworthy. The evidentialist reply (and Lasonen-Aarnio's) is that this conflates justification with responsibility; normative defeat is a fact about the agent, not the belief. Simion's answer relocates justification itself in norms of proper cognitive function, so that failing to take up available evidence is a malfunction, not a moral lapse.

**Higher-order defeat** — evidence that one's reasoning is compromised, with no bearing on the first-order question — is homed at `epistemology.disagreement`; here it matters as the hardest test case for defeat, since the undercutter attacks the reasoner rather than any reason, and the anti-akrasia constraint (Horowitz) says one may not believe *p* while believing *p* is unsupported.

**Lasonen-Aarnio's challenge** is the current front. If knowledge is safe belief, a misleading defeater does not make the belief unsafe; the knowledge-first theorist must say that one *still knows* after learning the red light is on. Rather than retreat, she embraces "unreasonable knowledge": reasonableness is a matter of manifesting dispositions that generally yield knowledge, and clinging to a belief through apparent defeat manifests bad ones — so defeated belief is unreasonable but may remain knowledge. Replies divide. Baker-Hytch and Benton argue knowledge-first can keep defeat because evidence is knowledge (E = K): the defeater changes the evidence, and belief that no longer fits it is not knowledge. Brown takes the failure to be a reason to prefer fallibilism. Beddor argues the parallel problem sinks process reliabilism. Williamson concedes the split and treats the retained knowledge as rationally dubitable — the level-splitting that the akrasia literature contests.
