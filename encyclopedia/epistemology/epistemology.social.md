# Testimony and Social Epistemology

Run an audit on what you know: your date of birth, the shape of the continents, the existence of electrons, what happened in the news this morning. Almost none of it came from your own eyes or your own inferences. It came on someone's word — a parent, a map, a textbook, a byline. Classical epistemology was written as if the knower were alone: Descartes's meditator certifies his beliefs in a room by himself, and testimony, if mentioned, is a minor annex to perception. Social epistemology is the field that takes the audit seriously. If most knowledge is secondhand, then the epistemology of trusting others is not an annex; it is most of the building.

The foundational dispute concerns what entitles a hearer to believe a speaker. Hume's reductionism says: nothing but ordinary induction. You may trust testimony exactly insofar as you have evidence that testifiers of this kind, on subjects of this kind, tend to be right — testimonial justification reduces to perception, memory, and inference. Its strength is hygiene: no belief on bare say-so, no gullibility built into the theory. Its notorious cost is that nobody has the required evidence base — children believe their parents long before they could run the induction, and any check on testifiers' reliability leans, as Coady pressed, on further testimony. Reid's anti-reductionism accepts the cost as a datum: we have a default entitlement to believe what we are told, defeasible by evidence of deceit or incompetence but not needing positive credentials — nature pairs a disposition to speak truly with a disposition to trust. Its strength is fit with actual epistemic life; its worry is that entitlement without evidence looks like sanctioned credulity. The assurance view (Moran, Faulkner) relocates the question: telling is not handing over evidence but performing an interpersonal act — the speaker offers her assurance and takes responsibility for the truth of what she says, which is why being lied to is a wrong done to you, not a malfunction in your instrument. Its open problem is explaining how a moral transaction generates distinctively epistemic warrant.

Dependence deepens with expertise. Goldman's novice/two-experts problem: when two credentialed experts disagree, the layperson by definition cannot evaluate their first-order arguments, and every proxy — track records, credentials, the concurrence of other experts, performance in debate — is either testimony again or requires expertise to read. Groups raise a different question: can a committee know? Summativists say group belief is just most members believing; Gilbert's joint-commitment account and List and Pettit's work on judgment aggregation say otherwise — the discursive dilemma shows a group's rational collective view cannot in general be any simple function of its members' views, suggesting group agents with epistemic states of their own.

Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice* gave the field its ethical turn: testimonial injustice, where prejudice deflates a speaker's credibility, wrongs her specifically as a knower; hermeneutical injustice leaves whole experiences — harassment before the word existed — without shared concepts to articulate them. And the field's newest frontier is institutional design: peer review, prediction markets, search rankings, and platforms are truth-machines that can be engineered well or badly, evaluated (in Goldman's veritistic program) by the accuracy of the beliefs they tend to produce — misinformation cascades being the failure mode now everywhere visible. What peers should do when they discover they disagree is homed at the disagreement node.

- To `ethics`: Fricker's categories are jointly ethical and epistemic — credibility is something owed, and its denial a wrong.
- To `science`: peer review and the division of cognitive labor (Kitcher) make the social structure of science a shared subject.
