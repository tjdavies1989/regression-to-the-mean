# Disagreement and Higher-Order Evidence

You split a dinner check in your head, carefully, and get $43. Your friend — as good at arithmetic as you, working from the same bill — gets $45. Before comparing answers you would have said you were probably right; now what? Christensen's case is trivial on purpose: nearly everyone concedes you should suspend judgment or split the difference. Substitute God, capital punishment, or the interpretation of quantum mechanics, and the same structure yields a disturbing result — for on those questions, too, there are people as informed and careful as you who have reached the opposite verdict. Discovering a *peer* who disagrees looks like evidence that you have made a mistake. The question is what that evidence rationally requires.

Conciliationism says: substantial revision. Elga's equal weight view holds that you should give a peer's verdict the same credence you give your own, since favoring yourself just because the disputed reasoning is *yours* is bootstrapping — using the very judgment in question to certify itself. Its strength is the check case and the independence requirement behind it: your assessment of a peer must not lean on the disputed reasoning. Steadfastness, in Kelly's hands, answers that conciliation throws away the evidence: if you in fact evaluated the first-order evidence correctly and your peer did not, the total evidence — which includes the arguments themselves, not just the head-count of who accepts them — can still favor your view. Equal weight makes rationality hostage to demographics and lets the mistaken party drag down the accurate one. Between the poles lie total-evidence and partial-conciliation views on which the two levels are weighed together. Conciliationism also faces a self-undermining problem: epistemologists are peers, and they disagree about conciliationism; the view applied to itself demands its own dilution. Elga's reply — that a fundamental epistemic principle must be dogmatic about its own correctness, as any consistent policy of belief revision must be — strikes critics as an exemption invented at the point of need.

Disagreement is one species of **higher-order evidence**: evidence about the reliability of your own judgment. Learn that you have been dosed with a reason-distorting drug, that you are hypoxic at altitude, that your training data was skewed, that your verdicts fit a known bias — and your first-order evidence has not changed, yet something seems to have. The puzzle is sharp. If you drop the belief, you flout evidence that (by hypothesis) still supports it. If you keep it, you seem committed to "p, but my belief that p is probably irrational" — epistemic akrasia, which Horowitz argues is never rational. Calibrationists (Christensen) accept that higher-order evidence can require retreating from a conclusion your evidence in fact supports — rational toxicity as the price of self-correction; level-splitters (Lasonen-Aarnio) deny it, allowing the levels to come apart.

Beneath both debates sits the uniqueness question. White's uniqueness thesis says a body of evidence fixes exactly one rational attitude toward a proposition; permissivists (Kelly, Schoenfield) hold that rational agents with different priors or epistemic standards may respond differently to the same evidence. If permissivism is true, peers can knowingly and blamelessly diverge, and conciliatory pressure weakens. And some disagreements go deeper still: Fogelin argued that when the clash is between frameworks — the hinge commitments by which each side evaluates evidence at all — argument runs out, because nothing counts as common ground. Religious, political, and cross-traditional disputes are the test cases, and philosophy itself is the reflexive one: a discipline of experts in permanent disagreement about nearly everything, its practitioners facing Elga's dilemma between spinelessness and dogmatism. What that standing fact means for philosophical belief, and for the field's claim to progress, is pursued in the metaphilosophy register.
