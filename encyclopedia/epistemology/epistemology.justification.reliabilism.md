# Reliabilism

Goldman's "What Is Justified Belief?" (1979) set itself a constraint no prior theory had accepted: the conditions for justified belief must be stated in non-epistemic vocabulary, so that the theory explains the property rather than trading in synonyms. The account it produced is recursive. A belief is justified if it results from a *belief-independent* process (perception, memory, introspection) that is unconditionally reliable — yields a high proportion of truths — or from a *belief-dependent* process (inference) that is conditionally reliable, yielding truths when its input beliefs are true, and whose inputs are themselves justified. Reliability is a property of process *types*, not of the belief or the believer's reasons, and the believer need not know it obtains. The literature is its wounds.

**The generality problem.** Every token process instantiates indefinitely many types. The belief that a car passes falls under *perception*, *visual perception in daylight*, *perception of moving objects by a tired forty-year-old*, and *this process on this occasion* — whose reliability is 1 or 0, the single-case problem. Broad types deliver verdicts no one accepts; narrow ones trivialize the theory. Goldman noticed this; Conee and Feldman ("The Generality Problem for Reliabilism," 1998) made it a refutation: the relevant type must be fixed by a principled rule, not case by case, and every candidate — psychologically real kinds (Alston), statistically relevant properties, the type the believer would cite — is either arbitrary or smuggles in epistemic terms. Responses fall into three kinds. Beebe's tri-level solution ties types to the computational problem being solved; Lyons lets cognitive science individuate processes. Comesaña's well-founded solution relativizes the type to the evidence the belief is based on — at the price, critics say, of becoming evidentialism. And Bishop and Comesaña argue the problem is everybody's: evidentialists must likewise fix what counts as the same evidence and the same fit, so generality is a tax on any theory, not a refutation of one.

**The new evil demon** is homed as a satellite at `epistemology.justification.internalism-externalism`; here only reliabilism's own moves matter. Goldman's *Epistemology and Cognition* (1986) indexed reliability to *normal worlds* — worlds as we believe the actual world to be — so the deceived duplicate's perception counts as reliable. The fix was abandoned within two years: it makes justification hostage to folk beliefs about the world, and it leaves the demon victim's processes unreliable in the world that matters to her. Goldman's 1988 strong–weak split and the 1992 "approved list" (a belief is justified if produced by a process on the community's list of approved virtues) replaced it; Comesaña's indexical reliabilism, which reads "reliable" as reliable-in-the-actual-world, is the current best.

**Bootstrapping and easy knowledge.** Vogel's Roxanne (2000) reads her gas gauge and, being reliable, is justified in believing the tank is full; she also sees the gauge reads "full"; she conjoins these, infers the gauge was accurate this time, and, after many trips, concludes by induction that her gauge is reliable — without ever checking it. Reliabilism licenses every step. Cohen (2002) generalized: any theory allowing *basic knowledge* — knowledge from a source without prior knowledge that the source is reliable — permits knowing that one's faculties are reliable too easily, by bootstrapping or by closure (the table looks red, so it is red, so it is not white under red light). The target is therefore dogmatist evidentialism as much as reliabilism. Replies: van Cleve accepts the consequence as the price of any non-skeptical epistemology; Weisberg's no-feedback constraint forbids using a source's outputs to confirm the source; Titelbaum locates the fault in the rule, not the conclusion; Cohen himself distinguishes knowledge from the rational assurance bootstrapping fails to give.

**Process versus indicator reliabilism.** Alston's "An Internalist Externalism" (1988) relocates reliability from the process to the *ground*: a belief is justified when based on an accessible ground that is a reliable indicator of its truth. The believer can survey her ground though not its reliability; Norman has no ground at all. Comesaña's evidentialist reliabilism (2010) fuses the two: a belief is justified when based on evidence such that believing on that evidence is reliable. Whether this dissolves the internalism–externalism divide or names its midpoint is contested.

**The virtue-reliabilist turn.** Sosa's *Knowledge in Perspective* (1991) and Greco moved reliability from processes to the agent's stable competences. This answers Norman (his clairvoyance is not integrated into his cognitive character), the generality problem (types are the agent's dispositions), and the value problem (an apt belief is an achievement). Whether it survives as reliabilism or replaces it is decided at `epistemology.virtue`.
