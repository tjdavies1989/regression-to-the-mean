# Epistemology

Epistemology asks what knowledge is, what entitles anyone to believe anything, and whether the entitlement we take ourselves to have could survive honest scrutiny. The questions look administrative until they are pressed. You believe the sun will rise tomorrow, that other people have minds, that you had breakfast. Ask *why* those beliefs deserve their confidence, and every answer either invokes another belief — which then needs its own credentials — or stops somewhere that looks arbitrary. That is the regress problem, already sharpened by Sextus Empiricus: a justification chain must circle, run forever, or halt at an unjustified stopping point. Foundationalists embrace the halt, arguing that some beliefs — perceptual seemings, simple logical truths — are credible without borrowing credibility from elsewhere. Coherentists deny that justification is a chain at all: beliefs are supported the way stones in an arch are, mutually, and the circle is vicious only if support must flow one way. Infinitists bite the remaining bullet.

A second pressure point is the concept of knowledge itself. For much of the twentieth century, knowledge was analyzed as justified true belief. Gettier's 1963 counterexamples — cases where a justified belief is true by luck, on evidence about the wrong fact — showed the three conditions can be met while knowledge is plainly absent. The repair industry that followed (causal conditions, defeasibility, safety, sensitivity) never converged, and Williamson's knowledge-first program draws the moral that knowledge cannot be built from more basic ingredients at all.

Third, skepticism. If you cannot rule out that you are dreaming, or a brain in a vat fed a perfect simulation, and if knowing you have hands requires ruling that out, then you do not know you have hands. Both premises are hard to deny; the conclusion is unbelievable. Responses map the field: Moorean confidence that we know the commonsense proposition better than any skeptical premise; externalist theories on which knowing requires reliable connection to the facts, not the ability to certify the connection from inside; contextualist proposals on which "know" shifts its standards with the conversation. The internalism–externalism dispute this opens — whether justification depends only on factors accessible to reflection, or also on facts (like a faculty's reliability) the believer cannot inspect — runs through the whole division.

The division's shape follows these pressures: the analysis of knowledge; the structure and sources of justification; skepticism; the a priori; and a newer social turn treating testimony, disagreement, and institutions as first-class epistemic topics, alongside formal methods that model rational belief as probabilistic degrees. Its borders: perception is homed in philosophy of mind, so perceptual *justification* is treated here while perceptual *experience* is bridged there; confirmation and induction shade into philosophy of science; the a priori opens into logic and metaphysics.

## Bridges

- To `mind`: perception is homed there; whether experience justifies belief directly (dogmatism) or only via background theory is a live joint dispute.
- To `logic` and `science`: formal epistemology shares Bayesian machinery with confirmation theory, and the problem of induction is claimed by both divisions.
- To `metaphysics`: the a priori and modal knowledge — how armchair reflection could reveal necessity — is epistemology's standing audit of metaphysical method.
- To the registers: Descartes, Hume, and Kant set the modern problem-space; Sextus and Nyāya debates over the sources of knowledge show the division's questions are not parochially European.
