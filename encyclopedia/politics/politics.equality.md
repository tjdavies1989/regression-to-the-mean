# Equality and Egalitarianism

Nearly everyone professes some commitment to equality; the difficulty is saying what it is a commitment *to*. Equal treatment under law? Equal votes? Equal money? Any determinate answer generates inequality somewhere else: give people equal incomes and the sick, who need more, end up worse off; equalize satisfaction and the person of expensive tastes claims a subsidy for champagne. So the founding question of the field, posed sharply by Amartya Sen, is: **equality of what?** Welfarists take the currency to be well-being itself, which respects the point that resources are only means — but seems to reward cultivated expensive tastes. Dworkin answered with *resources*: equalize what people have to work with, then hold them responsible for what they do with it, using a hypothetical insurance market to compensate for brute bad luck like disability. Sen and Nussbaum object that both miss the target: what matters is neither goods nor satisfaction but *capabilities* — what people are actually able to do and be — since the same resources yield different real freedoms for the pregnant, the disabled, or the socially subordinated.

Dworkin's distinction between chosen and unchosen disadvantage hardened, in Cohen and Arneson, into **luck egalitarianism**: inequalities are unjust exactly when they track brute luck — genes, parents, accidents — and just when they track genuine choice. Its strength is that it captures why inherited privilege offends while gambles freely lost do not, turning egalitarianism into a precise thesis about responsibility. Elizabeth Anderson's counterattack set the field's current agenda. Luck egalitarianism, she argued, abandons the imprudent to destitution (the harshness objection: the uninsured reckless driver may be left to die), humiliates the unlucky with state-certified judgments of their inferiority, and mistakes the point of equality altogether. Equality is not a distributive pattern but a **relation**: a society of equals is one without hierarchy, domination, or stigmatizing status — *democratic equality*. Distribution matters, on this view, only insofar as it secures the standing of citizens who face one another as peers.

Distributive egalitarianism faces a second famous challenge, Parfit's **leveling-down objection**: if equality matters in itself, then blinding the sighted improves the world in one respect, even though it benefits no one — which seems perverse. Several positions dodge the objection by denying that equality as such is the goal. Frankfurt's **sufficientarianism** holds that what matters is that everyone has *enough*; above the threshold, inequality is morally inert. Parfit's own **prioritarianism** holds that benefits matter more the worse off their recipient is — delivering equality-friendly verdicts without ever valuing equality intrinsically, though at the price of explaining why relative position so often seems to matter. The newest entry, Robeyns's **limitarianism**, adds a ceiling to the floor: beyond some point, surplus wealth has no moral claim and threatens political equality itself. Behind all this lies Parfit's diagnostic distinction: **telic** egalitarians hold inequality bad in itself, wherever it occurs, and absorb the full force of leveling-down; **deontic** egalitarians hold only that injustice requires unequal *treatment* by some agent or institution — a view immune to leveling-down but silent about inequalities no one produced. Whether equality matters, and how, may turn on which of these one can defend.
