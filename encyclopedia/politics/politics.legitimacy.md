# Legitimacy and Political Authority

The state does not merely predict your compliance; it claims you *owe* it. A mugger and a tax collector both take your money under threat, and the whole subject lies in the difference — if there is one. Two questions must be pried apart: **legitimacy** (what entitles the state to coerce — a right to rule) and **political obligation** (whether citizens must obey the law *because it is the law*, not merely because most laws forbid what was wrong anyway). They are usually assumed correlative, but can come apart.

The classic answer is consent: free equals are bound only by their own agreement. Its strength is real — consent is how obligation ordinarily arises among equals, and it explains the duty's content-independence: you obey because you promised, whatever the law says. Its failure for actual states is equally real. Almost no one expressly consents, and tacit consent through residence founders on Hume's ship: a man carried aboard while asleep, who can leave only by leaping into the sea, has not consented by staying. Simmons sharpened the point — tacit consent binds only given knowledge that silence counts and a reasonable way to refuse, conditions no state meets. And a merely hypothetical contract, Dworkin observed, is no contract at all.

The successor accounts each rescue one strand. Fair play (Hart, Rawls): accepting the benefits of a cooperative scheme obligates you to carry your share — against which Nozick asked whether neighbors running a public-address system you never requested may bill you for it. Natural duty: a standing duty to support just institutions, or (Wellman) a samaritan duty to rescue others from statelessness — but why does it bind you to *this* state rather than to just institutions anywhere (Simmons's particularity objection)? Associative accounts (Dworkin, Horton) model political obligation on family, constitutive of an unchosen membership — at the cost of hovering near mere conventionalism.

Raz relocated the question from consent to service. On the service conception, authority is legitimate over a person, in a domain, when she conforms better to reasons that already apply to her by following its directives than by deciding case by case; legitimate directives then *preempt* her own balancing. This dissolves the old paradox that submission betrays rational autonomy — but at an honest price: authority so grounded is piecemeal, varying by person and domain, and never as wide as states claim. A different family makes democratic procedure itself the ground — the fairness of equal say (Christiano), the qualified epistemic credentials of deliberation (Estlund) — pursued under the `democracy` sibling. A Rawlsian strand asks instead when coercion is *justifiable to* those coerced: the liberal principle of legitimacy and the public reason literature it spawned.

The sober current verdict — Simmons's a posteriori philosophical anarchism, after Wolff's a priori version — is that every general defense fails: there is no content-independent duty to obey the law as such. Nobody mans barricades over this; most of what law requires morality requires anyway, so the anarchist pays her taxes. Whether that inert conclusion is a discovery about states — that they run on habit, prudence, and morality rather than obligation — or a reductio of the arguments is itself contested. Either way it is not a theory of justice: the two are distinct axes, and a tolerably just usurper may lack the right to rule that a legitimate but imperfect state possesses.

## Bridges

- To `politics.democracy`: whether democratic procedure alone confers a right to rule is argued there.
- To `politics.justice`: a permission to enforce is no guarantee that what is enforced is just; the two diverge in both directions.
- To `politics.law`: law's claim to authority is a datum for jurisprudence — Raz argues from that claim to legal positivism.
- To `ethics`: consent, fair play, and gratitude are moral notions first; whether political obligation is a genuine moral duty is finally an ethical question.
