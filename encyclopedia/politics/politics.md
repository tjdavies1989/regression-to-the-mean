# Political and Social Philosophy

The state does things to you that no private person may do. It takes your money, forbids you conduct, and backs both with force. Political philosophy begins with the demand that this be justified. Consent theorists, from Locke onward, rest state authority on the agreement of the governed — and then face Hume's observation that almost no one has actually agreed, and that a peasant who cannot leave has not consented by staying. Rivals ground obligation in fair play — the benefits we accept from a cooperative scheme — in natural duty, or in democratic procedure itself. Philosophical anarchists conclude that the justification fails: states may be useful, but no one is *obligated* to obey the law as such. The dispute is hard because both poles are vivid — the arbitrariness of submitting to whoever holds power, and the catastrophe, or so Hobbes argued, of no one holding it.

Suppose the state is legitimate. Who should get what? This is the problem of justice, and its modern form is largely Rawls's: distributive principles are fair if they would be chosen behind a veil of ignorance hiding everyone's talents and social position — a device yielding strong protections for liberty and a demand that inequalities benefit the worst off. Nozick answered that justice is not a pattern to be imposed but a history to be respected: whatever arises from just acquisition and free transfer is just, and any pattern requires continuous interference to maintain. Beneath the rivalry run further contested notions. Liberty: is it mere absence of interference, as Berlin's negative conception has it, or the presence of self-mastery — or, as republicans argue, the absence of *domination*, of living at another's mercy even if the master never strikes? Equality: equality of what — welfare, resources, capabilities — and is equality even the point, rather than sufficiency or non-subordination? And democracy: why should the numbers rule? Plato's challenge — governing is a craft, and crafts belong to the competent — still sets the agenda; answers appeal to the fairness of equal say, the epistemic power of many minds, or the claim that only democratic procedure legitimates coercion.

A distinct strand doubts the framing. Marx, and after him feminist philosophy and critical theory, argue that official neutrality can itself be an instrument of power: ideologies present contingent arrangements as natural, and a theory of justice that abstracts from class, gender, or race may launder the domination it should expose. Steelmanned, this is not cynicism but a rival method — start from actual, structural injustice rather than an idealized contract.

The field articulates into a normative core — justice, legitimacy, liberty, equality, democracy, rights — flanked by philosophy of law, by extensions of justice beyond the state, by critical approaches, and by social ontology, the study of what social entities like institutions, money, and groups *are*. Its boundaries: what individuals owe one another belongs to ethics, what institutions owe to persons belongs here; social ontology faces metaphysics; the epistemology of disagreement and testimony underwrites democratic theory.

## Children

- `justice` — **Justice** — principles for distributing benefits and burdens: Rawls, his libertarian and egalitarian critics, and the currency and site of distribution. *(anchor)*
- `legitimacy` — **Legitimacy and Political Authority** — what, if anything, entitles the state to coerce and obligates citizens to obey. *(anchor)*
- `liberty` — **Liberty** — negative, positive, and republican conceptions of freedom, and the limits of justified interference. *(anchor)*
- `equality` — **Equality and Egalitarianism** — equality of what, and why: welfare, resources, capabilities; luck versus relational egalitarianism. *(anchor)*
- `democracy` — **Democracy** — why the many should rule: procedural fairness, epistemic defenses, deliberation, and Plato's standing challenge. *(anchor)*
- `rights` — **Rights** — what rights are, who holds them, and whether interests or wills ground them. *(standard)*
- `social-ontology` — **Social Ontology** — the nature of social entities: institutions, money, groups, and collective intentionality; bridges metaphysics. *(standard)*
- `law` — **Philosophy of Law** — the nature of law and its relation to morality: positivism, natural law, and interpretation. *(standard)*
- `global-justice` — **Global Justice and Migration** — whether justice stops at borders: cosmopolitanism, statism, and the ethics of immigration. *(standard)*
- `critical-approaches` — **Ideology, Power, and Critical Approaches** — Marxist, feminist, and critical-theoretic analyses of power and ideology; bridges the traditions register. *(standard)*

## Bridges

- To `ethics`: this division extends ethics to institutions — justice is what a basic structure owes persons — and shares its metaethical foundations.
- To `metaphysics`: social ontology asks what institutions, groups, and social kinds are, and whether they reduce to individuals.
- To `epistemology`: epistemic defenses of democracy, disagreement, and testimony are shared territory; ideology critique is partly a theory of socially produced belief.
- To the registers: the spine of figures runs from Plato and Hobbes through Locke, Rousseau, Marx, and Rawls; critical theory and feminist philosophy are homed in `traditions` and argued here.
