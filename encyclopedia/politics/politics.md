# Political and Social Philosophy

The state does what no private person may: it takes your money, forbids you conduct, and backs both with force. Political philosophy begins with the demand that this be justified. Consent theorists, from Locke onward, rest state authority on the agreement of the governed — and face Hume's observation that a peasant who cannot leave has not consented by staying. Rivals appeal instead to fair play, natural duty, or democratic procedure itself. Philosophical anarchists conclude that the justification fails: states may be useful, but no one is *obligated* to obey the law as such. The dispute is hard because both poles are vivid — the arbitrariness of submitting to whoever holds power, and the catastrophe, or so Hobbes argued, of no one holding it.

Suppose the state is legitimate. Who should get what? This is the problem of justice, and its modern form is largely Rawls's: distributive principles are fair if they would be chosen behind a veil of ignorance hiding everyone's talents and social position — a device demanding that inequalities benefit the worst off. Nozick answered that justice is not a pattern to be imposed but a history to be respected: whatever arises from just acquisition and free transfer is just. Beneath the rivalry run further contested notions. Is liberty mere absence of interference (Berlin's negative conception), positive self-mastery — or, as republicans argue, absence of *domination*, living at another's mercy even if the master never strikes? Equality of what — welfare, resources, capabilities — and is equality even the point, rather than sufficiency or non-subordination? And why should the numbers rule? Plato's challenge — governing is a craft, and crafts belong to the competent — still sets the agenda; answers appeal to the fairness of equal say, the epistemic power of many minds, or the legitimating force of procedure.

A distinct strand doubts the framing. Marx, and after him feminist philosophy and critical theory, argue that official neutrality can itself serve power: ideologies present contingent arrangements as natural, and a theory of justice abstracting from class, gender, or race may launder the domination it should expose. This is not cynicism but a rival method: start from actual, structural injustice rather than an idealized contract.

The field articulates into a normative core — justice, legitimacy, liberty, equality, democracy, rights — flanked by philosophy of law, by extensions of justice beyond the state, by critical approaches, and by social ontology, the study of what institutions, money, and groups *are*. Its boundaries: what individuals owe one another belongs to ethics; what institutions owe to persons belongs here; social ontology faces metaphysics.

## Bridges

- To `ethics`: this division extends ethics to institutions — justice is what a basic structure owes persons.
- To `metaphysics`: social ontology asks what institutions, groups, and social kinds are, and whether they reduce to individuals.
- To `epistemology`: epistemic defenses of democracy, disagreement, and testimony are shared territory; ideology critique is partly a theory of socially produced belief.
- To the registers: the spine of figures runs from Plato and Hobbes through Locke, Rousseau, Marx, and Rawls; critical theory and feminist philosophy are homed in `traditions` and argued here.
