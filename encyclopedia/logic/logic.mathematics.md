# Philosophy of Mathematics

Mathematics is the best knowledge we have: proved rather than conjectured, holding everywhere and always, indispensable to every science. Yet what is it knowledge *of*? Numbers, sets, and functions — if they exist at all — are abstract: not located in space, not participants in causes. Benacerraf's dilemma makes the discomfort precise. A uniform semantics says that "there are infinitely many primes" is true only if there really are infinitely many primes — objects the sentence is about. A credible epistemology says that knowledge of objects requires some connection to them. For mathematics we can have the semantics or the epistemology, it seems, but not both. Every position on the map is a way of paying one horn of this bill.

The platonist accepts the objects and owes the epistemology. Gödel posited a faculty of mathematical intuition; contemporary platonists more often argue that reliability, not causal contact, is what needs explaining — and that mathematics' role in science explains it. That role is itself an argument: the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument holds that since our best physics quantifies ineliminably over mathematical entities, believing the physics commits us to the entities. Its fortunes have wavered: Field's nominalist program tried to show mathematics dispensable in principle by reformulating physics without numbers (achieved for Newtonian gravitation, contested beyond), while the "enhanced" indispensability debate now turns on whether mathematics ever *explains* physical facts — the cicada's prime-numbered life cycle is the standard exhibit — rather than merely representing them. Fictionalists split the difference: mathematics is a useful story, "true in the fiction," and no more ontologically committing than Sherlock Holmes.

Structuralism relocates the subject matter: mathematics studies not objects but structures — the natural-number system rather than any particular things serving as numbers. This dissolves Benacerraf's other puzzle (which sets *are* the numbers? — no fact could decide), at the cost of a new question: is a structure any less abstract than a number?

The great foundational programs each proposed to ground the whole edifice. Logicism (Frege, Russell) derived arithmetic from logic alone; Russell's paradox wrecked Frege's system, but neo-logicists have revived the project from Hume's Principle, and how much of its philosophical payoff survives is a live question. Formalism treated mathematics as the manipulation of symbols, with Hilbert's program to secure infinitary mathematics by finitary consistency proofs; Gödel's incompleteness theorems showed that no such system can prove its own consistency, ending the program in its original form though not its descendants. Intuitionism (Brouwer) made mathematics a mental construction and paid willingly in classical theorems, bequeathing a logic that now thrives on independent grounds. The foundational question itself has reopened: set theory's default status is challenged by category theory and by univalent foundations, while within set theory the search for new axioms to settle the continuum hypothesis continues. Meanwhile a philosophy-of-practice turn asks questions the foundational debates ignored: what makes a proof explanatory, a theorem deep, a method pure — and what computer-assisted proof does to mathematical knowledge.

## Children

- `ontology` — **Mathematical Ontology** — Whether mathematical objects exist: platonism, nominalism, fictionalism, and the indispensability argument as the field's central battleground. *(anchor)*
- `structuralism` — **Structuralism** — Mathematics as the science of structure: ante rem and eliminative variants, and whether structures escape the epistemic problems of objects. *(standard)*
- `logicism` — **Logicism and Neo-Logicism** — Frege's derivation of arithmetic from logic, its collapse, and the revival from Hume's Principle. *(standard)*
- `formalism` — **Formalism and Hilbert's Program** — Mathematics as symbol manipulation, the finitist consistency project, and what survives Gödel. *(standard)*
- `intuitionism` — **Intuitionism and Constructivism** — Brouwer's mathematics of mental construction, its revisionary logic, and modern constructive mathematics. *(standard)*
- `foundations` — **Foundations of Mathematics** — Set theory, category theory, and univalent foundations as rival frameworks; new axioms and the continuum hypothesis. *(anchor)*
- `incompleteness` — **The Incompleteness Theorems** — What Gödel's theorems actually show, their philosophical import, and their abuse. *(standard)*
- `practice` — **Philosophy of Mathematical Practice** — Explanation, depth, purity of method, visualization, and computer-assisted proof as philosophical topics. *(standard)*

## Bridges

- **Metaphysics**: the existence and nature of abstract objects is the paradigm case of the ontology of the non-concrete; every position here is also a position there.
- **Epistemology**: Benacerraf's dilemma is at bottom an epistemological challenge — a priori knowledge, intuition, and reliability without causal contact.
- **Science**: the indispensability argument makes philosophy of mathematics hostage to philosophy of science — confirmation, explanation, and what accepting a theory commits us to.
- **Within logic**: the `mathematical` child supplies the technical results (incompleteness, independence proofs) this node interprets; `nonclassical` houses intuitionistic logic as a system.
