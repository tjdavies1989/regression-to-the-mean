# Logic

Some arguments cannot fail. If all whales are mammals and Moby is a whale, then Moby is a mammal — with a necessity that seems to owe nothing to whales. Logic begins here and asks what underwrites it. What is it for a conclusion to *follow from* premises? The dominant answer since Tarski: an argument is valid when every interpretation making the premises true makes the conclusion true. A rival tradition, running through Gentzen to modern inferentialists, says validity is instead a matter of proof — of what the rules governing our logical vocabulary license us to infer. The pictures agree on an enormous central territory, pull apart at the edges, and which is fundamental remains contested.

The questions harden quickly. Validity is supposed to hold in virtue of *form* — but which expressions belong to logical form and which are mere content? ("All" and "not," presumably; "necessarily"? "true"?) Is there one correct logic, or many, each right relative to a purpose — the monism-versus-pluralism debate? Is logic normative for thought, or just one more theory, revisable like any science, as anti-exceptionalists urge? The discipline also carries its own wounds: the liar paradox ("this sentence is false") and Russell's paradox arise from principles — truth's transparency, naive set formation — that seem undeniable yet jointly explode. Responses divide between restricting the principles and revising the logic itself, which has made the paradoxes the proving ground for nonclassical systems. Finally, logic's most spectacular success — the formalization of mathematics — generates its own philosophy: what are numbers, and how can we know them if, as the platonist holds, they are abstract and causally inert? Nominalists, structuralists, and neo-logicists each offer an escape, none cheap.

The field articulates cleanly. At its center sits the theory of consequence and the classical systems built to capture it, with their celebrated metatheory (completeness, incompleteness, undecidability). Around that core: the modal and intensional extensions; the nonclassical rivals; the four-limbed cluster of mathematical logic; the paradoxes and theories of truth; the philosophy of logic proper; and philosophy of mathematics, housed here by editorial decision though it bridges constantly outward. Informal argumentation theory and the long history of the discipline complete the picture. The boundary with philosophy of language runs through logical form and meaning; with epistemology, through justification and the a priori; with metaphysics, through necessity, truth, and abstract objects.

## Children

- `consequence` — **Logical Consequence** — What it is for a conclusion to follow from premises: the model-theoretic and proof-theoretic analyses, formality, and necessity. *(anchor)*
- `classical` — **Classical Logic** — Propositional and first-order logic as the standard system, with its metatheory: soundness, completeness, compactness, incompleteness. *(standard)*
- `modal` — **Modal and Intensional Logic** — Logics of necessity, time, knowledge, and obligation, their possible-worlds semantics, and what the apparatus commits us to. *(anchor)*
- `nonclassical` — **Nonclassical Logics** — The rivals to classical logic — intuitionistic, relevance, paraconsistent, many-valued, substructural — and what would make one of them correct. *(anchor)*
- `mathematical` — **Mathematical Logic** — The technical cluster of proof theory, model theory, set theory, and computability, treated for its philosophical yield. *(standard)*
- `paradox` — **Paradox and Truth** — The liar, sorites, Russell, and Curry, and the theories of truth and vagueness built to survive them. *(anchor)*
- `philosophy-of-logic` — **Philosophy of Logic** — What logic is about: logical constants, monism versus pluralism, normativity, and anti-exceptionalism. *(anchor)*
- `mathematics` — **Philosophy of Mathematics** — What mathematical objects are and how mathematical knowledge is possible: platonism, nominalism, structuralism, and the foundational programs. *(anchor)*
- `argumentation` — **Argumentation** — Informal logic, fallacy theory, and the study of real-world argument beyond formal validity. *(satellite)*
- `history` — **History of Logic** — From Aristotle's syllogistic and Stoic propositional logic through the medievals to Frege, Russell, and the twentieth-century explosion. *(standard)*

## Bridges

- **Language**: logical form, quantification, and natural-language semantics are joint property; Frege's revolution was a revolution in both fields at once.
- **Metaphysics**: modality and possible worlds, the nature of truth, and the ontology of abstract objects make this the busiest border in the encyclopedia.
- **Epistemology**: the a priori status of logic and mathematics, and logic's claimed normativity for belief, tie consequence to justification.
- **Figures and traditions**: Aristotle, Frege, Russell, Gödel, and Tarski anchor the figures register; the Stoics, the medievals, and the Vienna Circle enter through the history child.
