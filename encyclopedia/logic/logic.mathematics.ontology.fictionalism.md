# Fictionalism

Fictionalism holds that mathematics is good without being true: its sentences are untrue for want of objects, and the practice is in perfect order anyway. Conservativeness supplies the licence to go on using it (see the sibling entry on nominalist reconstructions); what the licence does not settle is a question about *us* — what are speakers doing when they say there are infinitely many primes? On this the position splits, along a line drawn by Burgess. **Revolutionary** fictionalism (Field) says mathematics as practiced asserts falsehoods, and prescribes a reform of attitude rather than of practice: keep every sentence, exchange belief for acceptance. **Hermeneutic** fictionalism says no reform is needed because no one was ever committed — mathematical discourse already is a game of make-believe or a figure of speech, and its ontological seriousness is a philosopher's misreading.

The hermeneutic version makes an empirical claim about speakers, and Stanley's challenge presses exactly there. Pretense has behavioral and linguistic marks — speakers can be brought to acknowledge it, can say roughly what the pretense is for, and drop it under pressure — and mathematical talk shows none of them; a mathematician assured she is only pretending will insist she is not. Worse, if utterances of "2 + 2 = 4" communicate some real, nominalistically kosher content, speakers should be able to identify it, and they cannot; a semantics whose contents are systematically unavailable to competent speakers fails the compositional standards any serious theory of communication must meet.

Yablo's **figuralism** is the hermeneutic version built to survive this. The model is not conscious pretense but figurative language of the most ordinary kind: "the average family has 2.3 children" is literally false — there is no such family — and no one is troubled, because the figure is a device for saying something true about birthrates. Nor can the figure always be paraphrased away by its own users, which blunts Stanley's demand: dead metaphors work in speakers who could not unpack them. Numbers, for Yablo, are *existential metaphors* — representational aids that let finite creatures state infinitely many facts about the concrete world at once. Melia's **weaseling** reaches a similar station by a blunter route: assert your best mixed theory, then take back its abstract commitments — "everything the theory says, except the bit about numbers" — a move he argues is routine in ordinary speech, and critics answer leaves no coherent content asserted at all.

Two pressure points structure the current debate. First, the **norms** question: if acceptance replaces belief, what makes a mathematical utterance correct? The natural answer — truth in the fiction — inherits the problem that the "fiction" has no author and no complete text: either it borrows platonistic determinacy or it leaves undecided sentences (the continuum hypothesis) neither correct nor incorrect. Field accepts the second horn; many find it a reductio. Second, the **oracle** test, wielded by Yablo: if an oracle announced that abstract objects exist — or that they do not — nothing in mathematics would change; no proof would be checked again, no theorem retired. A discourse wholly insulated from ontological news is a poor candidate for being *about* ontology, which cuts against platonism but also against the revolutionary fictionalist's charge of massive error: a strange error, whose correction would alter nothing.

The architecture generalizes. Rosen's modal fictionalism treats possible-worlds talk as true according to the Lewisian fiction, mathematics-fictionalism's exact twin — prefix operator, borrowed determinacy problems, the Brock–Rosen objection as the analogue of the norms question — and the two literatures now trade objections directly.
