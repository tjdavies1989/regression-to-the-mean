# History of Logic

If logic is a priori and its truths could not have been otherwise, why does it have a history at all? The puzzle is real: a discipline that studies what cannot fail has nonetheless transformed itself, root and branch, several times over — and each transformation changed not just the answers but what the questions were. The history of logic is therefore not a parade of names. It is the record of the discipline's self-transformations, and it is standing evidence in the dispute over whether logic itself changes or only our formulations of it do.

The first transformation is the invention of the formal system itself. Aristotle's *Prior Analytics* does not merely give examples of good arguments; it introduces schematic letters, isolates validity as a property of figures rather than contents, and proves metatheorems — every valid mood reduces to the first figure. That is a formal science, complete with its own metatheory, appearing essentially without precedent. The second transformation happened almost immediately and was then lost: the Stoics, above all Chrysippus, built a propositional logic — arguments valid in virtue of connectives rather than terms, five indemonstrable schemata, a compositional theory of the sayable — that antiquity subordinated to the syllogism and that was recovered as logic, rather than as rhetoric or curiosity, only in the twentieth century, when Łukasiewicz, Mates, and later Bobzien could finally see what it was. The lesson cuts deep: whether a text counts as logic depends on the logic its readers possess.

Formal logic was also invented a second time, independently. The Nyāya school analyzed inference (*anumāna*) through the pervasion relation between reason and target property, codified in a five-membered demonstration schema; the Buddhist logicians Dignāga and Dharmakīrti tightened this into the triple condition on a good reason and a theory of concept formation by exclusion (*apoha*). The Indian tradition kept logic entangled with epistemology — inference is a *pramāṇa*, a knowledge source — which makes it not a defective version of the Greek development but a differently shaped one; the comparison is itself a live research program. The medieval Latin schools, meanwhile, built genuinely original theory on Aristotelian foundations: supposition theory as a semantics of terms in context, the *consequentiae* as a general theory of inference beyond the syllogism, obligations disputations as something like a formal dialectic, and the *insolubilia* literature on the liar anticipating moves still made in `paradox`.

Then the famous stagnation. Kant announced in 1787 that logic had been unable to advance a step since Aristotle and was "to all appearance closed and completed." The judgment was wrong twice over — wrong backward, because it erased the Stoics, the medievals, and everything east of Greece; and wrong forward, because Leibniz's unpublished dream of a *calculus ratiocinator* was already on the shelf, Boole would algebraize logic within sixty years, and Frege's *Begriffsschrift* (1879) — quantifiers, the function–argument analysis displacing subject–predicate form, logic as a language and not merely a calculus — stands as the sharpest discontinuity in the discipline's history. The twentieth-century explosion that followed (Russell, Hilbert's metamathematics, Gödel's completeness and incompleteness theorems, Tarski, Gentzen, Church and Turing) turned logic into the plural, mathematized field the rest of this division describes, and made the historian's question urgent: was Frege's revolution a discovery about the same subject Aristotle had, or a change of subject?

## Bridges

- **Figures**: `aristotle` and `frege` mark the discipline's two founding discontinuities; `godel` closes the arc, turning the new logic on itself.
- **Traditions**: the Stoics, Nyāya, and the medieval schools are treated as living wholes in the traditions register; this node tracks only their logical theory.
- **Philosophy of logic**: the whole history is evidence in the anti-exceptionalism debate — a discipline that has repeatedly transformed itself is harder to call unrevisable.
- **Language**: Frege's revolution belonged simultaneously to logic and to the theory of meaning; supposition theory is medieval semantics as much as medieval logic.
